Phenomenon

An old string of holiday lights has fifty bulbs. You remove just one bulb — and the entire string goes dark. On a newer string, removing one bulb leaves the rest glowing.

Driving question: Why can a single missing bulb shut down an entire string of lights?

Notice & Wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: In a single-loop circuit, if you removed one resistor, what happens to the current everywhere else? Use the word path.

Initial Model

Sketch three bulbs wired in a single loop with a battery. Draw one arrow for the current. Predict: is the current the same at every bulb, or does it shrink as it passes each one?

Part C — Break it. Remove one resistor (open the loop). What happens to the current everywhere?

Key question: How does R₁ + R₂ + R₃ compare to the total resistance you’d compute from I = V ÷ R_total?

Make It Make Sense

  1. Three resistors (1 Ω, 2 Ω, 3 Ω) are in series with a 12 V battery. Find the equivalent resistance and the current.
  2. In a series circuit the current is 2 A everywhere. The resistors are 3 Ω and 5 Ω. Find the voltage drop across each.
  3. You add a fourth resistor in series. Does the current go up or down? Why?

Exit Ticket

A 24 V battery is connected in series to a 4 Ω resistor and an 8 Ω resistor.

  1. Find the equivalent (total) resistance.

  2. Find the current in the circuit.

  3. Find the voltage drop across the 8 Ω resistor, and check that the two drops add to 24 V.
