|---|---|
| **Duration** | 42 minutes (1 period) |
| **NYSSLS link** | HS-PS3-6 (core — V, I, R relationship) |

| **Strategy chips** | HOCHMAN (Because/But/So on the V–I relationship) |

| **Prior knowledge** | Charge (positive/negative), conservation of charge, and energy as the capacity to do work. |

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- Define **current** as charge per unit time (amperes) and **voltage** as the push that drives charge around a circuit.
- Use Ohm's Law, **V = I·R**, to solve for any one of V, I, or R given the other two.
- Interpret a V-vs-I graph: a straight line through the origin whose slope equals the **resistance**.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–2 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Quick whip-around (one sentence each, pass allowed): *"Name one thing in your home that needs to be 'plugged in' or charged to work."* Capture two or three answers; you'll return to the idea that all of these move electric charge.

### 2–7 min · Phenomenon hook — the energy stick

**Teacher actions.** Hold the energy stick by one metal end. Nothing happens. Grab the other end with your free hand — it lights and buzzes. Break contact — it stops. Then form a chain of students holding hands; the stick lights only when the loop is closed.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Watch — when the loop is open, nothing. The moment we close the loop with our hands, charge can flow all the way around and the light comes on. What's our skin doing in this circuit?"

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "We're the wire." — capture this; skin is a (poor) conductor completing the path.
- "Electricity needs a complete circle." — exactly; name it *closed loop* / *complete circuit*.
- "More people should make it brighter/dimmer." — flag it; we'll connect more people = more resistance later.

### 7–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two columns on the board. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: what has to be true for the charge to flow? Use the word *path* in your answer."

Target insight: *charge needs a complete, closed path and a push (the battery) to keep it moving.*

### Bridge to Phase 2

Each student writes a one-sentence **initial model**: "The stick lights up because ______."

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 30 min)*

### 10–14 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt on the board:

> *"Draw the energy-stick loop with two people holding hands. Mark where the push comes from and draw arrows showing which way the charge moves around the loop. Predict: what would make the light brighter?"*

### 14–30 min · Investigation — PhET Circuit Construction Kit DC

Students build a single battery + resistor loop, add an ammeter (current, A) in the loop and a voltmeter (voltage, V) across the resistor.

1. **Same resistor, change the battery voltage.** Record V and I for 9 V, 18 V, 27 V across a fixed resistor. Plot V (y) vs I (x).
2. **Same battery, change the resistor.** Watch the current drop as resistance rises.

**Teacher facilitation language:**

> "When you double the battery voltage, what happens to the current? Is it exactly double?"
> "Your V-vs-I points — are they making a line? What does the steepness of that line tell you?"
> "Which resistor let the most current through for the same push?"

Use the V-vs-I graph below to make the linear pattern (slope = R) visible once a group has three data points.

![Graph of potential difference V on the vertical axis versus current I on the horizontal axis: the points fall on a straight line through the origin, and the slope of the line equals the resistance R.](figures/v_vs_i.png)

The single-loop schematic students are building:

![Schematic of a simple circuit: a battery labeled V equals 6 volts on the left side drives a current I equals 2 amperes clockwise through a single resistor labeled R equals 3 ohms on the bottom wire.](figures/simple_circuit.png)

### Teacher facilitation during Explore

- **What to look for** — groups noticing that V ÷ I gives the same number every time for one resistor (that number is R).
- **What to resist** — don't hand them "V = IR" yet; let the straight line emerge from the data.
- **What to redirect** — students who say "more voltage means more resistance" should re-check: more voltage means more *current*; resistance is the slope, set by the resistor.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(30 – 36 min)*

### 30–33 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "Across your data, how did current respond when you increased the voltage but kept the same resistor?"

Target consensus:

> "Current grows in proportion to voltage. For one resistor, V divided by I is always the same number — that constant is the resistance."

### 33–36 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "The flow of charge is **current** — charge per second, measured in **amperes**. The push that drives it is the **voltage** (potential difference), in volts. What slows the flow is the **resistance**, in ohms. Put them together and you get **Ohm's Law: V = I·R** — voltage equals current times resistance. On your graph, the slope *is* R."

Reference relationships to post: **V = I·R**; power **P = V·I = I²·R**.

### Discussion prompts to deploy here

- "A 12 V battery drives 3 A through a resistor. What is the resistance?"
  - *Sample response:* "R = V/I = 12/3 = 4 Ω."
- "If you keep the resistor the same but double the voltage, what happens to the current?"
  - *Sample response:* "It doubles, because I = V/R and R didn't change."

---

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(36 – 40 min)*

### 36–38 min · Hochman Because/But/So

Students complete a Because/But/So sentence on the core relationship:

> *"Current increases when voltage increases **because** ____, **but** ____, **so** ____."*

Target: *…because voltage is the push that drives charge, but the resistance limits how much charge gets through, so the current is set by both together (I = V/R).*

### 38–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "On the energy stick, why does adding more people in the hand-chain make it harder to light? Answer in one sentence using *resistance* and *current*."

Target: "Each person adds resistance, so for the same small voltage the current drops below what the stick needs to light."

---

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

> *"A resistor has 6 V across it and carries 2 A of current.
> (a) What is its resistance? Show V = I·R.
> (b) The voltage is raised to 12 V with the same resistor. What is the new current?
> (c) In one sentence, what does the slope of a V-vs-I graph represent?"*

Expected: (a) R = V/I = 6/2 = 3 Ω; (b) I = V/R = 12/3 = 4 A; (c) the slope equals the resistance. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "What is one thing about electricity you understand now that you didn't this morning?"

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "Current gets used up as it goes around the loop." → **Correction:** Charge is conserved; the same current flows all the way around a single loop. Energy is transferred, not the charge itself.
- **Misconception:** "Voltage flows through the wire." → **Correction:** Current (charge) flows; voltage is the *difference* in electric potential between two points — the push, not the stuff that moves.
- **Misconception:** "More resistance means more current." → **Correction:** For a fixed voltage, more resistance means *less* current (I = V/R).

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frame: *"Current is the ___ of charge; voltage is the ___ that drives it; resistance ___ the flow."* Word box: {flow, push, opposes, amperes, volts, ohms}. Pair *current* with a hand-sweeping gesture (flow) and *resistance* with a hand-blocking gesture.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide the V-vs-I axes pre-drawn and a partly filled data table; students plot two given points and read the slope. Offer a triangle "V over I·R" cover-up card for solving Ohm's Law.
- **Extensions:** Have students compute the **power** delivered to the resistor in the exit ticket (P = V·I) and explain why a higher-resistance wire of the same length runs hotter for the same voltage (P = V²/R reasoning).

## Strategy Spotlight

**HOCHMAN (sentence-level literacy).** The Elaborate phase uses a Because/But/So expansion on a single physics relationship (voltage up → current up, limited by resistance). Insist on one complete sentence with all three conjunctions; the "but" clause is where the *resistance* idea must appear, which is exactly the conceptual hinge of Ohm's Law. Read two or three aloud and revise one together so students hear how the "but" sharpens the claim.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **current** — the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor, equal to charge ÷ time, measured in amperes (A)
- **voltage** — the potential difference (electrical "push") between two points that drives current around a circuit, measured in volts (V)
- **resistance** — the opposition a material offers to the flow of charge; the slope of a V-vs-I graph, measured in ohms (Ω)
