## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

Power is the *rate* of energy transfer — how fast work is done. It deepens **HS-PS3-1**: *"Create a computational model to calculate the change in energy of one component in a system when the change in energy of the other component(s) and energy flows in and out of the system are known."* (CCC: **Systems and System Models**.) Once students can compute work (Lesson 01), power lets them quantify *how quickly* energy flows in or out of a system — the time dimension of every energy model.

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | 42 minutes (1 period) |
| **NYSSLS link** | HS-PS3-1 (rate of energy flow in/out of a system) |
| **CCC focus** | Systems and System Models — power is *how fast* energy enters or leaves a system. |
| **Strategy chips** | ACTIVE LEARNING (stair-climb lab, station data, predict-and-test) |
| **Materials** | A safe staircase or a step/box; stopwatches or phones; bathroom scale (to get weight in N); meter stick (step height); whiteboards. |
| **Safety** | One climber at a time; hand on rail; walk, do not run; spotter at base. No climbing for students who opt out — they time and record. |
| **Prior knowledge** | Work (W = F·d) and the joule from Lesson 01; weight = mass × g. |

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- Define **power** as the rate of doing work and state its unit, the **watt**.
- Calculate power with P = W/t (and recognize P = Fv for constant-speed motion).
- Explain why doing the *same* work in *less* time means *more* power, using stair-climb data.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Pair-share, one sentence: *"Name something you do fast and something you do slow — and does doing it faster make it harder?"* Surface a few (running vs. walking up stairs, sprinting to class). Plant the idea that *speed of effort* matters, not just total effort.

### 3–7 min · Phenomenon hook — two climbers, same stairs

**Teacher actions.** Two volunteers (or a video) climb the same staircase: one quickly, one slowly. Both reach the top.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Same stairs, same height, roughly the same body weight — so they did about the same *work* against gravity. But something is clearly different. What is it, and can we measure it?"

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "The fast one worked harder!" — reframe: did they do more *work*, or the same work *faster*?
- "The fast one is more powerful." — capture the word *powerful*; we will define it precisely.
- "Time is the difference." — exactly the variable we'll add to work.

### 7–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two columns. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: if both climbers did the same work, what made the fast climber different? Use the word *time*."

Desirable insight: *same work ÷ less time = a bigger something.* Leave the name for Phase 3.

### Bridge to Phase 2

Each student leaves with an initial model sentence: "The faster climber was more ______ because ______."

---

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 28 min)*

### 10–14 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt:

> *"Write a formula in words for 'how fast work is done.' What would you divide by what? Predict whether a heavier climber or a faster climber has more of it."*

### 14–28 min · Investigation — Stair-Climb Personal-Power Lab

Active-learning rotation. Each student (who opts in) climbs a measured set of stairs; partners time and record. Compute personal power.

1. **Measure weight.** Read weight on the scale; convert to newtons (N) — or use mass × g with g ≈ 9.81 m/s².
2. **Measure height.** Height of the staircase (vertical rise only), in meters.
3. **Compute work.** W = F·d = (weight in N) × (vertical height).
4. **Time the climb.** t in seconds.
5. **Compute power.** P = W/t, in watts.
6. **Compare** fast vs. slow trials for the *same* climber.

Project as data arrive:

![Power output of people vs household appliances](figures/power_comparison.png)

**Teacher facilitation language:**

> "You climbed the same stairs twice. Same work? Then why is your power different?"
> "Whose power is bigger — and is it because of weight, height, or time? Isolate the variable."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "My second climb was faster, so my power went up even though the work was the same." — exactly; P = W/t.
- "A heavier person doing the same climb in the same time has more power." — yes; more work in the same time.

### Teacher facilitation during Explore

- **What to look for** — students dividing work by time and getting watts; recognizing same-work/different-time.
- **What to resist** — don't define "watt" until students need a name for W/t.
- **What to redirect** — "faster always means more work" → no, faster means same work in *less time*, so more *power*.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(28 – 35 min)*

### 28–31 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "From the lab: what single number tells us how *fast* work is done, and how did you compute it?"

Target consensus:

> "Power is work divided by time, P = W/t. The same work done in less time is more power. For something moving at steady speed, power is also force times speed, P = Fv."

### 31–35 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

From the reference table: **P = W/t = Fv**. The unit of power is the **watt (W)** — one watt is one joule per second (1 W = 1 J/s).

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Power, the rate at which work is done, is measured in **watts** — joules per second. **Energy** is what gets transferred; **power** is how *fast* it's transferred. Your stair climb was a few hundred watts; a hair dryer is over a thousand."

### Discussion prompts to deploy here

- "Two motors raise the same load the same height. Motor A takes 2 s, motor B takes 8 s. Which is more powerful, and by how much?"
  - *Sample response:* "A — same work, one-fourth the time, so four times the power."
- "A 60 W bulb runs for 10 s. How much energy did it use?"
  - *Sample response:* "W = P·t = 60 × 10 = 600 J."

---

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(35 – 40 min)*

### 35–38 min · Revise the model + read the graph

> *"Plot work vs. time for the fast climber and the slow climber. What does the slope of each line represent?"*

Project:

![Work vs time: the slope of each line is the power](figures/work_vs_time.png)

Target: *the slope of a work-vs-time graph is the power; a steeper line = more power.* Tie back to P = W/t.

### 38–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "In one sentence, explain why the fast stair-climber is 'more powerful' than the slow one even though they did the same work. Use the words *power* and *time*."

---

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

> *"An elevator does 48,000 J of work lifting a load.
> (a) If it takes 8.0 s, what is its power output? Show P = W/t.
> (b) A second elevator does the same 48,000 J in 16 s. How does its power compare?
> (c) A 700 N student runs up a 4.0 m staircase in 5.0 s. What is the student's power output?"*

Expected: (a) 6000 W; (b) half the power (3000 W) — same work, twice the time; (c) W = 700 × 4.0 = 2800 J, P = 2800/5.0 = 560 W. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "What is one thing you can now measure about yourself that you couldn't this morning — and who helped you figure it out?"

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "More power means more work." → **Correction:** Power is the *rate* of work; the same work done faster is more power even though total work is unchanged.
- **Misconception:** "Power and energy are the same thing." → **Correction:** Energy (joules) is the quantity transferred; power (watts) is how fast it transfers — joules *per second*.
- **Misconception:** "A watt is a unit of energy." → **Correction:** A watt is a unit of *power* (1 W = 1 J/s); a watt-second is a joule of energy.

---

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frame: *"The ___ climber has more power because they did the same work in ___ time, so P = W/t is ___."* Word box: {power, watt, energy, rate, per second}. Build a "per second" gesture (tapping a beat).
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide a pre-built data table with labeled columns (weight, height, time) and the P = W/t formula with one row worked. Offer the timer/recorder role to students who opt out of climbing — full participation, no physical demand.
- **Extensions:** Convert personal power to horsepower (1 hp ≈ 746 W) and compare to a car engine; explore why sprinting up stairs cannot be sustained (peak vs. average power).

---

## Strategy Spotlight

**ACTIVE LEARNING.** The whole lesson is built on student-generated data: each learner measures their *own* weight, height, and time, then computes their *own* power. The comparison that matters — same work, different time — emerges from their two trials, not from the teacher. Assign clear roles (climber, timer, recorder, calculator) so every student is doing physics, and rotate so opt-out students still own the math. The graph in Phase 4 turns their messy data into the clean "slope = power" idea.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **power** — the rate at which work is done (energy transferred per unit time); P = W/t = Fv
- **watt** — the SI unit of power; one watt equals one joule per second (1 W = 1 J/s)
- **energy** — the capacity to do work, measured in joules; power tells how *fast* energy is transferred
