Lesson Overview

Duration 42 minutes (1 period)
NYSSLS link HS-PS2-1 (thread continues)
CCC focus Cause and Effect — equal time intervals can produce equal displacements but unequal paths (or vice versa). Higher velocity = more displacement per unit time, not just more ground covered.
Strategy chips HOCHMAN (sentence expansion)
Materials Stopwatches (or phone timers, 1 per pair); meter sticks or tape measures; printed Hochman expansion organizer (one per student); access to the interactive on devices.
Safety None.
Prior knowledge Lesson 01 (vectors), Lesson 02 (distance vs. displacement); division.

Lesson objectives — students can:

  • Define average speed as distance ÷ time and average velocity as displacement ÷ time.
  • Explain why two trips can have the same average speed but different average velocities (or vice versa).
  • Use a kernel sentence and three Hochman expansions to describe motion precisely.

Phase 1 · Engage (0 – 12 min)

0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Quick check-in. 60–90 seconds.

3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — the two-student walk

Teacher actions. Pick two willing students. Student A walks straight to the door at a steady pace. Student B walks half the distance, doubles back to a friend’s seat (briefly stops to “say hi”), then walks the rest of the way. Both finish at the door at roughly the same time. Class times both with stopwatches.

Sample teacher language:

“I’m going to ask Student A and Student B to walk to the door. They’ll start at the same time. They’ll finish at roughly the same time. But they’re going to take different paths. After they’re done, I want you to tell me — did they ‘move the same’? Defend your answer with a number.”

Anticipated student responses:

  • “Yes, they moved the same — they both took the same time and ended in the same place.” — surface this; it’s a productive starting point.
  • “No, B moved faster because B’s path was longer.” — also surface; this is the speed-vs-velocity tension we’ll resolve.
  • “Both, depending on what you mean.” — if a student says this, they’re already at the lesson’s destination. Reflect it back: “Interesting — what do you mean by ‘depending on what you mean’?”

8–12 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Capture two columns. Then:

“Turn to your partner. Did the two students ‘move the same’? Defend your answer with a number — even a guess.”

After 90 seconds, take 2–3 responses. Note the dispute on the board. Don’t resolve yet.

Bridge to Phase 2

By end of Phase 1, every student has written a kernel sentence in their notebook: “The car moves.” This becomes the seed for the Hochman sentence-expansion work in Phase 2.

Phase 2 · Explore (12 – 32 min)

12–17 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Distribute the Hochman expansion organizer. Students work silently. The organizer has four rows:

  1. Kernel: “The car moves.”
  2. Expansion 1 — add speed: “The car moves at ___.”
  3. Expansion 2 — add direction (velocity): “The car moves ___ at ___.”
  4. Expansion 3 — add time + trip detail: “The ___ car moves ___ at ___ for ___ seconds.”

Students fill in their best guesses for the blanks. They don’t know what numbers go where yet — that’s fine. The point is to surface what kinds of information they think describe motion precisely.

17–32 min · Investigation — two-cars race + Hochman expansions

Students open the interactive two-cars race. They run three pre-baked scenarios — Both go straight (different speeds), B reverses, B passes the end and comes back — and record the readouts in their organizer.

Then walk the class through the three Hochman expansions of “The car moves” using Car B’s reversal data:

  1. Expansion 1 — add speed. “The car moves at 8 m/s.” (Use Car B’s average speed reading.)
  2. Expansion 2 — add direction (turning speed into velocity). “The car moves east at 8 m/s.” Pause: “What changed when we added ‘east’? Why does that matter for physics?”
  3. Expansion 3 — add time and trip detail. “Car B moves east at an average velocity of about 4 m/s over 5 seconds, even though its average speed was 8 m/s — because the path included a reversal.”

Sample teacher facilitation language:

“Read your sentence aloud. Stop. What does ‘fast’ mean exactly? Can someone reproduce the motion just from ‘the car moves fast’? No. Try again with one more piece of information.”

Teacher facilitation during Explore

  • What to look for — students realizing that “fast” is not measurable. The Hochman expansion forces measurable, reproducible language.
  • What to resist — don’t write the formal definitions yet. Let the expansion process surface why direction matters.
  • What to redirect — students who write only the magnitude (“30 m/s”) without direction should be redirected: “If I told a friend in another classroom ‘the car moves at 30 m/s,’ could they tell me where the car ends up?”

Phase 3 · Explain (32 – 37 min)

32–35 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

Bring the class back. Read three student final-expansion sentences aloud. Ask:

“Which of these sentences is precise enough that another physicist could re-create the motion in their classroom? Why?”

The target consensus statement:

“Speed is distance ÷ time. Velocity is displacement ÷ time. Speed is a scalar — just a number. Velocity is a vector — it has direction. Two trips can have the same average speed but different average velocities, or the same velocity but different speeds, depending on the path.”

35–37 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

Sample teacher language:

“Speed answers ‘how fast’ — it’s distance over time, always positive. Velocity answers ‘how fast in what direction’ — it’s displacement over time, can be zero or negative in 1-D. Average rate is the umbrella idea: total change ÷ total time. We use it for both.”

Discussion prompts to deploy here

  • “If two cars travel between the same two cities in the same time, must their average speeds be equal? Their average velocities?”
    • Sample student response: “Their average velocities are equal because displacement ÷ time is the same. Average speed depends on path length.”
  • “A round-trip cyclist returns to her starting point in 1 hour after biking 20 km of trail. What is her average speed? Her average velocity?”
    • Sample student response: “Average speed = 20 km/h, average velocity = 0 because displacement is 0.”

Phase 4 · Elaborate (37 – 40 min)

37–39 min · Revise the model

Students reopen their Hochman organizer from Phase 2. Specific prompt:

“Take ‘The car moves’ and complete a Hochman expansion that ends with a sentence precise enough to describe Car B’s motion from the simulator. Use both ‘speed’ and ‘velocity’ in your final sentence.”

39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

Re-call the two-student walk. Ask:

“Re-classify Student A and Student B from the start of class. Did they have the same speed? The same velocity? Use today’s vocabulary.”

Target: “Same average velocity (same start, same end, same time → same displacement ÷ time). Different average speeds (B’s path was longer because of the reversal).”

Phase 5 · Evaluate (40 – 42 min)

40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Distribute exit slips. Transfer prompt:

“A cyclist rides 20 km along a trail and returns to her starting point. The whole trip takes 1 hour. (a) What is her average speed? (b) What is her average velocity? (c) Use a Hochman expansion: start with the kernel ‘The cyclist rode,’ and add three details so the sentence is precise enough that someone else could check her work.”

Expected: (a) 40 km/h. (b) 0 km/h. (c) ends with: “The cyclist rode 20 km out and 20 km back in 1 hour, averaging 40 km/h in speed but 0 km/h in velocity because her displacement was zero.”

Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds, on the same exit slip):

“What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?”

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: “Speed and velocity are the same word for the same thing.” → Correction: Speed is a scalar (magnitude only); velocity is a vector (magnitude and direction). Two cars going 30 mph in opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities.
  • Misconception: “Average velocity equals (initial velocity + final velocity) ÷ 2.” → Correction: That formula only holds for constant acceleration. In general, average velocity = total displacement ÷ total time.
  • Misconception: “If average velocity is zero, you didn’t move.” → Correction: You can move a lot — a round trip — and still have zero average velocity because displacement is zero.

Access & Differentiation

  • ELL/ENL supports: Sentence frames embedded in the Hochman scaffold itself: “The ___ moves ___ at ___ m/s for ___ seconds.” Word-choice box: {speed, velocity, scalar, vector, displacement, rate, direction}.
  • IEP/SPED supports: Pre-fill two of the four expansion blanks; the student adds the remaining two. Provide a labeled diagram with the kernel sentence and one full expansion as a worked example.
  • Extensions: Students invent two scenarios — one where average speed > average velocity magnitude, and one where they are equal — and explain why.

Strategy Spotlight

Hochman sentence expansion (15 min) — see Phase 2, Investigation. The full process is: kernel sentence (“The car moves”) → expansion 1 (add speed) → expansion 2 (add direction, turning speed into velocity) → expansion 3 (add time + trip detail). After each expansion, students share with a partner what new information was added and why it matters for describing motion. The strategy works because Hochman expansion forces measurable, reproducible word choice — students who default to “the car moves fast” learn that “fast” is not a physics word.

Key Vocabulary (max 3)

  • speed — distance ÷ time; a scalar; always ≥ 0
  • velocity — displacement ÷ time; a vector; has direction and can be zero or negative in 1-D
  • average rate — total change of a quantity ÷ total time elapsed (used for both average speed and average velocity)
