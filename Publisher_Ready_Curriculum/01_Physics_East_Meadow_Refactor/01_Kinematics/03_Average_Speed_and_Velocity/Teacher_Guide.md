## CCC Focus

**Cause and Effect** — equal time intervals can produce equal *displacements* but unequal *paths* (or vice versa). The cause of higher velocity is moving more displacement per unit time, not just covering more ground.

## Discussion Prompts

- "If two cars travel between the same two cities in the same time, must their average speeds be equal? Their average velocities?"
- "A round-trip cyclist returns to her starting point in 1 hour after biking 20 km of trail. What is her average speed? Her average velocity?"
- *Sample student response:* "Average speed = 20 km/h, average velocity = 0 because displacement is 0."

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "Speed and velocity are the same word for the same thing." → **Correction:** Speed is a scalar (magnitude only); velocity is a vector (magnitude *and* direction). Two cars going 30 mph in opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities.
- **Misconception:** "Average velocity equals average of (initial velocity + final velocity)." → **Correction:** That formula only holds for *constant* acceleration. In general, average velocity = total displacement ÷ total time.
- **Misconception:** "If average velocity is zero, you didn't move." → **Correction:** You can move a lot — a round trip — and still have zero average velocity because displacement is zero.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frames embedded in the Hochman scaffold itself: *"The ___ moves ___ at ___ m/s for ___ seconds."* Word-choice box: {speed, velocity, scalar, vector, displacement, rate, direction}.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Pre-fill two of the four expansion blanks; the student adds the remaining two. Provide a labeled diagram with the kernel sentence and one full expansion as a worked example.
- **Extensions:** Students invent two scenarios — one where average speed > average velocity magnitude, and one where they are equal — and explain why.

## Strategy Spotlight

**Hochman *sentence expansion* (15 min).** Distribute the Hochman expansion organizer. Begin with a *kernel sentence*: "The car moves." Lead the class through three expansions, one at a time, each adding ONE detail:

1. **Expansion 1 — add speed.** "The car moves at 30 m/s."
2. **Expansion 2 — add direction (turning speed into velocity).** "The car moves east at 30 m/s."
3. **Expansion 3 — add time and distance/displacement.** "The red car moves east at 30 m/s for 4 seconds, traveling a displacement of 120 m."

After each expansion, students share with a partner what new information was added and why it matters for *describing motion*. Close by asking: "Which of these sentences is precise enough that another physicist could re-create the motion in their classroom? Why?"

**Why this works:** Sentence expansion forces precise word choice. Students who default to "the car moves fast" learn that "fast" is not measurable. Each layer makes the description progressively *quantifiable* — exactly the work HS-PS2-1 demands.

## Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

A cyclist rides 20 km along a trail and returns to her starting point. The whole trip takes 1 hour.

(a) What is her average speed? (b) What is her average velocity? (c) Use a Hochman expansion: start with the kernel "The cyclist rode," and add three details so the sentence is precise enough that someone else could check her work.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **speed** — distance ÷ time; a scalar; always ≥ 0
- **velocity** — displacement ÷ time; a vector; has direction and can be zero or negative in 1-D
- **average rate** — total change of a quantity ÷ total time elapsed (used for both average speed and average velocity)
