Lesson Overview

Duration 42 minutes (1 period)
NYSSLS link HS-PS2-1 (constant-acceleration special case); previews HS-PS2-4
CCC focus Cause and Effect — the cause of freefall acceleration is gravity. The effect is the same constant acceleration (a = −g) on every object, regardless of mass — as long as air resistance is negligible.
Strategy chips HOCHMAN (because/but/so refutation of the heavier-falls-faster misconception)
Materials A textbook and a sheet of paper (for the classic same-mass / different-shape demo); a vacuum tube demo if available; printed Hochman because/but/so organizers (one per student); access to the interactive on devices.
Safety None for the demo if dropped from waist height. If you do a stairwell drop, ensure no one is below.
Prior knowledge Lessons 01–05 (vectors, distance/displacement, velocity, acceleration, motion graphs).

Lesson objectives — students can:

  • Explain why all objects in freefall experience the same acceleration (g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward) regardless of mass — when air resistance is negligible.
  • Calculate the velocity and the fallen distance of a freely falling object after a given time.
  • Distinguish freefall (gravity only) from real-world falling (gravity plus air resistance).

Phase 1 · Engage (0 – 12 min)

0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Quick check-in. 60–90 seconds.

3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — Jumping from Space + textbook/paper drop

Teacher actions. Play 1 minute of Jumping from Space!. Then, in the room: hold up a textbook and a flat sheet of paper. Drop them from the same height, simultaneously.

Sample teacher language:

“I’m going to drop these two things at the same time. Predict — which lands first? Why?”

Drop them. The textbook lands clearly first.

“OK. Now I’m going to crumple the paper into a tight ball. Same height. Same instant. Predict again.”

Drop again. They land together (or very close).

“Same paper. Same gravity. Different result. What changed?”

Anticipated student responses:

  • “The paper has air resistance” — yes, surface this immediately.
  • “When you crumple it, the air doesn’t catch it as much” — exactly.
  • “Heavier things fall faster” — do not correct yet. This is THE misconception we’re going to refute. Note it as the kernel claim for Phase 2’s Hochman work.
  • “Gravity is the same on both” — affirm; this is the productive insight.

8–12 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Capture two columns. Then:

“Turn to your partner: which sentence do you believe right now — ‘heavier objects fall faster’ or ‘all objects fall at the same rate’? Defend your answer.”

After 90 seconds, take 3–4 responses on each side. Don’t resolve. Frame the lesson:

“We’re going to write the most precise sentence we can about how things fall. By the end of the lesson, every one of you will have a sentence that you’d defend in front of a physicist.”

Bridge to Phase 2

By end of Phase 1, every student writes the kernel claim in their notebook: “Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones.” This is the seed for the Hochman because/but/so work.

Phase 2 · Explore (12 – 32 min)

12–17 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Specific prompt on the board:

“Sketch v-t graphs for a falling rock and a falling feather, both released from rest, in air. Are they the same shape?”

Most students draw two different curves. Some draw two identical lines. Both are productive starting points. Surface neither as right or wrong yet.

17–32 min · Investigation — interactive drop + Hochman because/but/so

Distribute the printed Hochman because/but/so organizer. Then students open the interactive freefall race. They drop the rock and the feather side-by-side, with air resistance toggled ON, then OFF.

Walk the class through the Hochman expansion of the misconception:

The kernel claim (already in students’ notebooks): “Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones.”

Step 1 — the because clause. Students fill in:

“Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones, because ___.”

Anticipated student responses: “they have more weight,” “gravity pulls them harder,” “more mass means more force.” All three are real student thinking; capture a few on the board.

Step 2 — the but clause. Students fill in:

“… but ___.”

Anticipated responses: “in a vacuum, all objects fall at the same rate,” “the textbook and crumpled paper land together,” “I just saw it happen.”

Step 3 — the so clause. Students fill in:

“… so ___.”

Anticipated responses: “the apparent difference is caused by air resistance, not gravity,” “true freefall depends on g alone, not mass,” “without air, mass doesn’t matter.”

The full corrected sentence, read aloud at the end:

“Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones because they have more weight pulling them down, but in a vacuum (or with negligible air resistance) the textbook and the crumpled paper land at the same time, so the apparent difference is caused by air resistance, not by gravity itself — every object freely falls with the same acceleration g ≈ 9.8 m/s².”

Sample teacher facilitation language during the work:

“Read your because clause out loud. Does it sound like something a physicist would write — or something an everyday person would say? Both are OK; we’re going to refute it next.”

“Your but clause needs evidence. What evidence did you see in the simulator?”

Teacher facilitation during Explore

  • What to look for — students who write a but clause that genuinely contradicts their because clause. The strategy depends on the contradiction being real, not papered-over.
  • What to resist — don’t tell students the answer to the so clause. The whole point of because/but/so is that the student arrives at the corrected conclusion themselves.
  • What to redirect — students who are stuck on but often need: “Toggle air resistance OFF in the simulator. What happens to the feather?”

Phase 3 · Explain (32 – 37 min)

32–35 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

Pick three student sentences (one from each because/but/so step). Read them aloud. Ask:

“Which of these versions would convince a peer who has never been to physics class? Why?”

Take volunteers. The target consensus statement:

“Freefall is motion under the influence of gravity alone — no air resistance. In freefall, all objects accelerate at g ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward, regardless of mass. The reason a feather falls slower in air than a rock is air resistance, not gravity. When air resistance balances gravity, the object reaches terminal velocity.”

35–37 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

Sample teacher language:

“Freefall — only gravity acts. Mass doesn’t matter; acceleration is g. g — Earth-surface acceleration ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward. Terminal velocity — when air resistance equals gravity, the object stops accelerating and reaches a maximum steady speed. In freefall, terminal velocity doesn’t apply; that’s the air-resistance regime.”

Discussion prompts to deploy here

  • “If you drop a textbook and a flat sheet of paper at the same time, which hits first? What if you crumple the paper into a ball?”
    • Sample student response: “Textbook first when paper is flat. Together when paper is crumpled. Air resistance is the difference.”
  • “If we did this experiment on the moon, what would happen?”
    • Sample student response: “Same time — no atmosphere, no air resistance, only gravity.”

Phase 4 · Elaborate (37 – 40 min)

37–39 min · Revise the model

Students reopen their initial v-t sketches from Phase 2. Specific prompt:

“Update your sketches. Use a different color for the rock and the feather. Mark where the curves diverge — and label what causes the divergence.”

Target: Both curves start at slope = g. The feather’s curve flattens to a horizontal asymptote at terminal velocity; the rock’s continues at slope g until impact. The divergence is caused by air resistance, not gravity.

39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

Re-watch a 30-second clip of the Jumping from Space video — specifically the moment when the skydiver enters denser atmosphere and slows.

“Tell me what we just saw using the words freefall and terminal velocity.”

Target: “In near-vacuum at the start, he was in freefall — accelerating at g. As he entered denser atmosphere, air resistance grew, and he slowed toward terminal velocity.”

Phase 5 · Evaluate (40 – 42 min)

40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Distribute exit slips. Transfer prompt:

“A pebble is dropped from rest from a 45 m cliff. Ignore air resistance. Use g = 9.8 m/s². (a) What is its velocity 1 second after release? 2 seconds after? (b) How long until it hits the ground? (c) Use because/but/so to explain why a feather dropped from the same cliff in air takes longer than the pebble.”

Expected: (a) 9.8 m/s, 19.6 m/s. (b) ~3.03 s. (c) Because the pebble is heavier, students may think it should fall faster; but in a vacuum both fall at g, and even in air the acceleration due to gravity is the same; so the difference is air resistance, which has a much larger effect on the feather (low mass, high surface area) — the feather reaches a low terminal velocity quickly while the pebble is still accelerating when it hits.

Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds, on the same exit slip):

“What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?”

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: “Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones.” → Correction: This is the dominant freefall misconception, present even after demonstration. Use the Hochman because/but/so to confront it explicitly.
  • Misconception: “If air resistance is negligible, the velocity is constant during the fall.” → Correction: Velocity is constantly increasing by 9.8 m/s every second; acceleration is constant (g), not velocity.
  • Misconception: “Terminal velocity means the object stops.” → Correction: At terminal velocity the object reaches a maximum steady speed because air resistance equals gravity — it keeps moving, just no longer accelerating.

Access & Differentiation

  • ELL/ENL supports: The Hochman because/but/so scaffold itself acts as the sentence-frame for this lesson. Word-choice box: {gravity, mass, weight, freefall, air resistance, terminal velocity}. Pair the words mass and weight with concrete examples to head off the conflation.
  • IEP/SPED supports: Pre-fill the because clause; students complete the but and so. Provide a printed v-t graph template with the y-axis pre-labeled g·t increments (9.8, 19.6, 29.4, …).
  • Extensions: Compute how long it would take a feather to reach terminal velocity in air. Or: predict what fraction of the skydiver’s freefall is true freefall (no air resistance) vs. air-affected motion, and explain reasoning.

Strategy Spotlight

Hochman because/but/so (15 min) — see Phase 2, Investigation. The full process: kernel claim (“Heavier objects fall faster”) → because (surface reason) → but (counter-evidence from the simulator or demo) → so (corrected conclusion). Each clause is one to two sentences. The strategy works because it forces students to articulate their misconception explicitly — and then refute it with their own evidence — rather than have the teacher simply tell them the right answer. Self-correction is stickier than instruction.

Key Vocabulary (max 3)

  • freefall — motion under the influence of gravity alone (air resistance negligible)
  • gravity (g) — the acceleration due to Earth’s gravity, ≈ 9.8 m/s² downward near the surface
  • terminal velocity — the maximum steady speed an object reaches when air resistance equals gravity (reached only when air resistance is significant)
