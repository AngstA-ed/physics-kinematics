CCC Focus

Patterns — there is an optimal launch angle for maximum range on flat ground (45°). Angles symmetric around 45° (e.g., 30° and 60°) produce the same range with different times-of-flight. Patterns that hold across many trials are the kind HS-PS2-1 asks students to claim.

At-a-Glance

  • Duration: 42 minutes (1 period; consider double-period day if running the design challenge in full)
  • Materials: Index cards, popsicle sticks, rubber bands, cup or paper bowl (for catapult building, 1 set per group of 3); marshmallows or pom-poms (1 per launch); meter sticks; masking tape; printed target with concentric circles (one per group); access to the interactive on devices.
  • Safety: Eyes! Establish a “no launching toward faces” rule. Use marshmallows or pom-poms — not anything denser.
  • Prior knowledge: Lessons 01 (vectors), 06–08 (freefall, vertical and horizontal projectiles).

Lesson Objectives

I can… - Decompose an angled launch velocity into its horizontal and vertical components. - Predict the range and time-of-flight of a projectile launched at angle θ with speed v₀. - Identify and explain why 45° is the optimal launch angle for maximum range on level ground (with no air resistance).

Discussion Prompts

  • “Two players kick a ball at the same speed but different angles — 30° and 60°. Which lands farther?”
  • Sample student response: “Same range — they’re symmetric around 45°. The 30° kick stays low, the 60° kick goes high.”
  • “If you could launch a ball at 45° but air resistance is non-negligible, would the optimal angle still be 45°?” (Probe: real-world projectiles have optimal angles slightly below 45°.)
  • “Why does the time-of-flight depend on the vertical component but the range depend on both components?”

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: “The horizontal velocity changes during the flight.” → Correction: Horizontal velocity is constant (no horizontal force, ignoring air resistance). Only vertical velocity changes.
  • Misconception: “The peak of the trajectory is where the ball is moving fastest.” → Correction: Speed is minimum at the peak — it’s where the vertical component is zero, leaving only the (constant) horizontal component.
  • Misconception: “Higher angle = longer range.” → Correction: True only up to 45° (on flat ground, no air resistance). Beyond 45°, range decreases even though time-of-flight keeps growing.

Access & Differentiation

  • ELL/ENL supports: Sentence frames: *“At a launch angle of °, the range is m because v_x = ___ and v_y = ___.”* Word-choice box: {launch angle, range, time-of-flight, optimal, components, horizontal, vertical}.
  • IEP/SPED supports: Pre-decomposed component table on the worksheet (rows for 15°, 30°, 45°, 60°, 75°; columns for v_x, v_y, time, range — students fill in the trends rather than computing). Provide a printed protractor on the catapult.
  • Extensions: “If the launch and landing heights differ (e.g., a cliff), what is the optimal angle?” (Slightly less than 45°; depends on the height difference.) Or: have students hand-derive the range equation R = v₀² sin(2θ) / g and verify it against the simulator.

Strategy Spotlight

Active Learning — project-based design challenge (15 min).

The challenge. “Design a paper catapult that lands a marshmallow in the target circle at the front of the room (center is at distance D = 2.0 m from your launch table). Document your launch angle, your launch speed (estimate), and your prediction. You get exactly 3 launches.”

Phase 1 (4 min): Build. Random groups of 3. Each group gets the materials list above. The catapult must use exactly: 1 popsicle stick, 1 cup, 2 rubber bands. Limit the build to four minutes — the design is constrained, the calibration is the lesson.

Phase 2 (6 min): Calibrate. Groups make 2 calibration launches at any angle. They measure the actual range and back out the launch speed using v₀² = R·g / sin(2θ). They use the interactive on a phone or laptop to predict what angle to use for their final launch to hit the 2.0 m target.

Phase 3 (3 min): Final launch. One launch per group. Mark where the marshmallow lands.

Phase 4 (2 min): Debrief. Which groups landed in the target? What was their angle? Did all the successful groups choose the same angle? (They did not — multiple angles can hit the same range, by symmetry. 30° and 60° at the same v₀ have the same range.)

Why this works: Students engage with the optimization claim before they verbally state it. Teams that try 90° fail (zero range). Teams that try 45° succeed broadly. Teams that try 30° and 60° both succeed — exposing the symmetry. The experience makes the equation memorable.

Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

A soccer player kicks the ball at 14 m/s at 35° above horizontal. Use g = 9.8 m/s².

  1. What are the horizontal and vertical components of the launch velocity?
  2. How long is the ball in the air? (Assume launch and landing heights are equal.)
  3. What is the range?
  4. Predict whether kicking at 55° (same speed) lands closer or farther than the 35° kick. Explain.

Closing reflection (30 sec): What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?

Key Vocabulary (max 3)

  • launch angle (θ) — the angle above horizontal at which the projectile is launched
  • range equation — R = v₀² sin(2θ) / g, valid for level ground and no air resistance
  • optimal angle — the launch angle that produces the maximum range; 45° on level ground in vacuum
