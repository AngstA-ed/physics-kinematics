HS-PS2-1: Newton’s Second Law of Motion — Analyze data to support the claim that Newton’s second law of motion describes the mathematical relationship among the net force on a macroscopic object, its mass, and its acceleration. (Cause and Effect)

This is the lesson where acceleration becomes a measurable, sign-aware quantity. The HS-PS2-1 thread that began in Lesson 01 (vectors) reaches its first full statement here: net force changes velocity, and the rate of that change is acceleration.

Lesson Overview

Duration 42 minutes (1 period)
NYSSLS link HS-PS2-1 (anchor lesson)
CCC focus Cause and Effect — a change in velocity (cause: net force) produces acceleration (effect). Speeding up, slowing down, and changing direction all count as acceleration.
Strategy chips BTC (random groups + VNPS thin-slicing)
Materials Whiteboards or chart paper at standing height for VNPS-lite (4 stations); markers; deck of playing cards (for randomizing groups); printed thin-slicing task cards (4 levels per station); access to the interactive on devices.
Safety None.
Prior knowledge Lessons 01–03 (vectors, distance/displacement, velocity).

Lesson objectives — students can:

  • Define acceleration as the change in velocity divided by the time interval.
  • Identify positive, negative, and zero acceleration from a velocity-time graph or a written description.
  • Explain why “deceleration” is just acceleration with a sign opposite the motion.

Phase 1 · Engage (0 – 12 min)

0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Quick check-in. 60–90 seconds.

3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — Physics Classroom acceleration animations

Teacher actions. Open the Physics Classroom acceleration animations. Show the first animation (constant velocity). Pause. Show the second (speeding up). Pause. Show the third (slowing down).

Sample teacher language:

“I’m showing you three short animations of dots moving on a line. The dots are evenly spaced in time — every dot represents one second. But the spacing between them on the page changes from one animation to the next. Watch carefully. What’s the difference?”

Anticipated student responses:

  • “The dots get further apart in the second one” — affirm; that’s the visible signature of acceleration.
  • “It’s speeding up / slowing down” — affirm, then probe: “How can you tell from the picture? What exactly are you seeing?”
  • “The first one is just constant” — yes; surface that constant velocity = no acceleration.

8–12 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Capture two columns. Then:

“Some animations show dots that get further apart over time. Some show dots getting closer. Turn to your partner: what does that say about the object?”

After 90 seconds, take 2–3 responses. Surface the dispute that’s about to drive Phase 2: what number captures how the velocity is changing?

Bridge to Phase 2

By end of Phase 1, every student has sketched their initial model in their notebook: a v-t graph for “speeding up” and a v-t graph for “constant velocity.” They don’t have to be quantitative — just qualitative shapes.

Phase 2 · Explore (12 – 32 min)

12–17 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Specific prompt on the board:

“Sketch a velocity-time graph for an object that is speeding up at a constant rate. Then sketch one for an object at constant velocity. What’s the difference between the two graphs?”

This silent work surfaces the connection between v-t shape and acceleration. Many students will draw a curved line for “speeding up” — that’s a productive misconception we’ll surface in Phase 3.

17–32 min · Investigation — BTC random groups + VNPS thin-slicing

Time for the lesson’s main strategy. Form random groups of 3 using a deck of cards: shuffle, deal one card per student face-down; same-suit groups (clubs together, diamonds together, etc.). Direct groups to the four chart-paper stations around the room.

Each station has a thin-slicing task card with progressively harder puzzles:

  • Level 1: “Sketch the v-t graph for a car at constant 10 m/s east.”
  • Level 2: “Sketch the v-t graph for a car starting at 0 and reaching 20 m/s east in 5 seconds at constant acceleration.”
  • Level 3: “A v-t graph goes from 10 to −10 m/s in 4 seconds. What is the average acceleration? Is the car speeding up or slowing down?”
  • Level 4: “Sketch a v-t graph showing the car speed up, then slow down, then reverse direction. Label where acceleration changes sign.”

Three-minute rotations. Group works at chart paper; teacher walks but does not solve. Use the discussion prompts below to nudge.

Sample teacher facilitation language during the stations:

“Show me where on your graph the car is moving fastest.” “What does the slope of the line mean physically?” “If you flipped the slope upside down, what would the car be doing?”

Teacher facilitation during Explore

  • What to look for — groups discovering that the slope of the v-t graph IS the acceleration. The “aha” moment is when a group realizes that “constant acceleration” means a straight (non-horizontal) line on v-t, not a curve.
  • What to resist — do NOT write “slope = acceleration” on the board yet. Phase 3 is when that gets named.
  • What to redirect — groups stuck on Level 3 (the sign-change problem) often need: “Is the slope positive or negative? What does that tell you about whether the car is speeding up or slowing down — given the direction of motion?”

After 12 minutes of stations, gather the class around one station’s chart paper and ask: “What’s the same in everyone’s answers? What’s different?” Surface the convergence on slope = acceleration.

Phase 3 · Explain (32 – 37 min)

32–35 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

Sample teacher language:

“Look at the v-t graph on chart paper. Talk to your partner: what does the slope of this line tell us about the motion?”

After 90 seconds, take volunteers. The target consensus statement:

“The slope of a velocity-time graph is the acceleration. Positive slope = speeding up in the positive direction. Negative slope = either slowing down (if moving in the positive direction) OR speeding up in the negative direction. Zero slope = constant velocity = zero acceleration.”

35–37 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

Sample teacher language:

“Acceleration is the change in velocity ÷ time. Units: meters per second per second, or m/s². Vector — has a direction. Deceleration is just informal English for acceleration whose vector opposes the velocity — a negative acceleration on a forward-moving car. The slope of a v-t graph equals the acceleration.”

Discussion prompts to deploy here

  • “If a car’s velocity changes from 5 m/s east to 15 m/s east in 2 seconds, what’s its acceleration?”
    • Sample student response: “(15 − 5) / 2 = 5 m/s². Positive because it’s speeding up east.”
  • “What does it mean if acceleration is negative? Is the object always slowing down?”
    • Probe: “What if the object is moving west and acceleration is negative? Is it speeding up or slowing down?” (Speeding up — both vectors point west.)

Phase 4 · Elaborate (37 – 40 min)

37–39 min · Revise the model

Students reopen their initial v-t sketches from Phase 2. Specific prompt:

“Update your sketches. Add slope labels (positive, negative, zero). Use the word acceleration in a sentence describing each graph. If your ‘speeding up’ graph was a curved line, redraw it as a straight line and explain why.”

39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

Reopen one of the Physics Classroom acceleration animations. Ask:

“Predict the v-t graph for this animation. Then we’ll check it on the simulator.”

Take a volunteer’s sketch on the board; class compares to the simulator’s reading. The success criterion: the student sketches a straight line with the correct sign of slope.

Phase 5 · Evaluate (40 – 42 min)

40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Distribute exit slips. Transfer prompt:

“A sprinter starts from rest and reaches 9 m/s in 3 seconds. (a) What is her average acceleration? (b) Sketch her v-t graph. (c) After crossing the line, she slows from 9 m/s to 0 in 6 seconds. What is her acceleration during the slow-down phase? Is it positive or negative? Explain.”

Expected: (a) 3 m/s² forward. (b) Straight line from (0,0) to (3,9), slope +3. (c) −1.5 m/s², negative because velocity is decreasing — acceleration vector opposes the motion.

Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds, on the same exit slip):

“What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?”

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: “Negative acceleration always means slowing down.” → Correction: Negative acceleration means the acceleration vector points in the negative direction. Whether that speeds up or slows down an object depends on the object’s velocity sign. Negative acceleration on a westbound object speeds it up.
  • Misconception: “Constant velocity means there is acceleration.” → Correction: Constant velocity = zero acceleration. Acceleration is about change in velocity.
  • Misconception: “Acceleration is how fast something is going.” → Correction: Acceleration is how fast the velocity is changing — meters per second per second.

Access & Differentiation

  • ELL/ENL supports: Sentence frame: “The acceleration is ___ m/s² because the velocity changed by ___ m/s in ___ seconds.” Word-choice box: {acceleration, deceleration, slope, change, rate}.
  • IEP/SPED supports: Pre-printed v-t graph templates with axis labels and tick marks; students plot data points only. Pair them with a strong group at the BTC stations.
  • Extensions: Invent a v-t graph where the average velocity is positive but the acceleration is negative. Or: a v-t graph that depicts an object speeding up, then slowing down, then reversing direction.

Strategy Spotlight

Building Thinking Classrooms — random groups + vertical non-permanent surfaces (15 min) — see Phase 2, Investigation. The full process: random groups of 3 by playing-card suit; four chart-paper stations at standing height; each station has a thin-slicing task card with four progressively harder puzzles (Level 1 → 4). Three-minute rotations. Teacher walks but does not solve. After 12 minutes: “What’s the same? What’s different?” — surface the slope = acceleration insight.

The strategy works because visibly random groups signal that anyone can think with anyone, and the vertical non-permanent surface (chart paper at standing height) keeps groups on task and makes thinking public for the next group to verify.

Key Vocabulary (max 3)

  • acceleration — change in velocity ÷ time; a vector; units m/s²
  • deceleration — informal name for acceleration whose vector opposes the velocity vector (slowing down)
  • slope — on a v-t graph, the steepness of the line; equals the acceleration
