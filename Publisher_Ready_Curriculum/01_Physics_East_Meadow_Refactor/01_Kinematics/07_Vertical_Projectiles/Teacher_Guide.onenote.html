Lesson Overview

Duration 42 minutes (1 period)
NYSSLS link HS-PS2-1 (extension of freefall)
CCC focus Patterns — the upward and downward halves of a vertical-throw trajectory are symmetric: time to rise = time to fall, and the speed at the same height on the way up equals the speed on the way down (only direction differs).
Strategy chips (none)
Materials A volleyball or tennis ball; a phone or stopwatch; access to the interactive on devices.
Safety None for a soft ball thrown straight up at low height. Avoid throwing toward windows or fixtures.
Prior knowledge Lesson 06 (freefall, g, terminal velocity).

Lesson objectives — students can:

  • Describe the velocity of a vertically thrown object at three key moments: launch, peak, and landing.
  • Explain why the time to rise equals the time to fall (when launch and landing heights are the same).
  • Predict the peak height and total time-of-flight given an initial velocity.

Phase 1 · Engage (0 – 12 min)

0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Quick check-in. 60–90 seconds.

3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — toss a volleyball

Teacher actions. Toss a volleyball straight up so it rises ~2 m before falling back. A student times the air-time on a phone. Throw it three times and ask the class to record the air-times.

Sample teacher language:

“I’m going to throw this ball straight up. You time it from when it leaves my hand until it returns to my hand. Tell me — does it spend more time going up, or coming down? Or are they equal?”

Anticipated student responses:

  • “More time coming down because it speeds up” — surface; this is a productive misconception (slow-down/speed-up framing).
  • “Same time both ways because it’s symmetric” — surface; this is the lesson’s destination.
  • “Hard to tell — too fast” — also fine, surface the difficulty of estimation.

8–12 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Capture two columns. Then:

“At the very peak — the highest point — what is the ball’s velocity? What is its acceleration? Are they the same number?”

After 90 seconds, take 2–3 responses. The dispute that drives Phase 2: most students think v = 0 means a = 0 too. They don’t realize gravity never pauses.

Bridge to Phase 2

By end of Phase 1, every student has sketched their initial model: a v-t graph for the entire trip from launch to landing. They mark the peak.

Phase 2 · Explore (12 – 32 min)

12–17 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Specific prompt on the board:

“Sketch a v-t graph for a ball thrown straight up at 10 m/s. Mark t = 0 (launch), the peak, and t_landing. What value of v is at the peak? What value of a?”

Common student answers: v-t is two segments — a positive line going down to zero (rising), then a negative line going down (falling). Many students think the slope changes (it doesn’t) or that there’s a flat spot at the peak (there isn’t). Both productive starting misconceptions.

17–32 min · Investigation — interactive ball-thrown-up explorer

Students open the interactive vertical-projectile explorer. They slide v₀ and press Throw. The ball rises, peaks (with v = 0 highlighted), and falls. The v-t graph draws alongside as a single straight line through zero.

Specific challenge for students (write on board):

“Run the simulator at v₀ = 10 m/s. Then run it again at v₀ = 20 m/s. Is the second ball in the air twice as long? Predict before you check.”

Most students predict yes (linear scaling). The simulator confirms — yes, time-of-flight scales linearly with v₀, because t_of = 2v₀/g.

Teacher facilitation language during the investigation:

“What’s the slope of the v-t line? Does it change between rising and falling?” “What’s special about the moment v crosses zero?” “If you doubled v₀, what doubled? What didn’t?”

Teacher facilitation during Explore

  • What to look for — students realizing that the v-t graph is a single straight line, not two segments. The slope is −g throughout.
  • What to resist — don’t say “v = 0 at the peak but a = −g” yet. Phase 3 names it.
  • What to redirect — students who think there’s a “flat spot” at the peak need: “Look at the v-t graph in the simulator. Where’s the flat spot? Where does v cross zero? What’s the slope right at that moment?”

Phase 3 · Explain (32 – 37 min)

32–35 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

Sample teacher language:

“At the peak — when the ball is momentarily at rest — talk to your partner. What’s the velocity? What’s the acceleration? Are they the same number?”

Take volunteers. The target consensus statement:

“At the peak, v = 0 (momentarily). But a = −g still. Gravity never pauses. That’s why the ball immediately starts falling — there’s still acceleration even when there’s no velocity. The whole trip is a single straight line on v-t with constant slope −g.”

35–37 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

Sample teacher language:

“Peak — the highest point of the throw. v = 0 momentarily, a = −g. Time-of-flight — total time from launch to landing. For symmetric throws, t_of = 2v₀/g. Symmetry — time up = time down; speed at any height on the way up equals speed at the same height on the way down (only direction differs).”

Discussion prompts to deploy here

  • “If a ball is launched at 10 m/s, how fast is it moving when it returns to the launch height?”
    • Sample student response: “10 m/s — same magnitude, opposite direction. That’s the symmetry.”
  • “Why is the v-t graph a straight line, even though the ball is sometimes going up and sometimes going down?”
    • Sample student response: “Because acceleration is constant (a = −g) the whole time. The slope of v-t = a, so a constant slope means a constant acceleration.”

Phase 4 · Elaborate (37 – 40 min)

37–39 min · Revise the model

Students reopen their initial v-t sketch from Phase 2. Specific prompt:

“Update your sketch. Add: (1) the peak time t = v₀/g, (2) the launch and landing velocities (same magnitude, opposite signs), (3) the slope of the line (it’s −g throughout — but draw the temptation to flatten it at the peak, then refute it).”

39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

Re-call the volleyball toss. Ask:

“If your air-time was 1.0 s, what was your launch speed? Use today’s tools.”

Target: t_of = 2v₀/g, so v₀ = g·t_of/2 = 9.8 × 0.5 = 4.9 m/s. Take a volunteer’s calculation; class verifies.

Phase 5 · Evaluate (40 – 42 min)

40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Distribute exit slips. Transfer prompt:

“A volleyball is thrown straight up with an initial velocity of 12 m/s. Use g = 9.8 m/s². (a) How long until it reaches its peak? (b) What is the maximum height above the launch point? (c) What is the velocity when it returns to the launch height? (d) Total time-of-flight?”

Expected: (a) 1.22 s. (b) 7.35 m. (c) 12 m/s downward (same magnitude as launch). (d) 2.45 s. See Answer_Key.docx.

Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds, on the same exit slip):

“What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?”

Common Misconceptions

  • Misconception: “At the peak, both velocity and acceleration are zero.” → Correction: Velocity is zero, but acceleration is still −g. Gravity does not pause; that’s why the ball begins to fall again immediately.
  • Misconception: “It takes longer to fall than to rise (because the ball is slowing down on the way up).” → Correction: Both halves take exactly the same time when launch and landing heights match — that’s the symmetry.
  • Misconception: “Throwing harder makes the ball stay up longer in proportion.” → Correction: Time to peak = v₀/g, so doubling v₀ doubles the air-time. Linear, not magic.

Access & Differentiation

  • ELL/ENL supports: Sentence frames: *“At the peak, the ball’s velocity is ___ but its acceleration is ___.”* Word-choice box: {peak, launch, landing, symmetry, time-of-flight, gravity, zero}.
  • IEP/SPED supports: Provide a pre-printed v-t template with the peak time and zero crossing marked; students fill in the remaining values. Use color (purple for upward, blue for downward) to highlight the sign change.
  • Extensions: Predict the speed (magnitude) of the ball at half its peak height. Or: a ball thrown up from a balcony lands on the ground below — how does the asymmetry between rise and fall times change?

  • time-of-flight — total time from launch to landing
