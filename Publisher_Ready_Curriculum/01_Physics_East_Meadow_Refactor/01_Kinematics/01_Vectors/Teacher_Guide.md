## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

**HS-PS2-1: Newton's Second Law of Motion** — *Analyze data to support the claim that Newton's second law of motion describes the mathematical relationship among the net force on a macroscopic object, its mass, and its acceleration.* **(Cause and Effect)**

This lesson introduces the *vector* nature of the quantities (force, acceleration) that HS-PS2-1 requires students to reason about. Vectors are revisited in Lessons 02–05 (motion quantities) and Lessons 06–09 (gravity, projectiles).

### Phenomenon

Physics Classroom — Vector Animations: <http://www.physicsclassroom.com/mmedia/vectors/va.cfm>

Two arrows added head-to-tail produce the same resultant regardless of which arrow comes first. The phenomenon invites students to notice that the order in which vectors are added does not change the result — a foothold for commutativity of vector addition.

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | 42 minutes (1 period) |
| **NYSSLS link** | HS-PS2-1 |
| **CCC focus** | Cause and Effect — how the *direction* of a force changes motion, not just its magnitude. |
| **Strategy chips** | RESTORATIVE CIRCLE (unit opener) · ACTIVE LEARNING (Vector Golf stations) |
| **Materials** | Graph paper or vector grids (1 per student); rulers (1 per pair); printed Vector Golf course (4 stations × 1 copy each); chart paper for VNPS-style station work; colored markers (purple + blue per group, mirroring the brand palette); the slider-based interactive on each device or projected. |
| **Safety** | None. |
| **Prior knowledge** | Adding signed numbers; reading a coordinate grid; the difference between a measurement (number) and a measurement with direction (e.g., "30 mph" vs "30 mph north"). |

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- Describe a vector quantity using both **magnitude** and **direction**.
- Add two vectors graphically using the head-to-tail method.
- Decompose a vector into its x- and y-components.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 12 min)*

**Phenomenon-based lesson flow (PPTX slide 32):** show the phenomenon → notice & wonder → prioritize a question through a CCC lens → develop an initial model.

### 0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

**Restorative Circle — unit opener.** As students arrive, arrange chairs in a circle (or have students stand in a circle if the room is tight). Pass a small object hand-to-hand (a marker, a ball, a set of keys — anything that travels by hand). Each student answers a single prompt: *"What is one experience you've had with motion this week?"* Honor pass-rights — students may say "pass" without explanation. Do not respond to or evaluate any answer; nod, pass to the next student.

**Sample teacher language to open the circle:**

> "Today we start a new unit, and we're going to start with a circle. The point isn't to be deep — it's just to land in this room together before we start working. The question is: what is one experience you've had with motion this week? Pass-rights are honored — you can say 'pass' for any reason. I'll go first, then [name to your left]."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "I rode my bike to school" — affirm; this is a real-world example you'll come back to.
- "I dropped my phone" — connect to Lesson 06 (freefall).
- "I scored a goal" / "I caught a ball" — projectile, Lesson 09 will revisit.
- "Pass" — fine, move on. Don't push.

Close the circle: *"What's something you noticed about what people shared?"* — invite one volunteer; this is the bridge to Phase 1's content.

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook

**Teacher actions.** Project the [Physics Classroom Vector Animation](http://www.physicsclassroom.com/mmedia/vectors/va.cfm). Let it loop silently for ~30 seconds before saying anything. Resist the urge to narrate.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "I'm not going to tell you what this is yet. Watch carefully. After a minute I'll ask: what do you notice, and what do you wonder?"

After 30 seconds:

> "OK. What do you notice?"

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "There are arrows" — affirm, then probe: "What do the arrows do?"
- "They get added together head-to-tail" — *if a student says this*, surface it for the class: "That's a great word. We'll come back to it."
- "The dashed arrow is the resultant" — rare but possible. *Do not validate as 'right' yet.* Instead: "Interesting — what makes you think that's a special arrow?"
- "It looks like the order doesn't matter" — note this; we'll come back to it in Phase 3.

### 8–12 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Capture two columns on the board: **Notice** and **Wonder**. Take 4–5 student observations (mix of voices). Then transition:

> "Turn to your partner. One thing you noticed about the arrows. One thing you wondered. Pick a wondering you'd like the class to figure out today."

After 90 seconds, take 2–3 wonderings. Write them on the board. The most useful ones to surface:

- "Why is the dashed arrow shorter than the two solid arrows added together?"
- "Does it matter which arrow comes first?"
- "How would you tell someone how to draw the dashed arrow?"

**CCC framing.** Briefly note: "We're going to investigate *cause and effect* — when two forces or two motions combine, what determines the result? It's not just about how big each one is."

### Bridge to Phase 2

By the end of Phase 1, every student should have a written **initial model** — a sketch in their notebook answering: *"If arrow A points 4 squares right and arrow B points 3 squares up, where does the second arrow's tip end up — and how would you describe that ending point in words?"*

## Phase 2 · Explore *(12 – 32 min)*

**Investigation.** Students engage with the phenomenon through hands-on Vector Golf stations and the slider-based interactive. Teacher walks but does not solve.

### 12–17 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Distribute graph paper. Students sketch their initial model individually. Specific prompt on the board:

> *"Arrow A points 4 squares right. Arrow B points 3 squares up. Draw both arrows head-to-tail starting from the origin. Where does B's tip end up? In one sentence, describe that ending point."*

This is silent work. No collaboration yet. The point is to surface each student's *current* mental model — including misconceptions — before the investigation surfaces the correct rule.

**What to look for during the silent work:**

- Students who add 4 + 3 = 7 (additivity misconception — they're treating vectors like scalars). Mark these mentally; you'll surface them in Phase 3.
- Students who draw the resultant correctly but can't articulate it in words. Mark these too — they have intuition without vocabulary, which is exactly the gap vocabulary closes.

### 17–32 min · Investigation — Vector Golf stations (Active Learning)

**Setup.** Four stations around the room, each with a different "course" (a starting point and a target on a coordinate grid). Each station has chart paper at standing height (vertical non-permanent surface lite). Form **random groups of 3** using a deck of cards: shuffle, deal one card per student face-down; same-suit groups (clubs together, diamonds together, etc.). The randomness signals that *anyone* can think with *anyone*.

**Three-minute rotations.** At each station, the group must use head-to-tail vector addition to plot a sequence of arrows that lands on the target. They leave their solution on the chart paper for the next group to verify. Walk the room and ask the discussion prompts; resist solving — let groups argue.

**Sample teacher facilitation language during station work:**

> "What's your evidence that arrow lands on the target?"
> "Could you have gotten there with fewer arrows? More?"
> "What does the group BEFORE you have — does their solution work?"

### Teacher facilitation during Explore

- **What to look for** — groups discovering the head-to-tail rule by doing it. The "aha" moment is when a group realizes that A + B and B + A land in the same place.
- **What to resist** — do NOT explain the formal rule yet. The purpose of Phase 2 is for students to surface the pattern themselves. Resist the urge to validate "right answers" mid-investigation.
- **What to redirect** — if a group is stuck, redirect with: "What did the previous group try? What's different about your course?" If a group thinks the order of vectors matters for the resultant, let them test it — they will discover the commutativity themselves.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(32 – 37 min)*

**Sense-making + class consensus.** Students articulate the pattern they discovered. The teacher names the formal vocabulary (≤ 3 terms) — *only after* students have done the explaining work themselves. Per NYSSLS observation checklist item 5, vocabulary lands in the second half of the lesson.

### 32–35 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

Bring the class back together at one chart paper. Pick a group's solution that shows two arrows (A and B) with their resultant. Ask:

> "If A points right (magnitude 4) and B points up (magnitude 3), why does the resultant come out to 5 — not 7? Use the grid to convince your partner."

After 90 seconds, take volunteers. The target consensus statement:

> "When two vectors are perpendicular, the resultant's *magnitude* follows the Pythagorean theorem: c² = a² + b². When they're parallel, the magnitudes add. When they're anti-parallel, they subtract."

If no student arrives at the Pythagorean connection, prompt: "Look at the triangle the three arrows form. What do we know about right triangles?"

### 35–37 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

Now — and only now — name the three formal terms. Write them on the board with definitions and an example.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "When a quantity has both a *size* and a *direction*, we call that a **vector**. The size is its **magnitude**. The way it points is its **direction**. Today we worked with vectors of length 4 and 3 that pointed perpendicular to each other; their resultant had magnitude 5 and a specific direction."

### Discussion prompts to deploy here

- "When two vectors point the same direction, what happens to their resultant? When they point opposite? When they're perpendicular?"
  - *Sample student response:* "When perpendicular, the resultant is the diagonal of a rectangle, so we use Pythagoras to get its size."
- "Could two different pairs of vectors give the same resultant? How would you draw an example?"
- "If you swap A and B in head-to-tail addition, does the resultant change? Why or why not?"

---

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(37 – 40 min)*

**Apply the new ideas to the original phenomenon.** Students return to their initial model from Phase 2 and revise it using today's vocabulary and the pattern they surfaced.

### 37–39 min · Revise the model

Each student reopens their initial model sketch from Phase 2. Specific prompt on the board:

> *"Update your sketch. Use the words **magnitude** and **direction** in one sentence describing where B's tip ends up. If your initial answer was 7, write a sentence underneath explaining why the correct answer is 5."*

This is the moment where the student's *own* initial misconception (if they had one) gets corrected by their *own* hand. The Hochman *because/but/so* move can scaffold the correction:

> "I first wrote 4 + 3 = 7 *because* I added the magnitudes, *but* the arrows are perpendicular, *so* the resultant uses Pythagoras: √(16+9) = 5."

### 39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

Re-show the original Physics Classroom vector animation. Ask the class:

> "Now that we've worked this out, what would you say to a friend who watched this animation and said 'I don't get it'?"

Take one volunteer's response. The target response uses **vector**, **magnitude**, **direction** in a single sentence — and that's the lesson's success criterion in action.

---

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

**Transfer task assessment.** A new phenomenon (different from the lesson's hook) for students to apply their learning. Per the NYSSLS observation checklist (item 8), this is the in-lesson assessment.

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Distribute exit slips. The transfer prompt:

> *"A boat sails 5 km east, then 12 km north. Sketch the resultant displacement on a grid and find its magnitude. Show one sentence of reasoning."*

Expected: 13 km, with reasoning that references the Pythagorean theorem or head-to-tail rule. See `Answer_Key.docx` for the rubric and tolerance.

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds, on the same exit slip):**

> "What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?"

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "Vectors with bigger numbers are always longer." → **Correction:** Two vectors of equal magnitude but different directions add to *different* resultants — direction matters as much as magnitude.
- **Misconception:** "Adding vectors is the same as adding their magnitudes." → **Correction:** That is only true when they point in exactly the same direction. In every other case the resultant magnitude is *less* than the sum (and equals zero when they cancel).
- **Misconception:** "The resultant is always longer than each input vector." → **Correction:** Not when the vectors point opposite directions. The resultant can be shorter than either input, or even zero.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frames — *"The resultant of A + B has magnitude ___ because ___."* and *"Adding A and B gives a resultant that points ___."* Word-choice box: {magnitude, direction, resultant, components, scalar}. Visual-first framing — show two arrows on a grid before introducing the word *vector*. Bilingual glossary slot in student notebook for the three vocabulary words.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Use the printed Vector Golf course as a chunked, scaffolded organizer; pre-draw the first arrow for each problem so students start from the head of an existing arrow. Provide a step list: (1) draw A from origin, (2) draw B from head of A, (3) draw R from origin to head of B. Allow oral responses for the exit ticket.
- **Extensions:** After Vector Golf, prompt advanced students to find the *minimum* number of vector additions to reach the target — introduces optimization thinking and previews path-planning intuition. Or: ask them to invent a 3-vector course where the resultant is exactly zero.
