## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

This row of the Forces unit names no specific Performance Expectation, but it directly supports **HS-PS2-1** (a net force causes acceleration). The twist students must confront: an object moving in a circle at *constant speed* is still **accelerating**, because its velocity *direction* keeps changing. The net force that produces that acceleration points toward the center of the circle. The CCC focus is **Cause and Effect** — a center-directed (centripetal) force is the *cause*, and center-directed acceleration (a curved path) is the *effect*.

### Phenomenon

- Motorcycle "lowside" crash in a curve — the moment grip is lost, the bike stops turning and slides straight: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wRXOf-9YJU0>
- Water stays in a bucket swung in a vertical circle: <https://youtu.be/Zulw5bQ18Kk>
- A ball or rubber stopper swung overhead on a string (live demo).

### Javalab / Labs

- Circular Motion Labs — swing a rubber stopper on a string, vary radius and speed, and *feel* the tension change: <https://javalab.org/en/circular_motion_en/>
- oPhysics circular motion simulation (force vectors on a revolving object): <https://ophysics.com/r1.html>

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | 42 minutes (1 period) |
| **NYSSLS link** | Supports HS-PS2-1 (net force → acceleration, here center-directed) |
| **CCC focus** | Cause and Effect — a center-seeking net force *causes* a curved path; remove it and the path goes straight. |
| **Strategy chips** | ACTIVE LEARNING (swing-and-predict stopper stations) |
| **Materials** | Rubber stoppers on ~1 m strings (or beaded keychains); a few washers/weights; stopwatch; safety goggles; open floor space; whiteboards/markers. |
| **Safety** | Goggles on for all swinging. Clear a wide radius; swing in a vertical or horizontal plane away from faces. Inspect strings for fraying. |
| **Prior knowledge** | Velocity is a vector (size + direction); acceleration is *any* change in velocity (Lessons 01–04); net force causes acceleration. |

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- Explain why an object in uniform circular motion is accelerating even though its speed is constant.
- Identify the real force (tension, friction, gravity, normal) that acts as the **centripetal force** in a given situation.
- Use **Fc = m·v²/r** to relate force, mass, speed, and **radius**, and predict how each change affects the others.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Opening Connection (SEL)

Quick whip-around, one sentence each (pass allowed): *"Name a time you felt like you were being thrown outward — a car turn, a spinning ride, a sharp bike turn."* You are deliberately collecting the *feeling* of being thrown outward; you will name and overturn it as the centrifugal misconception in Phase 3.

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — the lowside crash

**Teacher actions.** Play the motorcycle lowside clip (pause the instant the tire loses grip). Replay in slow motion. Ask students to track the *direction* the bike travels the moment grip is lost.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Watch the curve. The rider is turning — turning — and the *instant* the tire loses grip, which way does the bike go? Not more curved. Watch."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "It shoots straight off the road." — exactly; capture *straight*.
- "It slides outward / centrifugal force throws it out." — capture this; we will test it. "Outward, or just *straight ahead*? Hold that thought."
- "The road was slippery, so it lost the force keeping it turning." — gold; "What force was that?"

### 8–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two-column board capture. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: while the bike was *successfully* turning, something had to keep pulling it into the curve. What was it, and which way did it point?"

Target seed: *something pointed inward (toward the center of the curve) — friction from the tires.* Leave open if no one lands it.

### Bridge to Phase 2

Every student leaves with a one-sentence **initial model**: "The bike kept turning because ______, and it went straight when ______."

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 30 min)*

### 10–14 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt on the board:

> *"Draw the bike on the curved road from above. Draw an arrow showing the force that keeps it turning. Then predict: if that force vanishes, draw the dotted path the bike takes."*

This surfaces the inward-vs-outward intuition before any vocabulary.

### 14–30 min · Investigation — swing it, feel it, let it go (ACTIVE LEARNING)

Goggles on. Stopper-on-a-string stations (rotate or run as a carousel):

1. **Which way is the pull?** Swing the stopper in a horizontal circle overhead at steady speed. *Which way does your hand pull on the string — toward you (center) or away?* What is the string doing to the stopper?
2. **Let it go.** On a count, *release* the string mid-swing (point it at an open wall, not a person). *Which way does the stopper fly — outward along the radius, or straight along a tangent?* Repeat and watch carefully.
3. **Speed and radius.** Swing faster: does the string pull harder or softer? Choke up to a *shorter* radius at the same speed: harder or softer? Add a washer (more mass): harder or softer?

**Teacher facilitation language:**

> "Feel your hand. Are you pulling the stopper *in* or pushing it *out*?"
> "When you let go, did it curve outward — or go straight in the direction it was already heading?"
> "Faster swing — what happens to the tension you feel? Shorter string — what happens?"

### Teacher facilitation during Explore

- **What to look for** — students reporting the *released* stopper flies off on a **tangent** (straight), not radially outward; and that faster/shorter/heavier all *increase* the felt tension.
- **What to resist** — don't supply "centripetal" yet; let their hands feel the inward pull.
- **What to redirect** — students who insist the stopper flew "outward" should re-run station 2 and sight along the path; it leaves on the tangent line.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(30 – 36 min)*

### 30–33 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "Across every station, which way did the force point — and what happened the instant we removed it?"

Target consensus:

> "The string always pulled the stopper *toward the center*. The speed was steady, but the *direction* kept changing — so the velocity changed — so it was accelerating. Remove the force and it flies off straight (tangent), because nothing is changing its direction anymore."

### 33–36 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms) + the misconception

**Sample teacher language:**

> "That inward, center-seeking pull is the **centripetal force** — *centripetal* literally means 'center-seeking.' It produces a **centripetal acceleration** pointed at the center, because the velocity *direction* is changing. How sharp the turn is depends on the **radius**. And here's the key: there is **no** 'centrifugal force' throwing you out. That outward *feeling* is just your inertia — your body wanting to go straight while the car/string curves you in. The only real force is the inward one."

Name the real provider each time: *tension* (string), *friction* (tires, the lowside), *gravity* (orbit, top of a vertical loop), *normal force* (a banked turn or the bucket bottom).

### Discussion prompts to deploy here

- "When you swing the bucket of water overhead, why doesn't the water fall out at the top?"
  - *Sample student response:* "It's moving fast and wants to go straight; the bucket bottom pushes it inward (toward the center) faster than gravity could pull it out, so it stays in."
- "A car turns left. Which way does the passenger feel thrown, and which way is the real force?"
  - *Sample student response:* "You feel thrown right (outward), but that's inertia — the real force, friction, pushes the car left, toward the center."

---

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(36 – 40 min)*

### 36–38 min · Revise the model

> *"Return to your bike drawing. Label the inward arrow 'centripetal force (friction)' and label the straight dotted release path 'tangent — inertia, no net force.' Write one revised sentence using centripetal force and radius."*

### 38–40 min · Quick quantitative reasoning (Fc = m·v²/r)

> "From the Reference Table, Fc = m·v²/r. In station 3 you doubled the speed. What happened to the tension?"

Target: "It got about *four times* as hard, because v is squared." Follow up: "Shorter radius at the same speed?" → "Harder, because r is in the denominator." Tie directly back to what their hands felt.

---

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

> *"A 0.20 kg ball is swung in a horizontal circle of radius 0.50 m at a constant speed of 3.0 m/s.
> (a) Is the ball accelerating? Explain in one sentence.
> (b) Calculate the centripetal force on the ball. Show the equation and units.
> (c) A student says a 'centrifugal force' pushes the ball outward. Is the student correct? Explain."*

Expected: (a) Yes — direction (and so velocity) is changing. (b) Fc = m·v²/r = 0.20 · (3.0)² / 0.50 = 3.6 N, directed toward the center. (c) No — the only real force is the inward (centripetal) tension; the outward feeling is inertia, not a force. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "What is one thing today that changed how you think about turning or spinning? Who helped you see it?"

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "A 'centrifugal force' throws objects outward in a turn." → **Correction:** There is no outward force. Objects tend to go *straight* (inertia); the only real force is the inward **centripetal force** that curves the path. The outward "throw" is a feeling, not a force.
- **Misconception:** "Constant speed means no acceleration." → **Correction:** Acceleration is any change in *velocity*; in a circle the *direction* changes constantly, so there is centripetal acceleration even at constant speed.
- **Misconception:** "Centripetal force is a new, separate kind of force." → **Correction:** It is always provided by a real, familiar force — tension, friction, gravity, or normal force — acting toward the center.
- **Misconception:** "Released objects fly outward along the radius." → **Correction:** They leave on the **tangent**, continuing in the straight-line direction they had at the instant of release.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frame: *"The ___ pulls the object toward the ___, so the object's ___ changes and it accelerates ___."* Word-choice box: {center, tension, friction, gravity, direction, inward, tangent}. Pair *centripetal* with a hand-pulling-inward gesture.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide a pre-drawn circle with the center marked; students draw and label only the inward arrow and the tangent release line. Offer the swing demo as a hands-on alternative to written explanation. Pre-fill the Fc equation with values to plug in.
- **Extensions:** Have students explain a *banked* highway curve or a satellite in orbit, naming which real force is centripetal, and reason about how doubling the radius changes Fc at fixed speed.

## Strategy Spotlight

**ACTIVE LEARNING (predict-and-test stations).** Today's core is a hands-on, predict-then-feel investigation. Students physically swing a stopper and (1) *feel* whether their hand pulls inward or outward, and (2) *predict* the release path, then test it by letting go. The "let it go" moment is the lesson's hinge: students who expect a curved/outward exit watch the stopper leave on a tangent, which directly dismantles the centrifugal-force misconception through their own observation rather than teacher assertion. Keep predictions written *before* each test (commitment increases the conceptual payoff), insist on goggles, and have each group sight along the release path to confirm "straight, not outward." Time-box each station to ~5 minutes.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **centripetal force** — the net inward, "center-seeking" force that keeps an object moving in a circle; always provided by a real force (tension, friction, gravity, or normal force): Fc = m·v²/r
- **centripetal acceleration** — the center-directed acceleration of an object in circular motion, caused by its constantly changing velocity *direction*: ac = v²/r
- **radius** — the distance from the center of the circle to the object; smaller radius means a sharper turn and (at fixed speed) a larger required force
