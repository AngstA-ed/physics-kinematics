## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

This lesson is the heart of **HS-PS2-1**: *"Analyze data to support the claim that Newton's second law of motion describes the mathematical relationship among the net force on a macroscopic object, its mass, and its acceleration."* It builds directly on Lesson 01 (Newton's First Law), where students established the *zero-net-force* baseline — motion does not change when forces are balanced. Here we ask the next question: when the net force is **not** zero, *how much* does the motion change? The answer is the proportional, mathematical relationship **F_net = m·a**. On the 2025 Physics Reference Table this appears as **a = F_net / m**. The lesson also seeds **HS-PS2-2** (momentum) by treating force as the agent of change in motion.

### Phenomenon

- **Slingshot launch:** the same rubber band, pulled back the same distance, launches three different-size balls (ping-pong, golf, steel) — the light ball rockets away while the heavy one barely leaves. Same pull, different acceleration.
- **Alternate (no slingshot needed):** an empty cart vs. a loaded cart given the *same* push — the loaded cart accelerates far less.

### Javalab / Labs

- Force and movement simulation: <https://javalab.org/en/force_movement_en/>
- oPhysics force-and-motion / inclined plane: <https://ophysics.com/f1.html>

### Assessments

- HS-PS2-1 cluster task **"The Gyro Drop"** — students analyze acceleration data from a drop ride to argue the F_net = m·a relationship.
- HS-PS2-1 cluster task **"Newton's 2nd Law"** — data-analysis cluster on net force, mass, and acceleration. The Exit Ticket below is the formative check for this lesson.

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | 42 minutes (1 period) |
| **NYSSLS link** | HS-PS2-1 (core) → HS-PS2-2 (thread) |
| **CCC focus** | Cause and Effect — net force is the *cause*; acceleration is the *effect*. Bigger cause → bigger effect; bigger mass → smaller effect. |
| **Strategy chips** | HOCHMAN (Because/But/So sentence expansion on F_net = m·a) |
| **Materials** | Rubber band/slingshot frame; ping-pong, golf, and steel balls (or 3 graduated masses); a rolling cart; objects to load the cart; whiteboards/markers; the 2025 Reference Table. |
| **Safety** | Launch balls into a soft target or open floor only — never toward people. Keep walkways clear for the cart. |
| **Prior knowledge** | Lesson 01 — net force, balanced/unbalanced forces, inertia; Kinematics — acceleration as the *rate of change* of velocity. |

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- State Newton's Second Law in words and as the equation **F_net = m·a** (equivalently **a = F_net / m**).
- Predict how acceleration changes when net force or mass changes, and justify it with the equation.
- Solve a quantitative problem: given any two of F_net, m, and a, find the third, with units in **newtons**.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Do Now + Opening Connection

Post the Do Now: *"In one sentence, what does it take to change how fast something is moving? (Hint from yesterday: balanced or unbalanced?)"* Take one or two responses to surface the bridge from Lesson 01: it takes an **unbalanced** (net) force.

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — the slingshot launch

**Teacher actions.** Hold the slingshot frame steady. Pull the same rubber band back the *same distance* each time and launch the ping-pong ball, then the golf ball, then the steel ball into a soft target. The light ball flies fast and far; the heavy one barely moves.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Same band, same pull every time — so the same push. Watch how *differently* each ball takes off. What is the pull doing differently to each one?"

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "The heavy one is harder to move." — capture; we will sharpen "harder to move" into *less acceleration*.
- "You pulled the light one harder." — correct gently: "Same pull every time — that's the whole point."
- "The big ball weighs more so it goes slower." — capture "more mass → less change in motion."

### 8–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two columns on the board. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: the *push* was the same on all three, so what made the light ball speed up so much more? Use the word *mass* in your answer."

Target insight to listen for (leave open if no one lands it): *same force, but less mass means more speeding-up.*

### Bridge to Phase 2

Every student leaves Phase 1 with a one-sentence **initial model**: "The light ball sped up more because ______."

---

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 30 min)*

### 10–15 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt on the board:

> *"Draw the three balls leaving the slingshot. Draw one arrow for the push (same size on each) and a second arrow for how fast each speeds up. Then write one sentence predicting which ball speeds up the most and why."*

This surfaces the proportional intuition before any equation.

### 15–30 min · Investigation — Javalab force-and-movement sim

Open <https://javalab.org/en/force_movement_en/> (or the loaded/empty cart if running hands-on). Students work the worksheet table in two rounds:

1. **Hold mass constant, change force.** Double the applied force. Record what happens to acceleration.
2. **Hold force constant, change mass.** Double the mass. Record what happens to acceleration.

**Teacher facilitation language:**

> "When you doubled the force and kept the mass the same — what did the acceleration do?"
> "Now keep the force the same and double the mass — which way did the acceleration go?"
> "Can you write that as a fraction? Acceleration equals what, over what?"

### Teacher facilitation during Explore

- **What to look for** — students seeing acceleration **double** when force doubles (direct), and acceleration **halve** when mass doubles (inverse).
- **What to resist** — don't hand them F_net = m·a yet; let the data build the pattern so the equation *names* what they already found.
- **What to redirect** — students who change force *and* mass at once should isolate one variable per trial (controlled investigation).

---

## Phase 3 · Explain *(30 – 36 min)*

### 30–33 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "Put your two patterns into one rule. Acceleration goes ___ when force goes up, and ___ when mass goes up."

Target consensus:

> "Acceleration is bigger when the net force is bigger, and smaller when the mass is bigger. Acceleration = net force ÷ mass."

### 33–36 min · Vocabulary introduction (≤ 3 terms)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "The single push left over after we combine all forces is the **net force**. The speeding-up or slowing-down it produces is the **acceleration**. And we measure force in **newtons** — one newton is exactly the force that gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s². Put it together and you get Newton's Second Law: **F_net = m·a**, or **a = F_net / m** on your Reference Table."

### Discussion prompts to deploy here

- "If you push two carts with the same net force but one has twice the mass, how do their accelerations compare?"
  - *Sample student response:* "The heavy one has half the acceleration — mass is on the bottom of the fraction."
- "A 2 kg ball accelerates at 3 m/s². What net force acted on it?"
  - *Sample student response:* "F = m·a = 2 × 3 = 6 newtons."

---

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(36 – 40 min)*

### 36–39 min · Revise the model + HOCHMAN sentence expansion

Students return to the three-ball drawing and re-label arrows using *net force* and *acceleration*. Then run the Hochman move (see Strategy Spotlight) — complete the Because/But/So expansion of F_net = m·a:

> *"The steel ball accelerated the least **because** ____, **but** ____, **so** ____."*

### 39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "Why does a loaded delivery truck take longer to speed up than an empty one, even with the same engine force? Answer in one sentence using *net force*, *mass*, and *acceleration*."

Target: "Same net force, but more mass, so the acceleration is smaller (a = F_net / m)."

---

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

> *"A net force of 12 N acts on a 4 kg cart.
> (a) Find the cart's acceleration. Show F_net = m·a (or a = F_net / m).
> (b) If the mass were doubled to 8 kg with the same 12 N, what is the new acceleration?
> (c) In one sentence, explain what happened to the acceleration and why."*

Expected: (a) a = 12/4 = **3 m/s²**; (b) a = 12/8 = **1.5 m/s²**; (c) doubling the mass halved the acceleration because acceleration is inversely proportional to mass. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "What is one thing today that changed how you think about force and motion? Who helped you make sense of it?"

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "More force always means more speed." → **Correction:** More net force means more *acceleration* (rate of change of velocity), not automatically more speed at a given instant.
- **Misconception:** "Heavy and light objects accelerate the same under the same push." → **Correction:** For the same net force, more mass gives *less* acceleration — mass is in the denominator (a = F_net / m).
- **Misconception:** "Each separate force has its own acceleration." → **Correction:** Only the single **net force** (all forces combined) determines the acceleration.

---

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frame: *"When the net force ___ and the mass stays the same, the acceleration ___."* Word-choice box: {net force, mass, acceleration, newton, doubles, halves}. Pair *acceleration* with a "speeding-up" hand gesture; pair *newton* with the 1 kg → 1 m/s² mental image.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide the equation triangle (F_net on top; m and a on the bottom) so students can cover the unknown to see the operation. Offer a pre-built data table for the Javalab sim so students focus on the pattern, not table setup.
- **Extensions:** Have students use the Javalab sim to find the net force that gives a 5 kg object an acceleration of 4 m/s² (answer 20 N), then predict and verify the acceleration for the same force on a 10 kg object.

## Strategy Spotlight

**HOCHMAN (Because/But/So sentence expansion).** Sentence-level writing makes abstract equations precise. In Phase 4, students expand a single kernel sentence — *"The steel ball accelerated the least"* — using the three Hochman conjunctions, each forcing a different kind of reasoning:

- **because** → a *cause* ("…because it has the most mass and a = F_net / m")
- **but** → a *contrast* ("…but the net force on it was the same as on the other balls")
- **so** → a *consequence* ("…so dividing the same net force by a larger mass gives a smaller acceleration")

Model one example aloud, then have students write their own for a different ball. Collect a few to check that "because" names the *cause* (mass), not just restates the claim — this is the comprehension signal you are looking for, and it maps directly onto the Cause-and-Effect CCC.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **net force** — the single force equivalent to all forces on an object combined (as vectors); the *cause* of acceleration
- **acceleration** — the rate at which an object's velocity changes; equal to net force divided by mass (a = F_net / m)
- **newton** — the SI unit of force; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s², the force that accelerates a 1 kg mass at 1 m/s²
