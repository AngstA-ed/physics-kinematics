### NYSSLS Standards

This lesson targets **HS-PS2-4** — "Use mathematical representations of Newton's Law of Gravitation and Coulomb's Law to describe and predict the gravitational and electrostatic forces between objects." The CCC focus is **Patterns**: the same *inverse-square* mathematical pattern governs both gravity and electric force, so the structure students master here transfers directly to Coulomb's Law in the later Electrostatics unit. Today the focus is gravitation; name the parallel pattern but do not teach Coulomb's Law yet.

## Lesson Overview

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | 42 minutes (1 period) |
| **NYSSLS link** | HS-PS2-4 (gravitation; parallels Coulomb's Law) |
| **CCC focus** | Patterns — the inverse-square relationship is a repeating mathematical pattern across gravity and, later, electric force. |
| **Strategy chips** | HOCHMAN (sentence-level literacy: Because/But/So + appositive on the inverse-square law) |
| **Materials** | Projector + PhET Gravity Force Lab Basics (one device per pair if possible); 2025 Reference Tables; whiteboards/markers; calculators. |
| **Safety** | None — simulation and pencil-and-paper. |
| **Prior knowledge** | Weight as a force (Fg = mg), the difference between a force and a property, vectors as size + direction. |

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- State the law of universal gravitation in their own words: every mass attracts every other mass, and the pull depends on the masses and the distance between them.
- Use **Fg = G·m₁·m₂/r²** (2025 Reference Table) to calculate or compare gravitational forces.
- Apply the **inverse-square law** as a *pattern*: when distance doubles, the force drops to one-quarter.
- Distinguish **mass** (an unchanging amount of matter) from weight (the gravitational force on that mass).

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Do Now / Opening Connection

Post the Do Now: *"You weigh about 1/6 as much on the Moon as on Earth — but a scale that counts atoms would read exactly the same. How can both be true?"* Take one or two responses; do not resolve it. This seeds the mass-vs-weight distinction you'll name in Phase 3.

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — Why doesn't the Moon fall?

**Teacher actions.** Show the Moon-orbit image or the Khan clip. Pose the anchoring question.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Earth's gravity reaches all the way to the Moon — it's what holds the Moon in orbit. So why doesn't the Moon just fall down onto us like a dropped ball?"

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "The Moon is too far away for gravity to reach." — surface; we'll show gravity *does* reach, just weaker with distance.
- "There's no gravity in space." — common misconception; flag it, address in Phase 3.
- "It's moving sideways fast enough to keep missing Earth." — excellent; capture it.

### 8–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Capture two columns. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: name two things that might change how strongly two objects pull on each other."

Target: *how much stuff (mass) each has, and how far apart they are.* Leave open if no one lands it.

### Bridge to Phase 2

Every student leaves with a one-sentence **initial model**: "Two objects pull harder when ______."

---

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 28 min)*

### 10–14 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt on the board:

> *"Draw two spheres. Draw an arrow showing the pull each feels from the other. Then predict: if I make one sphere heavier, what happens to the arrows? If I move them farther apart, what happens?"*

This surfaces intuition before any formula.

### 14–28 min · Investigation — PhET Gravity Force Lab Basics

Pairs (or whole-class projection) work the simulation:

1. **Mass test** — keep distance fixed; double mass 1. What happens to the force? (It doubles.)
2. **Distance test** — keep masses fixed; double the separation. What happens to the force? (It drops to **one-quarter**, not one-half — the surprise.)
3. **Pattern hunt** — triple the distance. Predict first, then check. (Force → one-ninth.)

**Teacher facilitation language:**

> "Predict before you drag. Doubling the distance — half the force, or something else?"
> "Force fell to one-quarter, not one-half. What did we square — the masses or the distance?"
> "Are the two arrows on the two masses equal or unequal? Even when the masses are very different?"

### Teacher facilitation during Explore

- **What to look for** — students noticing the force changes with the *square* of distance, and that the two pulls are always equal-and-opposite (Newton's Third Law cameo).
- **What to resist** — don't write Fg = Gm₁m₂/r² yet; let the simulation reveal the ÷4 pattern first.
- **What to redirect** — students who say "double distance, half force" should re-run the ×3 distance trial and confront the ÷9 result.

---

## Phase 3 · Explain *(28 – 35 min)*

### 28–31 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "What pattern did distance follow? Say it as a rule."

Target consensus:

> "More mass means more pull. But the distance matters in a stronger way — squaring it. Double the distance and the force isn't halved, it's quartered."

### 31–35 min · Vocabulary + the equation (≤ 3 terms)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "The pull every mass feels from every other mass is the **gravitational force**. The rule that the force falls with the *square* of the distance is the **inverse-square law**. And the amount of matter that sets how strong the pull is — and never changes — is **mass**."

Write the Reference-Table equation and unpack it:

> **Fg = G·m₁·m₂ / r²**, where G = 6.67×10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².
> Near Earth's surface this same gravitational force is what we call **weight**: Fg = mg, with g ≈ 9.81 m/s².

### Discussion prompts to deploy here

- "Why is *weight* on the Moon smaller but *mass* the same?"
  - *Sample student response:* "Mass is how much matter you have — unchanged. Weight is the gravitational force, and the Moon's smaller mass pulls less, so weight drops to about 1/6."
- "Earth pulls the Moon; does the Moon pull Earth equally?"
  - *Sample student response:* "Yes — the two forces are equal and opposite, even though Earth is far more massive."

---

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(35 – 39 min)*

### 35–37 min · Hochman Because/But/So

Project the stem and build it together (see Strategy Spotlight):

> *"The gravitational force between two objects depends on their masses **because** ___, **but** it depends even more sharply on distance **because** ___, **so** ___."*

### 37–39 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "Now answer the Do Now and the Moon question in one sentence each, using *gravitational force* and *inverse-square law*."

Target: Astronauts in orbit *do* feel Earth's gravity — they are in continuous free fall, circling Earth, so they float; and the Moon stays in orbit because gravity pulls it inward while its sideways speed keeps it from falling straight down.

---

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(39 – 42 min)*

### 39–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

> *"Two satellites pull on each other with a gravitational force of 8 N.
> (a) Their masses stay the same, but the distance between them doubles. What is the new force? Show your reasoning.
> (b) Is the amount of matter (mass) in each satellite changed by moving them apart? Explain."*

Expected: (a) 8 N ÷ 4 = **2 N** (inverse-square: double r → divide force by 2² = 4); (b) no — mass is unchanged; only the gravitational *force* changed. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "What is one thing today that changed how you think about gravity or weight? Who helped you make sense of it?"

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "There is no gravity in space, so astronauts float." → **Correction:** Earth's gravity reaches far into space (it holds the Moon!). Orbiting astronauts float because they are in continuous *free fall* around Earth, not because gravity is absent.
- **Misconception:** "Mass and weight are the same thing." → **Correction:** Mass is the unchanging amount of matter (kg); weight is the gravitational force on that mass (N) and depends on local g. Same mass on the Moon, smaller weight.
- **Misconception:** "Double the distance halves the force." → **Correction:** It is an *inverse-square* law — double the distance divides the force by 2² = 4.
- **Misconception:** "Only the smaller object is pulled." → **Correction:** Both objects pull on each other with equal and opposite gravitational forces, regardless of size.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frame: *"When the distance doubles, the gravitational force becomes ___ because the distance is ___ in the formula."* Word-choice box: {squared, one-quarter, mass, weight, attract, distance}. Pair *inverse-square* with a gesture (hands spreading apart while the other hand shrinks).
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide the PhET data table pre-labeled with columns (mass, distance, force); students fill values only. Offer the worked example with the substitution already set up so students complete the arithmetic.
- **Extensions:** Have students compute the actual gravitational force between two 50 kg students standing 1 m apart using Fg = Gm₁m₂/r², and explain why we never feel it (≈ 1.7×10⁻⁷ N — far too small).

## Strategy Spotlight

**HOCHMAN (sentence-level literacy).** Today's signature move is a **Because/But/So expansion** built around an **appositive** on the inverse-square law. First model the appositive in Phase 3 by renaming the key term: *"The inverse-square law — the rule that squaring the distance shrinks the force — explains the Moon's steady orbit."* The appositive (set off by dashes) forces students to define the term in-line, a Hochman comprehension check. Then in Phase 4, students complete the Because/But/So stem so that a single complex sentence captures the whole conceptual contrast: mass matters, *but* distance matters more sharply, *so* the inverse-square pattern dominates at large separations. Collect 2–3 finished sentences and read them aloud so the class hears the precise causal language.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **gravitational force** — the attractive force every mass exerts on every other mass; near Earth's surface it is what we call weight (Fg = mg)
- **inverse-square law** — the pattern in which a force shrinks with the *square* of the distance, so doubling the distance divides the force by four
- **mass** — the unchanging amount of matter in an object (in kilograms), which sets how strongly it gravitates and is not the same as weight
