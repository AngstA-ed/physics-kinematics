01 Phenomenon

A pot of water is boiling hard on a stove turned all the way up to maximum. A thermometer in the water reads 100 °C. You leave it boiling for five more minutes — the burner is still on full power, steam is pouring off the top. Energy is clearly still flowing into the pot the whole time. And yet, for the entire five minutes, the thermometer does not move. It stays locked at 100 °C.

02 Driving question

Why does the temperature of boiling water stay at 100 °C no matter how much heat you add?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: The burner keeps adding energy, but the temperature is not rising. Where could that energy be going if it is not making the water hotter? Try to name a specific destination — not just “it disappears.”

06 Make it make sense

Use the heating curve of water to answer each item. Show your reasoning.

1. A student says, “On the flat parts of the graph, no energy is being added — that’s why the temperature isn’t changing.” Is the student correct? Explain what is really happening to the energy on a flat plateau.

2. Look at segment C (liquid water warming from 0 °C to 100 °C). Is the kinetic energy of the particles increasing, decreasing, or staying the same? How do you know from the graph?

3. Two students heat identical beakers of ice. Student 1 uses a low burner setting; Student 2 turns the burner to maximum. Both graph the boiling plateau (segment D). Predict: will the two plateaus be at the same temperature? Will they be the same length (time)? Explain.

07 Key vocabulary

kinetic energy
the energy of particle motion; the faster the particles move, the higher the temperature, so a rising temperature (a sloped segment on a heating curve) signals rising kinetic energy
potential energy
energy stored in the arrangement and separation of particles; during a phase change (a flat plateau on a heating curve) the energy flowing in raises potential energy without changing the temperature
phase change
a change from one state of matter to another (melting, freezing, boiling/vaporization, condensation) that occurs at a constant temperature; on a heating or cooling curve it appears as a flat plateau where potential energy changes while kinetic energy (temperature) holds steady

Use each term in a sentence about today’s heating-curve investigation. Do not copy the definition — describe something you actually observed on the graph or in the data.

09 Exit ticket

A different liquid substance is cooled at a constant rate. Its cooling curve shows the temperature dropping, then holding flat at 80 °C for a while, then dropping again.

  1. What is happening to the substance during the flat plateau at 80 °C, and what is this temperature called?
  1. Along the sloped part before the plateau (temperature dropping), is the particles’ kinetic energy increasing or decreasing? Explain in one sentence.
  1. During the flat plateau, energy is still flowing out of the substance, yet the temperature does not drop. In one sentence, explain why, using the words potential energy and phase change.

10 Explore further

  • Heating curve of water lab (ice → steam): Students start with crushed ice in a beaker, place a thermometer in it, and heat at a steady rate while recording temperature every 30 seconds. They graph temperature vs. time and discover two plateaus: one at 0 °C (melting) and one at 100 °C (boiling). The Javalab "States of Matter / Heating Curve" simulation (`javalab.org` → Physics/Chemistry → *Change of State*) is an excellent no-spill digital version: students drag the heat slider and watch the particle animation and the temperature graph build in real time. Either the wet-lab or the simulation produces the temperature-vs-time graph that anchors the whole lesson — compare class data to `figures/heating_curve_water.png`.
  • Graph-interpretation practice: Using `figures/heating_curve_water.png`, students label the five segments (A–E), identify which two are phase changes, and mark where kinetic energy is rising (sloped segments) versus where potential energy is rising (flat plateaus).
