01 Phenomenon

Two food labels are on the screen. One product “contains sodium.” The other “contains sodium chloride.” Sodium (Na) is a metal that bursts into flame in water. Sodium chloride (NaCl) is the salt on your fries. Then look at three formulas that look almost identical: CO (carbon monoxide — a deadly gas), CO₂ (carbon dioxide — the gas you exhale), and CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate — chalk and seashells). A tiny change in the formula or the name flips a substance from safe to dangerous.

02 Driving question

Why does chemistry need such a precise naming system — and what rules make a name tell you exactly which atoms are present and how many?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: NaCl is named “sodium chloride” with no number-prefix, but CO₂ is named “carbon dioxide” — with a “di-” telling you there are two oxygens. In one sentence: why might one type of compound need a prefix to count atoms while another type does not?

Helpful reminders (from your flow-chart):

  • Binary ionic: cation name + anion root + -ide (chlorine → chloride).
  • Ternary ionic: cation name + the polyatomic ion’s Table E name, kept whole (SO₄²⁻ = sulfate).
  • Covalent: Greek prefixes on both elements (mono = 1, di = 2, tri = 3, tetra = 4), dropping mono- on the first element.
  • Acid: -ide → hydro-…-ic acid; -ate → -ic acid; -ite → -ous acid.

07 Key vocabulary

polyatomic ion
a group of two or more atoms bonded together that carries one overall electric charge and travels as a single unit (e.g., sulfate SO₄²⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻); in a name the polyatomic ion keeps its Table E name unchanged, so Na₂SO₄ is "sodium sulfate," never "sodium sulfide"
covalent (molecular) compound
a compound of two nonmetals that share electrons; because the same two nonmetals can combine in several ratios, covalent names use Greek prefixes (mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-…) to count the atoms of each element, dropping *mono-* on the first element (CO = carbon monoxide; CO₂ = carbon dioxide)
acid
a hydrogen-containing compound dissolved in water (aq) named with its own rule set: a binary acid uses *hydro-…-ic acid* (HCl → hydrochloric acid); an oxyacid from an *-ate* ion uses *-ic acid* (sulfate → sulfuric acid) and from an *-ite* ion uses *-ous acid* (sulfite → sulfurous acid)

10 Explore further

  • Compound-Naming Card Game (the core ACTIVE LEARNING activity): Each group gets a deck of cards. One color holds formulas (NaCl, CaCl₂, Na₂SO₄, CO₂, N₂O₄, HCl, etc.); a second color holds names; a third color holds ion cards (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, etc.). Students play three rounds: (1) match each formula card to its name card; (2) build a formula by combining cation + anion ion cards so charges balance; (3) challenge round — a student draws a formula and the table races to name it. No external URL required. For a digital option, the PhET "Build a Molecule" or a Javalab ionic-compound builder can precede the physical cards.
  • Nomenclature Flow-Chart Practice: Students use `figures/naming_flowchart.png` as a decision tool. Given a formula, they walk the chart — *What kinds of elements? → which rule set? → apply it* — and record the name in a structured table. This is the bridge from the card game's intuition to the formal IUPAC rules.
