01 Phenomenon

A gardener plants two hydrangea bushes that are cuttings from the same parent plant — genetically identical. One goes in the front yard, one in the backyard. The next summer, the front bush blooms deep blue and the backyard bush blooms bright pink. Same plant, same sunlight, same water from the same hose. The gardener swears she did nothing differently. The hidden difference is the soil: a hydrangea flower acts like a living color-changer that responds to how acidic the dirt is.

02 Driving question

Why does a hydrangea plant produce blue flowers in some yards and pink in others?

07 Key vocabulary

pH
a measure of the hydrogen-ion concentration of a solution, on a scale from 0 (most acidic) to 14 (most basic), with 7 neutral; related to concentration by [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ, so each pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]
hydrogen-ion concentration ([H⁺])
the amount of hydrogen ions dissolved in a solution, in mol/L; the physical quantity that pH reports (a higher [H⁺] means a lower pH and a more acidic solution)
indicator
a substance that changes color over a specific pH range, used to estimate whether a solution is acidic, neutral, or basic without a meter; examples include litmus, phenolphthalein, bromothymol blue, and red cabbage juice

10 Explore further

  • Red cabbage indicator lab: Boil shredded red cabbage to extract a natural pH indicator (the pigment is anthocyanin). Students add the purple cabbage juice to a row of household substances — lemon juice, vinegar, water, baking-soda solution, dish soap, ammonia cleaner — and record the color produced (red/pink in acid, purple near neutral, green/blue in base). This makes the abstract pH number visible and ties directly to the hydrangea phenomenon (anthocyanins are the same family of pigment that colors hydrangea blooms). For a digital companion, a Javalab "pH and indicators" simulation lets students drag a probe across solutions and watch both the pH readout and indicator color change together; no external URL is required for the hands-on version.
  • pH of household substances: Using pH paper or a calibrated probe, students measure and rank common substances (lemon juice ≈ pH 2, coffee ≈ 5, milk ≈ 6.5, pure water ≈ 7, baking soda ≈ 8.3, bleach ≈ 12) and convert at least two readings to an approximate [H⁺] using [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
  • Acid rain investigation: Compare the pH of tap water, distilled water, and distilled water that has had exhaled breath (CO₂) bubbled through it. Students observe the pH drop as dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, and connect this to acid rain and its environmental impact (PS1-11 daily-life application).
  • Bar-/scale-reading: Students read `figures/ph_scale_indicators.png` to answer: which indicator would best distinguish a solution of pH 9 from a solution of pH 7? (Phenolphthalein — its color-change range straddles those two values.)
