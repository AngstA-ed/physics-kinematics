01 Phenomenon

A star is trillions of kilometers away, and no spacecraft will ever land on one to collect a sample. Yet astronomers say with confidence that the Sun is about three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium. In class, your teacher lights a tube of hydrogen gas. With your eyes it glows a single pinkish-purple color. But look at that same glow through a spectroscope and the one color shatters into four sharp colored lines on a black background — and nothing in between. Switch to helium and you get a completely different set of lines. Each gas has its own pattern, like a barcode. Starlight carries that same barcode across the galaxy.

02 Driving question

How can we identify what something is made of using only the light it gives off?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: The full rainbow (continuous spectrum) contains every color. The hydrogen panel shows only four lines on a black background — most of the rainbow is missing. In one sentence: what does it mean that the gas gives off only a few specific colors and leaves the rest out?

07 Key vocabulary

bright-line spectrum (emission spectrum)
the set of specific wavelengths of light an element gives off, appearing as discrete bright colored lines on a dark background; each element's pattern is unique, so it serves as a fingerprint for identifying the element
energy level
one of the fixed, specific energies an electron is allowed to have around the nucleus (the shells of the Bohr model); electrons can occupy these allowed levels but not the energies in between, which is why spectra show discrete lines rather than a continuous band
electron transition
the jump of an electron from one energy level to another; when an electron drops from a higher level to a lower one, the atom releases the energy difference as light of one specific color, producing a single bright line in the spectrum

10 Explore further

  • Spectroscope observation of gas-discharge tubes: Set up gas-discharge tubes (hydrogen, helium, neon, and one "mystery" tube the class will not name) with handheld or cardstock diffraction-grating spectroscopes. Students sketch the line pattern they see for each known tube, then sketch the mystery tube and compare it to their reference sketches to identify it. If physical tubes are unavailable, the PhET "Models of the Hydrogen Atom" simulation or a Javalab emission-spectrum module reproduces the same discrete-line observation on screen — no external URL required for the core observation; the gas tubes are the preferred hands-on version.
  • Element-identification challenge: Print or project a set of reference bright-line spectra (hydrogen, helium, neon, sodium, mercury) and one or two unknown spectra. Working in pairs, students line up the unknown against each reference and decide which element it matches, citing the specific lines that agree. The reference figure `figures/bright_line_spectra.png` provides hydrogen and helium; add neon and sodium reference cards from your supplier or the NIST Atomic Spectra Database for the full challenge.
