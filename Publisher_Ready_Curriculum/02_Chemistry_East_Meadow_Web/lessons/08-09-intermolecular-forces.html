01 Phenomenon

A water strider — a bug heavier than the air around it — stands on top of a pond without sinking, its thin legs just dimpling the surface. At the lab bench, the same thing happens with a steel paper clip: drop it in and it sinks, but lay it flat on the surface and it floats on the water’s “skin.” Then your teacher adds one drop of dish soap, and the floating clip drops straight to the bottom. Same clip, same water — completely different result.

02 Driving question

Why can a water strider (or a steel paper clip) sit on top of water without sinking — and why does one drop of soap make it sink?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: The paper clip is denser than water, so this is not buoyancy. In one sentence, what could be holding the clip up at the surface — and why would soap break it?

05 Investigate

You will work at a whiteboard in a random group of three, rotating through three stations. At each station, record what you observe and write one claim about the pull between the molecules (stronger or weaker).

Station A — Paper clip on water (surface tension)

Float a steel paper clip flat on the water. Observe the surface. Then add one drop of dish soap.

  • What happened after one drop of soap: _______________________________________________
  • Claim about the pull between water molecules: the soap made the pull ___________ (stronger / weaker)

06 Make it make sense

For each situation, decide whether the molecules pull on each other more strongly or more weakly, and explain what you would observe.

  1. Liquid X pours out of its bottle very slowly and stays in a thick blob; liquid Y splashes out and spreads thin.

    • Which liquid has the stronger pull between molecules? _______________
    • What property is this (surface tension or viscosity)? _______________
    • Explain in one sentence why the stronger pull makes liquid X pour slowly: _______________________________________________

  2. On one penny you can pile 28 drops of liquid before it spills; on another, only 9 drops of a different liquid before it spills.

    • Which liquid has the stronger pull between molecules? _______________ (the one that held ___ drops)
    • What property lets the drops dome up past the rim? _______________
    • Explain in one sentence: _______________________________________________

  3. Substance P boils at 65 °C. Substance Q boils at 210 °C. Both are made of small molecules.

    • Which substance has the stronger pull between its molecules? _______________
    • When P or Q boils, are you breaking the covalent bonds inside each molecule, or the forces between molecules? _______________
    • Explain in one sentence why stronger forces between molecules give a higher boiling point: _______________________________________________

07 Key vocabulary

intermolecular force
an attraction *between* one molecule and another (the prefix *inter-* means "between"); much weaker than the covalent bond *inside* a molecule, it is the force overcome when a liquid boils, and its strength sets a substance's bulk properties (boiling point, viscosity, surface tension)
surface tension
the inward pull at the surface of a liquid, caused by intermolecular forces drawing the surface molecules together into a film; strong surface tension lets water bead up, dome on a penny, and support a paper clip or a water strider, and soap lowers it
viscosity
a liquid's resistance to flowing, caused by how strongly its molecules attract one another; liquids with stronger intermolecular forces (honey) have high viscosity and pour slowly, while liquids with weaker forces (water) have low viscosity and pour quickly

10 Explore further

  • Paper clip on water (surface tension): Students float a steel paper clip flat on the surface of water in a beaker or cup (lowering it gently on a second bent clip "fork" works well). They observe the dimpled surface, then add a single drop of dish soap and watch the clip sink. Discussion: what held it up, and what did the soap destroy? Connect to the dipole panel of `figures/imf_dipoles_and_bp.png`.
  • Drops on a penny (surface tension, quantitative-lite): Students predict, then count, how many drops of water they can pile onto a penny before it overflows — the water domes far past the rim because the surface molecules pull inward. A second penny with a drop of soap (or a comparison liquid such as rubbing alcohol) holds far fewer drops. Record the counts; the higher count signals stronger surface tension.
  • Viscosity races (relating IMF to flow): Students tilt three identical trays (or race drops down a slanted plate) of water, cooking oil, and honey and time which reaches the finish line last. Honey is slowest — its molecules grip one another the most — water is fastest. Ranking flow speed ranks the strength of the molecule-to-molecule attraction.
  • Javalab "States of Matter / Intermolecular forces" simulation (optional digital extension): students slow the molecules and watch attractions pull neighbors together as a liquid forms; no external URL required for the hands-on core.
