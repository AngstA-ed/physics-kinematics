01 Phenomenon

Sodium is a soft metal that catches fire when it touches water — it has to be stored under oil. Chlorine is a poisonous green-yellow gas. But when one sodium atom bonds to one chlorine atom, you get sodium chloride — ordinary table salt — which is safe to eat and which your body needs every day. Same two ingredients. A dangerous metal and a poisonous gas combine into the harmless salt on your french fries.

02 Driving question

Why does table salt taste, look, and behave so differently from the sodium and chlorine that make it?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: If you stirred a pinch of pepper into a salt shaker, you’d have a mixture — you could still taste the salt and the pepper separately, and even pick the pepper out. But the salt itself, made of sodium and chlorine, tastes like neither metal nor gas. In one sentence: what is different about how the salt was made versus how the salt-and-pepper was made?

07 Key vocabulary

element
a pure substance made of only one kind of atom; it cannot be broken down into a simpler substance by a chemical reaction (e.g., copper Cu, oxygen O₂ — bonded pairs of the *same* atom are still an element)
compound
a pure substance made of two or more *different* elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio; its properties are new and not a blend of its elements' properties (e.g., water H₂O, table salt NaCl)
mixture
two or more substances physically combined but not chemically bonded, in no fixed ratio; each part keeps its own properties and can be separated by physical means (e.g., salt water, trail mix, air)

10 Explore further

  • Element / Compound / Mixture diagram-drawing station: Students draw particle-level diagrams for a set of substances — copper wire (element), water (compound), salt water (mixture), oxygen gas (element), carbon dioxide (compound), trail mix / sand-and-iron (heterogeneous mixture). Use the panel in `figures/particle_models_ecm.png` as the model for the three diagram "grammars" (identical circles = element; bonded two-color pairs = compound; two unbonded circle types = mixture). For a digital interactive option, the PhET *Build a Molecule* or a Javalab "states and particles" sandbox lets students assemble bonded vs. unbonded arrangements on screen before drawing their own.
  • Comparing properties of components vs. compound: A teacher-led comparison (no hazardous demo required) using the iron + sulfur classic on paper or video: iron filings are magnetic and gray; sulfur is yellow and non-magnetic; *mixed*, a magnet still pulls the iron out (properties retained → mixture); *heated until they react*, the product iron(II) sulfide is a brittle dark solid that a magnet no longer attracts (new properties → compound). Students record "before bonding" vs. "after bonding" properties in a two-column table. This makes the structure→function link empirical: bonding changes properties; mixing does not.
