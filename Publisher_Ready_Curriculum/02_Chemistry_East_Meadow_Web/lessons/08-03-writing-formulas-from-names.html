01 Phenomenon

Your teacher holds up two index cards. One says aluminum oxide. The other says Al₂O₃. They are the same substance — the grit on sandpaper and the protective coating on an aluminum window frame. But nowhere in the name “aluminum oxide” does it say two aluminums or three oxygens. Yet every chemist on Earth writes the exact same formula: Al₂O₃ — never AlO, never Al₃O₂. Something hidden forces the ratio.

02 Driving question

How does a chemical formula tell us the exact ratio of atoms?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: Table salt is NaCl — one sodium, one chlorine, a 1-to-1 ratio. But aluminum oxide is 2-to-3. In one sentence, what might be different about the ions in these two compounds that changes the ratio?

04 Initial model

Before your teacher names any procedure: a sodium ion is Na⁺ and a chloride ion is Cl⁻. An aluminum ion is Al³⁺ and an oxide ion is O²⁻.

  1. For NaCl, how many of each ion do you need so the positive and negative charges exactly cancel? Na: ____ Cl: ____

  2. For aluminum oxide, make a guess: how many Al³⁺ and how many O²⁻ do you need so the total positive charge cancels the total negative charge? Al: ____ O: ____

  3. Explain your reasoning for part (b) — what did you have to make equal?

05 Investigate

Part 1 — Build the easy formulas (on your group’s board)

For each ion pair, write a formula where the total positive charge exactly cancels the total negative charge, using the fewest atoms possible. Check by writing the charge total for each side.

Ion pair Total + charge Total − charge Formula
Na⁺ and Cl⁻
Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻
Ca²⁺ and O²⁻

For Ca²⁺ and O²⁻: you could have written Ca₂O₂. Is that the fewest atoms? What smaller formula also makes the charges cancel? _______________

Part 2 — Cross the charges + polyatomic ions

Now the harder pairs. Use the crossing-charges idea: the charge number on one ion becomes the subscript on the other. Then check the charge totals. For polyatomic ions (the multi-atom ions), keep the ion together and use parentheses if its subscript is greater than 1.

Ion pair Total + charge Total − charge Formula
Al³⁺ and O²⁻
Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻
Ca²⁺ and NO₃⁻
Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻

07 Key vocabulary

crossing charges
the procedure for writing an ionic formula in which each ion's charge number becomes the subscript of the *other* ion; this makes the total positive charge equal the total negative charge so the compound is neutral (e.g., Al³⁺ + O²⁻ → Al₂O₃)
reducing subscripts
dividing both subscripts in a formula by their greatest common factor to express the compound as the lowest whole-number ratio of ions (e.g., Ca₂O₂ reduces to CaO)
polyatomic ion
a group of atoms bonded together that carries a single overall electric charge and acts as one unit in a formula (e.g., nitrate NO₃⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻); when its subscript is greater than 1 it is enclosed in parentheses, as in Ca(NO₃)₂

10 Explore further

  • Formula-writing dominoes: Print a deck of cation cards (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺, NH₄⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe³⁺) and anion cards (Cl⁻, O²⁻, S²⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻). Each "domino" is a cation matched to an anion; the group writes the neutral formula on a vertical surface and only "plays" the next domino once the formula is verified by another group. This is the core BTC task — see Phase 2.
  • Polyatomic ion practice: Students keep the 2025 NYS Reference Tables open to the polyatomic-ion table (Table E). They build formulas that require parentheses — Ca(NO₃)₂, Al₂(SO₄)₃, (NH₄)₃PO₄ — and articulate *why* the parentheses are needed (the subscript multiplies the whole ion, not one atom).
