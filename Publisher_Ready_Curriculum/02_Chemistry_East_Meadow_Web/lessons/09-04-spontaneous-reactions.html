01 Phenomenon

Two beakers sit side by side. In the first, a grey strip of zinc metal is dropped into clear blue copper(II) sulfate solution. After a few minutes the strip is coated with a reddish-brown solid, and the blue color of the solution fades. In the second beaker, a bright strip of copper metal is dropped into colorless zinc sulfate solution — and nothing happens. Same kind of setup (a metal placed in a solution of another metal’s salt), but only one beaker reacts.

02 Driving question

Why does a piece of zinc react with copper sulfate solution, but a piece of copper does nothing in zinc sulfate solution?

03 Notice & wonder

I notice… I wonder…

Turn & Talk #1: The zinc replaced the copper, but the copper could not replace the zinc. If you had a third metal — say magnesium — how would you figure out whether it could replace copper, without just guessing? In one sentence, what would you need to know?

07 Key vocabulary

activity series
a ranking of metals from most active (most easily loses valence electrons) to least active (NYS Reference Table J); a metal higher on the list replaces any metal lower on the list from its compound, which lets you predict whether a single-replacement reaction will occur
active metal
a metal that loses its valence electrons easily to become a positive ion; more active metals sit toward the lower-left of the periodic table (low electronegativity, low ionization energy) and higher on the activity series; activity, not density or appearance, determines reaction outcome
single replacement
a reaction in which one element replaces one element in a compound (element + compound → new element + new compound), e.g., Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu; it occurs only when the free metal is more active than the metal in the compound

10 Explore further

  • Activity-series single-replacement micro-lab: In a well plate, students place small pieces of three metals (zinc, copper, and either iron or magnesium) into drops of three solutions (copper(II) sulfate, zinc sulfate, and silver nitrate or iron(II) sulfate). Each metal–solution combination is one well. Students record reaction (coating forms, color fades, bubbles) vs. no reaction, then build their own ranking. Micro-scale keeps reagent volumes tiny and waste minimal. The class-built ranking is then checked against `figures/activity_series_ladder.png` (NYS Reference Table J). No external URL required; a Javalab "single replacement" or "activity series" virtual lab can substitute or precede the hands-on version where reagents are limited.
  • Reference-table reading: Students locate Reference Table J (Activity Series) in the 2025 NYS Chemistry Reference Tables and confirm that the order they discovered experimentally matches the published table. Connect each "reaction / no reaction" result to relative position on `figures/activity_series_ladder.png`.
