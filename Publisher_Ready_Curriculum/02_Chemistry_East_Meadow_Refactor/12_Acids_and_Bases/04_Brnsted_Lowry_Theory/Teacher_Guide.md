## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

This lesson extends the Acids & Bases unit and is anchored to **PS1-11** — *reactions are limited to Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry acid-base reactions.* In Lesson 01 students learned the **Arrhenius** model: an acid produces H⁺ in water; a base produces OH⁻ in water. That model is powerful but incomplete. It cannot explain how ammonia, NH₃ — a molecule with **no OH⁻ anywhere in its formula** — produces a basic solution that turns red litmus blue. The Brønsted-Lowry model closes that gap by redefining acids and bases in terms of what they *do* with a proton (H⁺) rather than what ion they contain: a **Brønsted-Lowry acid is a proton donor**, and a **Brønsted-Lowry base is a proton acceptor**.

For the 2025 NYSSLS-aligned Regents, students are expected to identify the proton donor and proton acceptor in a given equation, and to identify **conjugate acid-base pairs** — two species that differ by exactly one H⁺. This is a direct PS1-11 expectation and recurs throughout the rest of the unit (neutralization, pH, and titration are all Brønsted-Lowry proton-transfer events).

The Cross-Cutting Concept of **Systems and System Models** is the explicit lens: the Arrhenius model and the Brønsted-Lowry model are two *models* of the same acid-base system. A more complete model (Brønsted-Lowry) explains every case the older model (Arrhenius) explained, plus new cases (ammonia as a base) the older model could not. Students see that scientific models are tools that get revised when new evidence — a base with no OH⁻ — does not fit.

### Phenomenon

Open a fresh bottle of household ammonia cleaner (or describe it vividly). It is a base: it feels slippery, it turns red litmus blue, and on a pH probe it reads around 11. Now write its formula on the board — **NH₃** — and circle it. There is no OH⁻ in that formula. By the Arrhenius rule students learned in Lesson 01 (*a base produces OH⁻ in water*), NH₃ should not be a base at all. Yet the litmus paper is unmistakably blue. Set a strip of red litmus into the ammonia solution and let students watch it turn. The model says one thing; the evidence says another.

**Driving question:** How can ammonia (NH₃) be a base when it doesn't contain OH⁻?

### Javalab / Labs

- **Conjugate-pair identification practice (ACTIVE LEARNING anchor):** Working in pairs, students receive a set of acid-base equations (printed cards or the worksheet table) and, *before any vocabulary is given*, sort each equation by tracking the H⁺: which species *lost* an H⁺ going left-to-right (the donor), and which species *gained* one (the acceptor)? Students physically annotate each equation, then group the species into "differs by one H" pairs. This proton-tracking activity is the Explore-before-Explain investigation; the formal terms (donor, acceptor, conjugate pair) are named only afterward in Phase 3. Connect every example back to the `figures/proton_transfer_nh3.png` particle diagram.
- **Arrhenius vs. Brønsted-Lowry model comparison:** Using a short list of species (HCl, NaOH, NH₃, CH₃COOH, HCO₃⁻), students fill a two-column table — "Can Arrhenius explain it?" and "Can Brønsted-Lowry explain it?" — and identify the one case (NH₃) where only Brønsted-Lowry succeeds. This is the model-revision evidence for the Systems & System Models CCC.
- For a digital option, a Javalab "acid–base" proton-transfer animation or a PhET acid-base solutions module can preview the NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ reaction before the hands-on sort; no external URL is required for the core lesson.

### Assessments

- **Brønsted-Lowry conjugate pair quiz** (district checkpoint, following lesson; see `Assessments/` folder once created): students label the proton donor and acceptor in an equation and identify both conjugate acid-base pairs.
- The **Exit Ticket** below is the formative check: students analyze the proton transfer **HF + H₂O → F⁻ + H₃O⁺** (a reaction they did **not** sort in the investigation), label the donor and acceptor, and identify both conjugate pairs. The exit-ticket reaction is deliberately distinct from the worksheet practice set. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Opening Circle + Do Now

Begin with a brief **community-building opening circle** (2–3 min). Everyone — teacher included — answers: *"Think of a time you handed something off to someone, or received something handed to you. What got passed?"* One round, one sentence each, no judgment. This primes the central image of the lesson: an acid-base reaction is a *hand-off* — one particle passes a proton, another receives it.

Then post the **Do Now**:

> *"In Lesson 01 you learned the Arrhenius rule: a base produces OH⁻ in water. Here is the formula for ammonia: NH₃. Yet a bottle of ammonia cleaner turns red litmus blue — it is clearly a base. Write one sentence: how can NH₃ be a base when there is no OH⁻ anywhere in its formula?"*

Give students 2 minutes to write silently, then take two or three responses.

**Anticipated student responses to Do Now:**

- "Maybe NH₃ isn't really a base." — affirm the careful thinking, then show the litmus turning blue: the evidence says it *is* a base. So the rule must be incomplete, not the substance.
- "Maybe the N is like the O in OH and grabs something." — very close to the proton-acceptor idea; validate and bank it for Phase 3.
- "Maybe it reacts with water and makes OH⁻ somehow." — excellent prediction; that is exactly what happens. We will track where that OH⁻ comes from.

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — a base with no OH⁻

**Teacher actions.** Display the ammonia cleaner (behind glass or in a fume hood). Read its pH on a probe or pH paper (~11) and dip red litmus in — it turns blue. Then write **NH₃** large on the board and circle it.

> "Every base you have met so far — NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ — wears its OH⁻ right in the formula. That is the Arrhenius rule. But look at this. Ammonia is a base. The litmus proves it. And there is no OH⁻ in NH₃. Either the rule is wrong, or the rule is incomplete. Which do you think it is?"

Project the `figures/proton_transfer_nh3.png` figure:

![Particle diagram of the reaction NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. On the left, an ammonia molecule (nitrogen in purple with a labeled lone pair and three hydrogens in blue) is labeled 'proton ACCEPTOR (base)', and a water molecule (oxygen in green with two hydrogens, one highlighted orange as the transferring proton) is labeled 'proton DONOR (acid)'. A curved orange arrow shows an H⁺ transferring from water to the nitrogen's lone pair. On the right, the products are an ammonium ion NH₄⁺ (nitrogen with four hydrogens, the gained one highlighted orange, charge +) labeled 'conjugate ACID', and a hydroxide ion OH⁻ (charge −) labeled 'conjugate BASE'. A dashed bracket across the top notes that conjugate acid–base pairs differ by one H⁺.](figures/proton_transfer_nh3.png)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Here is the secret. When ammonia dissolves, it does not bring its own OH⁻. Instead, the nitrogen — which has a lone pair of electrons, those two dots — reaches out and **takes an H⁺ off a water molecule**. Follow the orange proton. Water hands an H⁺ to the ammonia. Ammonia becomes NH₄⁺. And the water, now missing one H⁺, is left as OH⁻. *That* is where the OH⁻ comes from — not from the ammonia at all, but from the water it stole a proton from. Ammonia is a base because it *accepts* a proton, not because it contains hydroxide."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "So the OH⁻ comes from the water, not the ammonia?" — exactly. The base does not have to *contain* OH⁻; it has to *cause* OH⁻ by pulling a proton off water.
- "Why does the nitrogen grab the H and not the oxygen?" — the lone pair on nitrogen is what attracts the positive H⁺. We will not go deeper than "lone pair attracts the proton" — that is enough for this standard.
- "Is the water an acid here?" — yes — and that surprises students. Water donated a proton, so in *this* reaction water is the acid. Bank it; this is the model-revision payoff in Phase 3.

**Driving question** (post on the board and leave it there):

> *How can ammonia (NH₃) be a base when it doesn't contain OH⁻?*

### 8–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two columns on the board: **I notice… / I wonder…**

Collect 3–4 responses about the figure or the litmus demonstration. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: in this reaction, one particle *gave away* an H⁺ and one particle *took* an H⁺. Looking at the figure, which one gave and which one took? Point to the orange proton and trace where it goes."

Target insight (leave open if no one lands it yet): water **gave** the H⁺ (it lost one and became OH⁻); ammonia **took** the H⁺ (it gained one and became NH₄⁺). The whole reaction is a single proton hand-off. We will name "giver" and "taker" formally in Phase 3.

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 30 min)*

### 10–15 min · ABCs Activity — track the proton before any definition

**Before any formal vocabulary is introduced**, students build the donor/acceptor pattern by physically tracking H⁺ through a set of equations. No terms yet — just "who lost an H, who gained an H."

Hand out (or project) the equation set. The task:

**Part 1 — Mark the proton:**

> "For each equation, circle the species on the left that has *more* hydrogens than its partner on the right, and draw an arrow showing the H⁺ moving to whichever species *gained* one. Do not use any vocabulary yet — just follow the H."

Equation set (also on the worksheet):

| # | Equation | Lost an H⁺ (left → right) | Gained an H⁺ (left → right) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺ | HCl → Cl⁻ | H₂O → H₃O⁺ |
| 2 | NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ | H₂O → OH⁻ | NH₃ → NH₄⁺ |
| 3 | CH₃COOH + H₂O → CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ | CH₃COOH → CH₃COO⁻ | H₂O → H₃O⁺ |

**Teacher facilitation language (circulate):**

> "Count the H atoms on each species before and after. HCl has one H on the left; Cl⁻ has zero on the right. So HCl *lost* its H. Where did that H go? To water, which gained one and became H₃O⁺."

> "Look at equation 2 — our ammonia reaction. This time water *lost* an H and became OH⁻, and ammonia *gained* one. Compare that to equation 1, where water *gained* an H. Water did opposite things in the two reactions. What does that tell you about water?"

**Anticipated student responses during ABCs:**

- "In equation 1 water gained an H, but in equation 2 water lost an H — that's weird." — excellent observation; bank it. Water can both donate and accept a proton depending on its partner. We will name that ("amphoteric" is a bonus term, not required) but the key point is that the *same model* handles both.
- "HCl always loses its H." — yes; HCl is a strong proton donor. Affirm and connect: that matches its Arrhenius behavior (HCl makes H⁺/H₃O⁺ in water).
- "How do I know which one loses the H?" — count hydrogens before and after on each species. The one with fewer H's on the right gave it away.

### 15–22 min · Initial Model — the ammonia reaction in your own words

**Prompt on the board:**

> *"Before we name anything: in equation 2 (NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻), write two sentences. (1) Which particle gave away an H⁺, and what did it become? (2) Which particle took the H⁺, and what did it become? Then answer: where did the OH⁻ in solution actually come from?"*

Students write individually for 3 minutes, then compare with a partner.

Target answer:

> (1) Water gave away an H⁺ and became OH⁻. (2) Ammonia took the H⁺ and became NH₄⁺. The OH⁻ came from the *water*, because water lost a proton — not from the ammonia, which has no OH⁻.

**Teacher facilitation language:**

> "Look at the figure again. Did the OH⁻ come out of the ammonia? Trace it. The OH⁻ is the *leftover* of the water after it gave up its proton. The ammonia never contained OH⁻ at all — it just made the water produce it by taking a proton away."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "So ammonia is a base because it *takes* a proton." — exactly. Hold that sentence; it is the definition we will name in Phase 3.
- "Then water is acting like the acid?" — yes, in this reaction water donated the proton, so water is the acid here. That is the surprising part — and it is allowed, because acid/base is now about *role*, not *formula*.
- "Does the OH⁻ count even though it's the leftover?" — yes; the OH⁻ is real and in solution, which is exactly why the litmus turns blue. The Arrhenius rule was not wrong about OH⁻ causing basicity; it just could not explain where the OH⁻ came from.

### 22–30 min · Investigation — group conjugate-pair sort

Groups of three or four work through a structured sort. The goal: identify, in each equation, the **two pairs of species that differ by exactly one H⁺**. Students do not yet have the word "conjugate" — the worksheet calls them "partner pairs."

**Sort task (written on the board or on the worksheet):**

For each equation, find the two "differ-by-one-H" partner pairs and write them in the table.

**Equation 1: HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺**

| Partner pair | Has more H⁺ | Has fewer H⁺ |
|---|---|---|
| Pair A | HCl | Cl⁻ |
| Pair B | H₃O⁺ | H₂O |

**Equation 2: NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻**

| Partner pair | Has more H⁺ | Has fewer H⁺ |
|---|---|---|
| Pair A | NH₄⁺ | NH₃ |
| Pair B | H₂O | OH⁻ |

**Equation 3: CH₃COOH + H₂O → CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺**

| Partner pair | Has more H⁺ | Has fewer H⁺ |
|---|---|---|
| Pair A | CH₃COOH | CH₃COO⁻ |
| Pair B | H₃O⁺ | H₂O |

**Teacher facilitation prompts (circulate):**

> "A partner pair is two formulas that look identical except one has exactly one more H⁺ — and exactly one more positive charge to go with it. NH₄⁺ and NH₃: same nitrogen, four H versus three H, charge +1 versus 0. That is a partner pair."

> "Be careful with HCl and Cl⁻: HCl has one H and no charge; Cl⁻ has zero H and a −1 charge. Removing an H⁺ takes away one positive charge, so what is neutral becomes negative. Check the charge bookkeeping every time."

> "Watch H₂O — it shows up in a pair in every equation, but on different sides. In equation 1 water is in the 'fewer H' slot (it becomes H₃O⁺). In equation 2 water is in the 'more H' slot (it becomes OH⁻). Same molecule, opposite role."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "Isn't HCl paired with H₃O⁺?" — no; pair by *what differs by one H on the same backbone*. HCl ↔ Cl⁻ (chlorine backbone); H₂O ↔ H₃O⁺ (oxygen/water backbone). Cross-pairing the donor with the other product's acceptor is the most common error.
- "NH₄⁺ has a + charge but NH₃ doesn't — did I pair them wrong?" — you paired them right; the charge difference of +1 is *exactly* what one extra H⁺ adds. The charge change confirms the pair.
- "Acetic acid is huge — CH₃COOH." — only the one acidic H matters; CH₃COOH ↔ CH₃COO⁻ differ by that single H⁺. The carbon skeleton is the unchanged backbone.

### 28–30 min · Reconnect + surface the method

Bring groups back together. Ask one group to put equation 2's two partner pairs on the board. Ask the class:

> "In every equation, the giver and the taker each had a *partner* — the giver's leftover after it donated, and the taker's new form after it accepted. What is always true about a giver and its leftover partner? (They differ by exactly one H⁺ and one unit of positive charge.)"

Surface the key idea: every proton transfer creates *two* pairs, each differing by one H⁺. The proton-giver and its leftover are one pair; the proton-taker and its new form are the other pair. We are ready to name these.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(30 – 36 min)*

### 30–33 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "Look at your completed sort. Turn to your partner: in Lesson 01 the Arrhenius rule said a base must *contain* OH⁻. But in equation 2, ammonia is a base and contains no OH⁻. What did ammonia actually *do* that made it behave like a base?"

Target consensus: ammonia behaved like a base because it **accepted a proton (H⁺)** from water. The definition of a base no longer depends on what it contains, but on what it does with a proton.

> "And in equation 1, what did HCl do that made it an acid? Did it produce H⁺ the way Arrhenius said?"

Target: HCl *donated* a proton to water (making H₃O⁺). The Arrhenius and Brønsted-Lowry models *agree* about HCl — both call it an acid. The two models only diverge for cases like ammonia, where there is no OH⁻ to point to. The newer model explains everything the old one did, plus more.

### 33–36 min · Vocabulary introduction (exactly 3 terms)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Let's name what we've been doing all period. The particle that *gives away* a proton — HCl in equation 1, water in equation 2 — is the **Brønsted-Lowry acid**: a **proton donor**. The particle that *takes* the proton — water in equation 1, ammonia in equation 2 — is the **Brønsted-Lowry base**: a **proton acceptor**. That is the entire model. An acid donates H⁺; a base accepts H⁺. Notice the word *donor* and *acceptor* — this is about a transaction, a hand-off, exactly like our opening circle."

> "Now the third term. When an acid donates its proton, what's left behind is a base — it could accept the proton right back. When a base accepts a proton, what it becomes is an acid — it now has a proton it could give back. Those before-and-after partners are a **conjugate acid-base pair**: two species that differ by exactly one H⁺. NH₃ and NH₄⁺ are a conjugate pair. H₂O and OH⁻ are a conjugate pair. The base member always has *one fewer* H⁺ than its conjugate acid."

Post the three terms on the board. Students fill them in on their notes.

**Discussion prompts to deploy here:**

- "In NH₃ + H₂O → NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, name the conjugate acid of NH₃." — *Expected response:* NH₄⁺. It is NH₃ plus one H⁺. NH₃ accepted a proton (it is the base), and NH₄⁺ is its conjugate acid.
- "Why is water the acid in the ammonia reaction but the base in the HCl reaction?" — *Expected response:* because acid/base is a *role*, not a fixed property. Whether water donates or accepts a proton depends on its reaction partner. This is exactly why the Brønsted-Lowry model is more complete: it handles a substance that switches roles, which the Arrhenius "contains OH⁻ / contains H⁺" rule cannot.

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(36 – 40 min)*

### 36–39 min · Revise the model + Because/But/So expansion

Students return to their Initial Model (the two sentences about the ammonia reaction). They revise it by adding the vocabulary: label the proton donor (acid), the proton acceptor (base), and bracket each of the two conjugate acid-base pairs.

Then run a **Because / But / So** sentence about the phenomenon. Starter on the board:

> *"Ammonia (NH₃) acts as a base in water even though it contains no OH⁻."*

Model one aloud:

> "Ammonia acts as a base **because** it accepts a proton (H⁺) from water, which is exactly what a Brønsted-Lowry base does — **but** ammonia has no OH⁻ in its own formula, so the Arrhenius model could not explain its basic behavior — **so** the OH⁻ that turns the litmus blue actually comes from the *water* that lost its proton, and the Brønsted-Lowry model explains the whole reaction by tracking the proton transfer."

Then have students write their own B/B/S using one of these starters:

- *"In the reaction HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺, water acts as a base…"* (hint: water *accepts* the proton here)
- *"NH₄⁺ and NH₃ are a conjugate acid-base pair…"* (hint: they differ by exactly one H⁺)

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "Because water accepted the proton from HCl…" — good start; push for the But/So: "but in the ammonia reaction water donated a proton instead, so water's role depends on its partner — it is the base with HCl and the acid with NH₃."
- "Because NH₄⁺ has one more H than NH₃…" — excellent; push for the So: "so removing one H⁺ from the conjugate acid NH₄⁺ gives back the conjugate base NH₃, and the +1 charge disappears with the proton."

### 39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "Return to our driving question: how can ammonia be a base with no OH⁻? You now have the answer. In one sentence, using the words *proton acceptor*, explain where the OH⁻ in an ammonia solution comes from."

Target: "Ammonia is a base because it is a proton acceptor — it pulls an H⁺ off a water molecule, and the water left behind becomes OH⁻, which is what makes the solution basic."

> "The Arrhenius model wasn't wrong about OH⁻ making things basic. It just couldn't tell you where the OH⁻ came from when the base had none of its own. Brønsted-Lowry follows the proton, and the proton tells the whole story."

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "A base has to contain OH⁻." → **Correction:** That is the Arrhenius rule, and it is incomplete. A Brønsted-Lowry base is a *proton acceptor*; it need not contain OH⁻ at all. Ammonia (NH₃) is a base because it accepts an H⁺ from water — the resulting OH⁻ comes from the water, not from the ammonia.
- **Misconception:** "Brønsted-Lowry replaces and contradicts Arrhenius." → **Correction:** The two models *agree* on every Arrhenius acid and base (HCl is an acid in both; NaOH is a base in both). Brønsted-Lowry is a *broader* model that explains additional cases (like NH₃) the Arrhenius model cannot. A more complete model extends the older one; it does not erase it.
- **Misconception:** "A substance is permanently an acid or permanently a base." → **Correction:** Acid and base are *roles* in a particular reaction, not fixed labels. Water acts as a base (proton acceptor) when it reacts with HCl, but as an acid (proton donor) when it reacts with NH₃. The same species can play either role depending on its partner.
- **Misconception:** "Conjugate pairs are any acid and base in the equation." → **Correction:** A conjugate acid-base pair is two species that differ by *exactly one H⁺* on the same backbone. In HCl + H₂O → Cl⁻ + H₃O⁺, the pairs are HCl/Cl⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O — *not* HCl/H₃O⁺. Pairing the donor with the other product's acceptor is the most common error; always pair by the one-H difference.
- **Misconception:** "The conjugate acid and conjugate base have the same charge." → **Correction:** Adding an H⁺ adds one unit of positive charge. The conjugate acid always has a charge one unit more positive than its conjugate base: NH₄⁺ (charge +1) vs. NH₃ (charge 0); H₂O (charge 0) vs. OH⁻ (charge −1). Tracking the charge change is a reliable check on a correct pairing.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Proton-transfer sentence frame: *"___ donated an H⁺, so it is the acid; ___ accepted an H⁺, so it is the base."* and *"___ and ___ are a conjugate pair because they differ by one ___."* Word-choice box displayed on the board throughout: {proton, donor, acceptor, conjugate pair, H⁺}. Pair each term with a gesture: open hand pushing out for *donor*, cupped hands pulling in for *acceptor*. Color-code H⁺ in the same orange used in `figures/proton_transfer_nh3.png` so students can match the figure to the equations.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Pre-print the equations with the hydrogens in each species already counted (e.g., "HCl = 1 H, Cl⁻ = 0 H") so the proton-tracking lookup is removed as a barrier and the student focuses on the donor/acceptor decision. Provide a partially completed sort table where one of the two pairs is already filled in for each equation. Offer a two-color highlighter routine: highlight the proton-giver in one color and the proton-taker in another before naming anything. Calculator not required; charge bookkeeping can be supported with a +1/0/−1 number line card.
- **Extensions:** (1) Water acts as an acid in one reaction and a base in another — research the term for a substance that can do both (*amphoteric*) and find one other amphoteric species (e.g., HCO₃⁻). (2) Write the conjugate base of H₂SO₄ and then the conjugate base of *that* (a two-step proton loss: H₂SO₄ → HSO₄⁻ → SO₄²⁻). (3) Explain in three sentences why the Brønsted-Lowry model is called a *more complete* model than Arrhenius, using the ammonia case as your evidence — connect to the Systems & System Models CCC.

## Strategy Spotlight

**ACTIVE LEARNING — proton-tracking sort before the vocabulary.** Active learning means students *construct* the concept through doing, then attach the name afterward — the opposite of being told a definition and then practicing it. In this lesson the entire donor/acceptor/conjugate-pair framework is *discovered* by students physically tracking H⁺ through three equations in Phase 2, before a single vocabulary term is spoken. By the time the teacher names "proton donor" and "proton acceptor" in Phase 3, students have already sorted every species into givers and takers with their own hands. The word lands on a pattern they already own.

**How to run it in this lesson (Phase 2 → Phase 3):**

1. Give students the three equations with **no vocabulary** and the single instruction: follow the H⁺. Circle the giver, arrow to the taker (Phase 2, 10–15 min).
2. Have them sort the species into "differ-by-one-H partner pairs" — still no term for it (Phase 2, 22–30 min). The cognitive work of pairing by backbone and checking charge happens *before* the word "conjugate" exists for them.
3. Only in Phase 3 (33–36 min) attach the three names — proton donor, proton acceptor, conjugate acid-base pair — to the categories students have already built. Cold-call two or three students to point at their own sort and say which is the donor and which pair is conjugate.

**Why active learning fits Brønsted-Lowry:** The model is fundamentally a *verb* ("donates," "accepts") rather than a *noun* ("contains OH⁻"). Students who are handed the definition tend to revert to the Arrhenius habit of scanning the formula for OH⁻. Students who first *track the proton with their pencil* internalize the transfer as an action, which is precisely the conceptual shift the standard (PS1-11) requires. The doing prevents the misconception.

**CRSE connection:** The opening circle prompt (a time you handed something off, or received something) draws on every student's everyday experience of giving and receiving — a universally shared human action across cultures and home languages. Grounding an abstract particle-level idea (proton transfer) in the familiar social act of a hand-off honors students' lived knowledge and gives ENL students a concrete, language-light anchor for the donor/acceptor relationship before the technical vocabulary arrives.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **Brønsted-Lowry acid (proton donor)** — a substance that donates (gives away) a hydrogen ion, H⁺, in a reaction; e.g., HCl donates a proton to water, and in the ammonia reaction water itself is the proton donor
- **Brønsted-Lowry base (proton acceptor)** — a substance that accepts (takes) a hydrogen ion, H⁺, in a reaction; e.g., NH₃ accepts a proton from water and becomes NH₄⁺, which is why ammonia is basic even though it contains no OH⁻
- **conjugate acid-base pair** — two species that differ by exactly one H⁺ (and one unit of charge); the conjugate acid has one more H⁺ than the conjugate base. Examples: NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻
