# Arrhenius Theory of Acids & Bases — Answer Key

## Cover

Unit: Acids and Bases — Lesson 01: Arrhenius Theory of Acids & Bases
East Meadow Schools × Valley Stream Central High School District
NYSSLS tag: HS-PS1-2 (pattern of chemical properties explained by a common ion)

## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. HNO₃ (never tested):**

- **Acid or base:** Acid.
- **How you know from the formula:** The formula starts with a leading H (HNO₃). Substances whose formula begins with H release H⁺ in water and are Arrhenius acids.
- **Ion produced in water:** H⁺ (the hydrogen ion). HNO₃ → H⁺ + NO₃⁻.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "acid"; 1 point for citing the leading H **and** naming H⁺ as the ion. Naming "acid" without the formula reasoning earns partial credit. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2.

**2. KOH (never tested):**

- **Acid or base:** Base.
- **How you know from the formula:** The formula contains an OH group bonded to a metal (potassium). Substances with a metal–OH formula release OH⁻ in water and are Arrhenius bases.
- **Ion produced in water:** OH⁻ (the hydroxide ion). KOH → K⁺ + OH⁻.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "base"; 1 point for citing the OH group **and** naming OH⁻ as the ion. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2.

**3. Lemon juice and dilute HCl gave the same results:**

- **Single ion both produce:** H⁺ (the hydrogen ion). The acid in lemon juice (citric acid) and HCl both release H⁺ in water.
- **Why this explains the shared properties:** Lemon juice and HCl are made of completely different other atoms, but the one ion they both release into water is H⁺. Because H⁺ is the part they have in common, and the litmus and metal behavior is also the same, the shared properties must be caused by the shared ion (H⁺) — not by the parts of each substance that are different. This is the Patterns CCC: a shared pattern of properties points to a shared underlying cause.

*Rubric:* 1 point for naming H⁺ as the shared ion; 1 point for explaining that the shared property traces to the shared ion (not to the differing parts). Full credit requires the cause-and-pattern link, not just "they're both acids." NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2.

**4. NaCl (table salt):**

- **Acid, base, or neither:** Neither (neutral).
- **Prediction for litmus:** No color change to either red or blue litmus — it behaves like the distilled-water control. NaCl has no leading H (no H⁺ released) and no OH group (no OH⁻ released), so it produces neither ion in water and shows no acid or base properties.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "neither/neutral"; 1 point for predicting no litmus change **and** explaining the absence of a leading H or OH group. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) and (b) — Classification from the formula and the ion produced:**

| Formula | Classification | Ion produced in water | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| HI | Arrhenius acid | H⁺ | Leading H → releases H⁺. HI → H⁺ + I⁻ |
| LiOH | Arrhenius base | OH⁻ | OH group bonded to a metal (lithium) → releases OH⁻. LiOH → Li⁺ + OH⁻ |
| HClO₃ | Arrhenius acid | H⁺ | Leading H → releases H⁺. HClO₃ → H⁺ + ClO₃⁻ |

These three substances were **not** tested in the stations lab, so students must apply the formula rule rather than recall a result. Note that HClO₃ contains both an H and oxygen, but the leading H is what makes it an acid — students who classify it as a base because they see "O…" should be redirected to the leading-H rule.

*Rubric:* part (a) — 1 point for each correct classification (3 points total). Part (b) — 1 point for correctly pairing H⁺ with the two acids and OH⁻ with the base (1 point total for the full set; partial credit for a partial set). A common error is calling HClO₃ a base because it contains oxygen and an implied O–H; flag the leading-H rule. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2.

**(c) Why all acids share the same properties:**

*Full-credit model answer:* All acids share the same properties because they all release the same ion — the hydrogen ion, H⁺ — into water; even though the acids are made of different other elements, the shared H⁺ is the common cause behind the shared pattern of properties (sour, reacting with metals, turning blue litmus red).

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that (i) identifies H⁺ (the hydrogen ion) as the common ion all acids release, and (ii) connects that shared ion to the shared properties (the Patterns idea — same ion, same behavior, regardless of the other elements). Responses that say only "they're all acids" or "they all have H in the formula" without naming H⁺ as the released ion responsible for the behavior earn partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for naming H⁺ (hydrogen ion) as the common ion every acid produces in water; 1 point for explaining that the shared ion is the cause of the shared property pattern (not the differing parts of each formula). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of how two substances from totally different sources (lemon juice and HCl, or soap and drain cleaner) produced the *same* result at a station — students who connect the surprise to the shared-ion idea are showing that the Patterns concept landed.
- Responses that name a partner or a specific station result ("the metal only fizzed at the acid stations") as the thing that changed their thinking — this signals that the active-learning structure (data before vocabulary) did the cognitive work it was designed to do.
- The strongest transfer appears when a student connects the lab back to the opening circle — a sour food or slippery soap from their own experience now sorted into the right family by behavior.

If a student classified substances by *source* ("it came from a lemon, so it's natural and not really an acid") rather than by the leading H / OH group and the litmus behavior, have a brief one-on-one before the next lesson: re-anchor on the formula rule (leading H → H⁺ → acid) and the station data, which sorted household and lab substances together purely by behavior.
