## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Compound: 40.0% C, 6.7% H, 53.3% O; molar mass 180 g/mol.**

Assume a 100 g sample → C = 40.0 g, H = 6.7 g, O = 53.3 g.

| Element | Mass (g) | ÷ molar mass | = moles | ÷ smallest = ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | 40.0 | 12.0 | 3.33 | 3.33 ÷ 3.33 = 1 |
| H | 6.7 | 1.0 | 6.7 | 6.7 ÷ 3.33 ≈ 2 |
| O | 53.3 | 16.0 | 3.33 | 3.33 ÷ 3.33 = 1 |

**Empirical formula: CH₂O.**

Empirical formula mass = 12.0 + 2(1.0) + 16.0 = **30.0 g/mol**. Multiplier = 180 ÷ 30 = **6**. Multiply every subscript by 6 → **Molecular formula: C₆H₁₂O₆** (glucose).

*Rubric:* 1 point for moles of each element (must divide mass by molar mass); 1 point for the empirical formula CH₂O via divide-by-smallest; 1 point for the molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₆ with the multiplier 6 shown. A student who reports the *mass* ratio (40 : 6.7 : 53.3) as the formula skipped the mole conversion — provide written feedback: convert grams to moles before taking the ratio. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**2. Compound: 75.0% C, 25.0% H.**

Assume a 100 g sample → C = 75.0 g, H = 25.0 g.

| Element | Mass (g) | ÷ molar mass | = moles | ÷ smallest = ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | 75.0 | 12.0 | 6.25 | 6.25 ÷ 6.25 = 1 |
| H | 25.0 | 1.0 | 25.0 | 25.0 ÷ 6.25 = 4 |

**Empirical formula: CH₄.**

Empirical formula mass = 12.0 + 4(1.0) = 16.0 g/mol. The molecule's molar mass equals its empirical mass (16 g/mol), so the multiplier is 1 and the **molecular formula is also CH₄** (methane). Empirical and molecular formula are identical here.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the moles (6.25 mol C, 25 mol H); 1 point for the empirical formula CH₄ (ratio 1 : 4). A common error is rounding 6.25 ÷ 6.25 incorrectly or failing to recognize 25 ÷ 6.25 = 4 — check the divide-by-smallest step. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**3. Magnesium oxide: 0.243 g Mg combined with 0.160 g O.**

| Element | Mass (g) | ÷ molar mass | = moles | ÷ smallest = ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mg | 0.243 | 24.3 | 0.0100 | 0.0100 ÷ 0.0100 = 1 |
| O | 0.160 | 16.0 | 0.0100 | 0.0100 ÷ 0.0100 = 1 |

**Empirical formula: MgO** (1 : 1 ratio).

Step-by-step: both elements give 0.0100 mol, so the atom ratio is 1 : 1. Even though magnesium is about 60% of the *mass*, it is an equal *count* of atoms because each Mg atom is heavier than each O atom. This matches the pie chart `mgo_percent_composition.png` and is the formula written on the vial.

*Rubric:* 1 point for converting both masses to 0.0100 mol; 1 point for the 1 : 1 ratio → MgO. A student who writes Mg₃O₂ or "more Mg" used the mass ratio instead of the mole ratio — the key teaching point of the lesson; flag for a quick re-check. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Empirical formula of the 30.4% N / 69.6% O compound:**

Assume a 100 g sample → N = 30.4 g, O = 69.6 g.

| Element | Mass (g) | ÷ molar mass | = moles | ÷ smallest = ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| N | 30.4 | 14.0 | 2.17 | 2.17 ÷ 2.17 = 1 |
| O | 69.6 | 16.0 | 4.35 | 4.35 ÷ 2.17 ≈ 2 |

**Empirical formula: NO₂** (ratio N : O = 1 : 2).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the moles (2.17 mol N, 4.35 mol O — must divide by molar mass); 1 point for the empirical formula NO₂ via divide-by-smallest. A student who reports the mass ratio or "more O" without converting to moles missed the core skill — note for follow-up. The N and O compound is *not* shown on either figure, so students must run the full routine rather than read an answer. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**(b) Molecular formula given molar mass 92 g/mol:**

Empirical formula mass of NO₂ = 14.0 + 2(16.0) = **46.0 g/mol**. Multiplier = 92 ÷ 46 = **2**. Multiply every subscript by 2 → **Molecular formula: N₂O₄** (dinitrogen tetroxide).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the empirical formula mass (46 g/mol); 1 point for the multiplier 2 and the molecular formula N₂O₄. Partial credit for a correct multiplier method applied to an incorrect empirical formula carried from part (a). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**(c) Why the empirical formula gives only a ratio while the molecular formula gives the actual count:**

*Full-credit model answer:* The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number *ratio* of atoms, so it tells you the proportion (1 N to 2 O) but not how many atoms are actually in one molecule; the molecular formula scales that ratio up by a whole-number multiple set by the molar mass, giving the true number of each atom in one molecule (here N₂O₄, which is two empirical units).

*Acceptable variations:* Any response stating that the empirical formula is a ratio/proportion and the molecular formula is the actual number of atoms (a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula) earns full credit. Mentioning that you need the molar mass to get from one to the other is a bonus, not required.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for identifying the empirical formula as a ratio/simplest proportion; 1 point for identifying the molecular formula as the actual count / whole-number multiple. A response that only says "they're different sizes" without naming ratio vs. count earns partial credit. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the mass-vs-mole surprise — students who note that 60% magnesium by mass still gave a 1 : 1 atom ratio are showing they grasped that mass ratio is not atom ratio, the central idea of the lesson.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("empirical formula — the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms — is found by converting mass to moles") are demonstrating the Hochman writing move from the notes and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary.
- Students who name a group member who helped them catch the mass-vs-mole error are signaling that the BTC vertical-surface structure supported their reasoning — note this as evidence that the visibly-random-group format is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows correct molar-mass lookups but a mass ratio reported as the formula (e.g., "N : O is 30 : 70"), the error is the missing mole conversion, not the Periodic Table — a brief check of "what did you divide each mass by?" at the start of the next lesson should resolve it. If the divide-by-smallest step is the problem (e.g., 4.35 ÷ 2.17 reported as 1 rather than 2), reteach that step before stoichiometry builds on it.
