## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Atoms in 3 moles of helium (He):**

> Setup: 3 mol × (6.02 × 10²³ atoms / 1 mol)
> Answer: **1.806 × 10²⁴ atoms** (accept 1.81 × 10²⁴)

Step-by-step: the "mol" units cancel, leaving atoms. 3 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 18.06 × 10²³ = 1.806 × 10²⁴ atoms. Because we have *more* than one mole, the count is correctly larger than 6.02 × 10²³.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct setup (6.02 × 10²³ on top so "mol" cancels); 1 point for the correct answer with the exponent adjusted to 10²⁴. A student who writes 1.8 × 10²³ kept the wrong exponent — note that 3 × 6.02 = 18.06, which shifts the power of ten up by one. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**2. Moles in 3.01 × 10²³ molecules of water (H₂O):**

> Setup: 3.01 × 10²³ molecules × (1 mol / 6.02 × 10²³ molecules)
> Answer: **0.500 mol** (accept 0.5 mol)

Step-by-step: "molecules" cancel, leaving mol. 3.01 × 10²³ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 0.500. This is *half* a mole, which makes sense because 3.01 × 10²³ is half of Avogadro's number.

*Rubric:* 1 point for placing 6.02 × 10²³ on the bottom so "molecules" cancels; 1 point for the correct answer (0.500 mol). A student who multiplied (getting ~1.8 × 10⁴⁷) set the conversion factor upside down — redirect to the unit-cancellation check. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**3. Formula units in 0.25 mole of NaCl:**

> Setup: 0.25 mol × (6.02 × 10²³ formula units / 1 mol)
> Answer: **1.505 × 10²³ formula units** (accept 1.5 × 10²³)

Step-by-step: "mol" cancels, leaving formula units. 0.25 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.505 × 10²³ formula units. Because we have *less* than one mole, the count is correctly smaller than 6.02 × 10²³. Note: NaCl is ionic, so its particles are "formula units," not molecules — Avogadro's number is the same either way.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct setup (6.02 × 10²³ on top); 1 point for the correct answer (1.505 × 10²³). Accept "molecules" terminology with a note that ionic compounds are counted in formula units. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**4. Why a mole of carbon (12 g) and a mole of sugar (180 g) hold the same number of particles but weigh different amounts:**

*Full-credit model answer:* Both containers hold exactly one mole — 6.02 × 10²³ particles — so the *count* is identical, but each sugar molecule (C₆H₁₂O₆) is built from 24 atoms and is far heavier than a single carbon atom, so the total mass of a mole of sugar is much greater even though the number of particles is the same.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that states the counts are equal (one mole / 6.02 × 10²³ particles) AND attributes the mass difference to the particles themselves being different (heavier molecules/atoms, or more atoms per particle) earns full credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points — 1 point for stating the particle counts are equal (one mole each); 1 point for attributing the mass difference to the kind/mass of the individual particles, not the count. A response that says only "sugar is heavier" without explaining the equal count earns 1 point. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Atoms in 2 moles of helium (He):**

> Setup: 2 mol × (6.02 × 10²³ atoms / 1 mol)
> Answer: **1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms** (accept 1.20 × 10²⁴)

Step-by-step: "mol" cancels, leaving atoms. 2 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 12.04 × 10²³ = 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms. Because we have two moles, the count is twice Avogadro's number, correctly larger than 6.02 × 10²³.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the setup (6.02 × 10²³ on top so "mol" cancels); 1 point for the correct answer with the exponent shifted to 10²⁴. A student who writes 1.2 × 10²³ kept the wrong exponent — 2 × 6.02 = 12.04 raises the power of ten by one. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**(b) Moles in 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms of iron (Fe):**

> Setup: 1.204 × 10²⁴ atoms × (1 mol / 6.02 × 10²³ atoms)
> Answer: **2.00 mol** (accept 2 mol)

Step-by-step: "atoms" cancel, leaving mol. 1.204 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.00 mol. Because 1.204 × 10²⁴ is twice Avogadro's number, the answer is correctly two moles.

*Rubric:* 1 point for placing 6.02 × 10²³ on the bottom so "atoms" cancels; 1 point for the correct answer (2.00 mol). A student who multiplied set the factor upside down — redirect to the unit-cancellation check. The element (iron) does not change the conversion: Avogadro's number is the same for every substance. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

**(c) Why a mole of lead has more mass than a mole of aluminum:**

*Full-credit model answer:* Each lead atom is much heavier than each aluminum atom — lead's molar mass is 207 g while aluminum's is only 27 g — so when you count out the same number of atoms (6.02 × 10²³, one mole) for each, the lead atoms collectively have a far greater total mass.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that identifies the greater mass of an individual lead atom (larger molar mass, more protons/neutrons) as the reason, AND notes that the particle count is the same in both moles (one mole / 6.02 × 10²³ atoms), earns full credit. A response that says only "lead is heavier" without explaining the equal count earns partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points — 1 point for identifying the greater atomic/molar mass of lead as the reason; 1 point for acknowledging that both moles contain the same number of atoms (6.02 × 10²³). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the scale of a mole (6.02 × 10²³) and a moment of surprise at how large it is — students who connect the huge number to the smallness of atoms are showing they understand *why* the mole exists, not just what it equals.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("a mole — a fixed count of 6.02 × 10²³ particles — is...") are demonstrating the Hochman writing move from Phase 3 and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary into active sentence structure.
- Students who name a partner who helped them get the conversion direction right (particles on top vs. bottom) are signaling that the group investigation supported their learning — note this as evidence the collaborative structure is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows the conversion factor inverted (multiplying when they should divide, or vice versa), the error is in deciding which way 6.02 × 10²³ goes — reinforce the unit-cancellation check at the start of the next lesson. If a student treats the mole as a mass (e.g., answers "2 moles = 12 g of helium" for part a), revisit the count-vs-mass distinction before stoichiometry builds on it.
