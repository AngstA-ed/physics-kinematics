## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Bending a copper wire into a coil.**

- **Physical or chemical:** physical change.
- **Evidence:** the wire is still copper — only its shape changed; no new substance, no sign of chemical change.
- **Reversible?** Yes — it can be straightened back out, which is consistent with a physical change. · *Rubric:* 1 point for "physical"; 1 point for citing "still copper / shape only" as evidence. Saying "physical" with no evidence → partial. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**2. Lighting a candle (melting wax vs. burning wick).**

- **Melting wax — physical change** because the wax changes from solid to liquid (a state change) but is still wax; the particles are re-spaced by added thermal energy, and it would re-solidify on cooling.
- **Burning wick — chemical change** because it produces new substances (it gives off light and heat and leaves soot/ash); the burning cannot be reversed. · *Rubric:* 1 point for each correctly classified change with a matching reason — 2 points total. A student who calls all of "lighting a candle" chemical without separating the melting from the burning earns partial credit; the discriminating skill is recognizing two *different* changes happening at once. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**3. Antacid bubbles vs. boiling bubbles.**

- **Antacid bubbles — chemical change** because the gas produced is a **new substance** (carbon dioxide) that was not present before; this is the "gas produced" sign of chemical change.
- **Boiling bubbles — physical change** because the gas is the **same substance** (water turning to water vapor); no new substance forms. · *Rubric:* 1 point for correctly distinguishing the two; 1 point for the key reason — *new gas* (chemical) vs. *same substance as vapor* (physical). This item directly targets the "bubbles always mean chemical" misconception. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**4. Sulfur — 5 g vs. 50 g sample.**

- **Color:** still yellow → **intensive** (does not depend on amount).
- **Mass:** the 50 g sample is *not* the same mass as the 5 g sample → **extensive** (depends on amount). · *Rubric:* 1 point for each correct classification with the "depends on amount?" reasoning — 2 points total. Note: melting point (115 °C) is also intensive; accept that as bonus reasoning. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Toasting bread until brown with a burnt smell.**

*Chemical change.* Signs of chemical change used: a **color change** (the bread turns brown) and a **new odor / gas produced** (the burnt smell), and the change is **hard to reverse** (you cannot un-toast the bread). New substances form as the bread's molecules break down and recombine under heat.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "chemical"; 1 point for naming at least one valid sign of chemical change (color change, new odor, or irreversibility). A student who says "physical because it's still bread" should receive feedback: the browning and burnt smell indicate new substances formed — toasting is not just a change of shape. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**(b) Intensive or extensive for the gold ring.**

- **(i) Density of the gold ring — intensive.** Density does not depend on how much gold you have; a tiny shaving and the whole ring have the same density.
- **(ii) Mass of the same gold ring — extensive.** Mass depends on the amount of substance present; a larger piece of the ring has more mass.

*Rubric:* 1 point each — 2 points total. A common error is calling mass intensive "because everything has mass"; correct to: the *amount* of mass depends on how much substance there is, so mass is extensive. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**(c) Why a chemical change makes a new substance but a physical change does not (using "bonds" and "energy").**

*Full-credit model answer:* In a chemical change, enough **energy** is added to break the **bonds** inside the substance's particles so the atoms recombine into new substances; in a physical change, the energy only re-spaces or rearranges the particles without breaking those internal bonds, so the substance keeps its identity.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that (1) states a chemical change breaks/remakes bonds to form a new substance and (2) states a physical change does not break those bonds (only changes form/state/arrangement), using the words "bonds" and "energy," earns full credit. Responses that only say "it makes something new" without referencing bonds/energy receive partial credit.

*Tolerance/rubric:* (a) 2 points (classification + valid sign); (b) 2 points (1 each); (c) 2 points — 1 for the chemical-change/bonds-broken idea, 1 for the physical-change/no-bonds-broken contrast, both using "bonds" and "energy." NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A specific everyday change the student now classifies differently — e.g., "I used to think dissolving sugar in my tea was chemical, but it's physical because the sugar is still sugar" — rather than a generic "I learned about changes."
- Responses that name a peer, a specific station, or the magnesium demo as the moment something clicked — this signals the BTC group structure and the public vertical-surface thinking landed.
- Any response that connects to the opening circle's "something that couldn't go back" theme shows the strongest transfer between the irreversibility intuition and the formal chemical-change concept.

If a student still labels a state change (melting, boiling, dissolving) as chemical in the reflection or on the Exit Ticket, flag them for a brief one-on-one at the start of the next lesson: re-ask "is it still the same substance?" and have them recover the dissolved salt by evaporation as a concrete check. This is the single most common misconception in this lesson and is worth catching before the unit builds further.
