## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

**HS-PS4-4** — *"Evaluate the validity and reliability of claims in published materials of the effects that different frequencies of electromagnetic radiation have when absorbed by matter."* This lesson turns the radioactivity students have studied (Lessons 01–03) toward a societal decision: nuclear chemistry produces both ionizing radiation (high-frequency, high-energy: gamma rays and X-rays) and the energy and medical isotopes that society depends on. Students are expected to read published claims about the effects of radiation — from a power-plant fact sheet, a hospital's isotope-treatment page, a news article about Chernobyl or Fukushima — and judge whether each claim is *valid* (does the evidence actually support it?) and *reliable* (is the source trustworthy and the result repeatable?). The performance expectation is not to memorize a verdict on nuclear power; it is to evaluate evidence and weigh benefit against risk like a scientifically literate citizen.

The Cross-Cutting Concept of **Cause and Effect** is the explicit lens, paired with the engineering idea that every technology has both intended effects (a benefit) and side effects (a risk). Ionizing radiation has a dose-dependent cause→effect relationship: a small dose images a tumor (benefit); a large, targeted dose destroys it (benefit); an uncontrolled dose damages healthy DNA (risk). Same radiation, different dose, different effect. Students use this dose→effect chain to evaluate published claims rather than reacting to the word "nuclear."

### Phenomenon

A real proposal lands in your community: an energy company applies to build a nuclear power plant fifteen miles from your school. The town splits. One flyer says the plant will deliver carbon-free electricity for 60 years, create hundreds of jobs, and meet safety standards far stricter than the coal plant it replaces. Another flyer shows a photo of the abandoned city of Pripyat near Chernobyl and asks, *"Do you want this next door?"* A third source — the same hospital where a student's aunt was treated for thyroid cancer with radioactive iodine — points out that the *same* nuclear science that powers the plant cured her. Every flyer is technically about "radiation," yet they point to opposite conclusions. The phenomenon is not a chemical reaction in a beaker; it is a genuine community decision where the chemistry is real, the benefits are real, the risks are real, and a citizen has to evaluate competing published claims to vote responsibly.

### Javalab / Labs

- **Risk–benefit case-study stations (jigsaw):** Three printed case packets — (1) **Medical isotopes** (Technetium-99m imaging and Iodine-131 thyroid treatment; tie to the decay curve in `figures/medical_isotope_decay.png`), (2) **Power generation** (a modern reactor fact sheet vs. the Chernobyl 1986 and Fukushima 2011 accident summaries), and (3) **Nuclear weapons** (a brief, age-appropriate historical and treaty-focused summary). Each group reads one packet, extracts the benefit claim and the risk claim, and rates each claim's validity and reliability on a shared rubric. No external URL is required; a one-page summary per case works well. For a digital option, a Javalab radioactive-decay simulation can run alongside the medical-isotope station so students *see* the isotope clearing the body.
- **Dose-comparison reading:** Students interpret `figures/radiation_dose_comparison.png` (a log-scale chart of typical effective doses) to answer: which everyday activities expose you to radiation, and how does the legal occupational limit compare to a single CT scan? This grounds the "is it dangerous?" question in numbers instead of fear.
- **Structured debate:** After the jigsaw, the class runs a 5-minute structured mini-debate on the driving question, with assigned roles (see Strategy Spotlight: ACTIVE LEARNING).

### Assessments

- **Argumentative position paper** (district summative, following lesson): students write a one-page risk–benefit position on the proposed plant, stating a claim, citing at least two pieces of evidence from the case studies, weighing one counter-argument, and evaluating the reliability of one source they used. See `Assessments/` folder once created.
- **Exit Ticket** (Phase 5): three items — one validity/reliability judgment of a published claim, one dose-comparison reasoning item, and one sentence evaluating a benefit against its paired risk. The Exit Ticket values are kept distinct from the worksheet practice. See `Answer_Key.docx`.

**Lesson objectives — students can:**

- Distinguish a benefit from a risk in a published claim about nuclear technology, and identify the dose→effect reasoning behind each.
- Evaluate the **validity** (does the evidence support the claim?) and **reliability** (is the source trustworthy and repeatable?) of a published claim.
- Use the dose-comparison data to put a radiation risk in quantitative perspective rather than reacting to the word "nuclear."
- Construct an evidence-based **risk–benefit** argument and weigh at least one counter-claim, using Cause and Effect.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Opening Circle + Do Now

Begin with a brief **community-building opening circle** (2–3 min). Everyone — teacher included — answers: *"Name one technology you use that has a real benefit AND a real downside. Cars? Phones? Something else?"* One round, one sentence each, no judgment. This primes the central move of the lesson: useful things carry trade-offs, and judging them means weighing both sides, not picking a slogan.

Then post the **Do Now**:

> *"Your town just received an application to build a nuclear power plant 15 miles from our school. Before you know anything else, write your gut reaction in one sentence: are you for it, against it, or unsure — and what is the ONE thing you'd most want to know before deciding?"*

Give students 2 minutes to write silently, then take three or four "one thing you'd want to know" responses (not the for/against vote — that comes later).

**Anticipated student responses to Do Now:**

- "Against it — nuclear is dangerous, like Chernobyl." — capture; ask: *how dangerous, compared to what? That's exactly the kind of claim we'll learn to check.*
- "For it — it doesn't make pollution like the coal plant." — affirm; ask: *is that claim true, and how would we verify it?*
- "Unsure — I'd want to know how often these plants actually have accidents." — excellent; that is a validity/reliability question, and we will answer it with evidence today.

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — the three flyers

**Teacher actions.** Project or hold up three sources, one at a time. Flyer 1 (pro-plant): carbon-free electricity for 60 years, hundreds of jobs, stricter safety standards than the coal plant it replaces. Flyer 2 (anti-plant): a photo of abandoned Pripyat near Chernobyl with the caption *"Do you want this next door?"* Source 3 (a hospital page): the radioactive iodine that treated a student's aunt's thyroid cancer comes from the *same* nuclear science that powers the plant.

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Here is the strange part. Every one of these sources is talking about the exact same thing — the behavior of unstable nuclei and the radiation they give off. The chemistry doesn't change from flyer to flyer. Yet flyer 1 says 'build it,' flyer 2 says 'never,' and the hospital says 'this saved a life.' Same science, opposite conclusions. So the question can't be answered by the word 'nuclear.' It has to be answered by looking at *what the radiation actually does* — and at *whether each flyer's claim is backed by good evidence*."

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "They're just trying to scare us with the Chernobyl picture." — strong observation; that's a reliability question — does a scary photo count as evidence? We'll name that move today.
- "But the hospital one is real — my family has had scans." — yes; that's a documented benefit; we'll examine the dose that makes it safe.
- "How can the same thing cure cancer AND cause it?" — that is the heart of the lesson: the answer is *dose*. Hold that question.

**Driving question** (post on the board and leave it there):

> *Should our community accept a nuclear power plant nearby? How do we evaluate the competing claims — benefit and risk — fairly?*

### 8–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two columns on the board: **I notice… / I wonder…**

Collect 3–4 responses about the three flyers. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: all three sources are about radiation, but they push opposite conclusions. What information would you need to decide whether each flyer's claim is actually *true* — not just loud?"

Target insight (leave open if no one lands it yet): you need the **evidence behind the claim** and the **trustworthiness of the source** — that is, validity and reliability. We'll name those tools formally in Phase 3.

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 30 min)*

### 10–14 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt on the board:

> *"Before we read any case studies, make your best two-column list: in column A, write every BENEFIT of nuclear chemistry you can think of; in column B, every RISK. Then circle the one benefit and the one risk you think are the most important for the plant decision."*

Students write silently for 3–4 minutes. This surfaces prior knowledge from Lessons 01–03 and from life (medical scans, the news). Collect the range: most will list "energy" and "bombs"; fewer will list medical imaging or cancer treatment; almost none will distinguish *dose*. That gap is the instructional target.

### 14–26 min · ABCs Activity — Risk–Benefit Case-Study Jigsaw

**Before any formal vocabulary is introduced**, students build the evidence base by working a case study in groups. Assign each group ONE of three case packets. Each student fills in the rating rubric individually, then the group reaches consensus.

![Bar chart titled 'Putting radiation risk in perspective' showing typical effective radiation doses on a log scale in millisieverts: eating one banana ≈ 0.0001 mSv, a dental X-ray ≈ 0.005 mSv, a chest CT scan ≈ 7 mSv, average annual natural background ≈ 3 mSv, and the US annual occupational dose limit for a nuclear plant worker ≈ 50 mSv; bars increase across the chart and each is labeled with its value.](figures/radiation_dose_comparison.png)

**Case A — Medical isotopes (benefit-forward).** Technetium-99m is injected to image organs; Iodine-131 is swallowed to destroy an overactive or cancerous thyroid. Both work because they emit radiation a scanner can detect or that kills target cells — and both *clear the body quickly* because they have short half-lives. Pair this station with the decay curve:

![Line graph titled 'Why medical isotopes fade fast' showing the percent of Iodine-131 remaining versus time measured in half-lives (each half-life is 8 days for I-131); the curve starts at 100% and halves at each step to 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.125%, and about 1.6% after six half-lives, illustrating that a medical isotope decays away rapidly so the patient is not irradiated indefinitely.](figures/medical_isotope_decay.png)

**Case B — Power generation (benefit and risk).** A modern reactor fact sheet: carbon-free electricity, high energy density (one uranium fuel pellet ≈ one ton of coal), strict containment. Paired with two accident summaries: **Chernobyl (1986)** — a flawed reactor design plus operator error during an unauthorized test caused an explosion and a large radioactive release; and **Fukushima (2011)** — a magnitude-9 earthquake and tsunami knocked out cooling, causing meltdowns, though radiation-attributable deaths were far fewer than the tsunami itself caused.

**Case C — Nuclear weapons (risk-forward).** A brief, age-appropriate, treaty-focused summary: the same fission that powers a plant can, uncontrolled and weaponized, release destructive energy; international non-proliferation treaties exist precisely because the science is dual-use.

**The rating rubric each student completes (your answer-key reference):**

| Column | What students record |
|---|---|
| Benefit claim | The specific good the source claims (e.g., "carbon-free electricity") |
| Risk claim | The specific harm the source claims or implies |
| Evidence given | What data or example backs each claim |
| **Validity** (1–3) | Does the evidence actually support the claim? |
| **Reliability** (1–3) | Is the source trustworthy, and would the result repeat? |
| Dose / cause→effect note | What dose or condition turns this from benefit to risk? |

**Teacher facilitation language (circulate):**

> "Don't argue yet about whether nuclear power is good or bad. First just *extract* — what exactly is each flyer claiming, and what evidence does it give? A claim with a number you could check is different from a claim that's just a scary photo."

> "On the dose chart: a single chest CT scan is about 7 mSv. The annual background radiation you get just from living on Earth is about 3 mSv. So one CT is a little over two years of normal background — meaningful, but not the apocalypse. Where does that put the risk?"

**Anticipated student responses during ABCs:**

- "The Chernobyl flyer doesn't actually give a number — it's just a picture." — excellent; that's a low-reliability claim *as evidence*; the accident was real, but a photo alone doesn't quantify the present-day risk of a modern plant.
- "Wait, the medical iodine is radioactive too? Why is THAT safe?" — because of dose and half-life: a small, targeted amount that decays away in days (point them to the decay curve), versus a reactor's enormous continuous inventory.
- "Coal kills people too, from air pollution." — strong point; valid risk–benefit reasoning compares a technology to its *alternatives*, not to zero risk.

### 26–30 min · Cross-group share-out (jigsaw reassembly)

Re-form mixed groups (one student from each case) or run a quick whole-class round-robin. Each case reports its single strongest benefit claim and single strongest risk claim, plus the validity/reliability score it earned.

> "Put all three cases side by side. What pattern do you see across medical, power, and weapons? What is the ONE variable that decides whether the radiation helps or harms?"

Surface the key idea (leave the formal term for Phase 3): the deciding variable is **dose and control** — how much radiation, how targeted, and whether it is contained. The same nuclei, the same gamma rays, with a different dose and different control, land on opposite sides of the benefit/risk line.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(30 – 36 min)*

### 30–33 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "Look across your case rubrics. Turn to a partner: which flyer made a claim you'd actually *trust*, and which made a claim you'd *doubt*? Don't tell me which side it was on — tell me WHY you trust or doubt it."

Target consensus:

> "A claim is worth trusting when (1) it gives real evidence that actually supports what it says — that's whether it's *valid* — and (2) the source is credible and the result would hold up if checked again — that's whether it's *reliable*. A scary photo with no data can be about a real event yet still be a weak claim, because it doesn't show what the risk is *now*, for *this* plant."

> "And the reason the same radiation can cure or harm comes down to one word we keep circling: dose. Let's name all of this."

### 33–36 min · Vocabulary introduction (exactly 3 terms)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "First term. When you weigh the good a technology does against the harm it could cause — and decide based on the balance — you are doing a **risk–benefit analysis**. It is not 'is there any risk?' (everything has some); it is 'do the benefits, with the risks controlled, outweigh the harms, compared to the alternatives?' That's exactly what you did with the three cases."

> "Second term. When you ask 'does the evidence actually support this claim?' you are checking its **validity**. A flyer can be loud and still be invalid if its evidence doesn't fit its conclusion — like using a 1986 Chernobyl photo to argue a 2025 plant is unsafe. The event is real; the *logic from that event to this plant* may not be valid."

> "Third term. When you ask 'can I trust this source, and would this result repeat?' you are checking its **reliability**. A peer-reviewed safety study or a hospital's documented treatment record is more reliable than an anonymous flyer. Reliability is about the *source and the repeatability*, validity is about the *evidence-to-claim fit*. Good citizens check both."

Post the three terms on the board. Students fill them in on their notes.

**Discussion prompts to deploy here:**

- "The hospital says radioactive iodine cured thousands of thyroid patients with a documented success rate. Is that claim *valid*, *reliable*, or both?" — *Expected response:* both — documented outcomes are evidence that supports the claim (valid) and a hospital's records are a trustworthy, repeatable source (reliable).
- "A flyer says 'radiation is radiation — a plant is just a slow bomb.' What's wrong using validity and reliability language?" — *Expected response:* invalid — the evidence (fission happens in both) does not support the claim, because a reactor is engineered for controlled, sub-critical reactions and a bomb is engineered for an uncontrolled one. Dose and control are entirely different.

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(36 – 40 min)*

### 36–39 min · Structured mini-debate + Cause-and-Effect expansion

Run a 5-minute **structured debate** (see Strategy Spotlight: ACTIVE LEARNING). Assign roles so every voice is used: **Pro-plant** team cites a benefit claim and its evidence; **Caution** team cites a risk claim and its evidence; **Fact-checkers** rate one claim from each side for validity and reliability. Then every student writes one Because/But/So sentence.

> *"A nuclear power plant produces ionizing radiation, just like a medical scanner does."*
> *"This is **because** ______, **but** _______, **so** _______."*

Model one aloud:

> "A nuclear plant and a CT scanner both involve ionizing radiation **because** both rely on high-energy emissions from nuclear processes, **but** a contained reactor and a controlled medical dose deliver radiation in carefully limited, shielded amounts while an *uncontrolled* release does not, **so** whether the radiation is a benefit or a risk depends on dose and control — not on the word 'radiation' itself."

Then have students try one for a risk claim (e.g., the Fukushima cooling failure) using validity/reliability language.

### 39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "Return to the town vote. You now have a method, not just a gut reaction. Using the words *risk–benefit*, *validity*, and *dose*, write the one sentence you would say at the town hall to explain how you'd evaluate the plant — without yet saying yes or no."

Target: "I'd run a risk–benefit analysis — checking whether each flyer's claim is valid and reliable, and comparing the controlled dose at a modern plant against its alternatives — instead of voting on the word 'nuclear.'"

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Post or read aloud:

> *(a) A campaign mailer states: "A power line near homes emits radiation, so it must cause the same cancers as a nuclear meltdown." Power lines emit low-frequency, non-ionizing radiation. Judge this claim's **validity** in one sentence — does the evidence support the conclusion?*
> *(b) Using the dose chart logic: a worker's annual occupational limit is 50 mSv and one chest CT is about 7 mSv. About how many CT-scans' worth of dose is the yearly worker limit, and is the limit set above or below a single scan?*
> *(c) Name one real BENEFIT and its paired RISK of nuclear technology, and state in one sentence what variable decides which one you get.*

Expected answers are in `Answer_Key.docx`. (These items are deliberately different from the worksheet's Make-It-Make-Sense practice.)

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "Before today, did you have a gut reaction to the word 'nuclear'? In one sentence: did evaluating the evidence change how confident you feel — and who or what (a case study, a classmate, the dose chart) moved your thinking?"

Collect rubrics; flag students who scored every claim identically (a sign they rated by topic, not evidence) for a brief check before the position paper.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "All radiation is equally dangerous — radiation is radiation." → **Correction:** Effect depends on *dose, type, and control*. A banana, a dental X-ray, and a CT scan all deliver radiation, but across six orders of magnitude of dose (see `figures/radiation_dose_comparison.png`). Ionizing radiation (gamma, X-ray) at high dose damages DNA; at a small, targeted dose it images or treats. The cause→effect chain is dose-dependent, not all-or-nothing.
- **Misconception:** "A nuclear power plant can explode like an atomic bomb." → **Correction:** Reactor fuel is low-enriched and the reaction is engineered to stay controlled and sub-critical; a weapon requires highly enriched material and an engineered *uncontrolled* chain reaction. Same underlying fission, opposite engineering. The Chernobyl explosion was a steam/hydrogen explosion and reactor-design failure, not a nuclear detonation.
- **Misconception:** "If a source is about a real event (Chernobyl), its claim must be valid." → **Correction:** Validity is about whether the evidence supports the *specific conclusion*. A real 1986 accident does not by itself validate the claim "a modern 2025 plant is unsafe." A claim can reference true events and still reason invalidly from them.
- **Misconception:** "Medical isotopes are safe, so they aren't really radioactive." → **Correction:** They are genuinely radioactive — that's *why* they work. Their safety comes from small, targeted doses and short half-lives, so they decay away quickly (the decay curve: I-131 drops to ~1.6% after six half-lives) instead of irradiating the patient indefinitely.
- **Misconception:** "A good risk–benefit decision means choosing the zero-risk option." → **Correction:** No real technology is zero-risk, and the alternative (e.g., a coal plant) carries its own large risks. Sound risk–benefit reasoning compares controlled risks and benefits *against the realistic alternatives*, not against an imaginary risk-free baseline.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frames for the rating rubric and Exit Ticket: *"The benefit is ___; the risk is ___; the dose that decides is ___."* and *"This claim is valid/invalid because the evidence ___ support the conclusion."* Word-choice box displayed on the board throughout: {risk–benefit analysis, validity, reliability, dose, ionizing radiation, half-life}. Pre-translate the three case packets' key terms in a bilingual glossary; allow students to discuss in home language but record judgments in English. Pair each abstract judgment with the visual dose chart so a numeric anchor is always present.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide a pre-structured rating rubric with the benefit/risk columns partially filled for one case, so the student's task is the *judgment* (validity/reliability score) rather than the extraction. Assign clear debate roles (one student is the timekeeper, one reads the team's prepared claim aloud, one is the evidence-holder) so participation is concrete. Pre-highlight the one or two sentences in each case packet that carry the central claim, reducing reading load. Offer the Exit Ticket with the (b) arithmetic scaffolded (50 ÷ 7 written as a setup).
- **Extensions:** (1) Find one number on the dose chart and write a sentence comparing the plant-worker annual limit to the dose from a cross-country flight (~0.04 mSv) — how many flights equal the limit? (2) Research one real nuclear-medicine isotope not covered in class (e.g., Cobalt-60, Lutetium-177) and write a validity/reliability note on a manufacturer's safety claim about it. (3) Compare the projected lifetime deaths-per-unit-energy of nuclear vs. coal vs. solar from a published source, and write one sentence on which alternative your risk–benefit analysis would favor and why.

## Strategy Spotlight

**ACTIVE LEARNING — case-study jigsaw + assigned-role structured debate.** Active learning means every student is *doing the cognitive work* — extracting claims, rating evidence, defending a position — rather than receiving a verdict from the teacher. For a values-charged topic like nuclear power, active learning is also the antidote to a lecture that would simply tell students what to think. The jigsaw guarantees each student becomes the "expert" on one case and is accountable to teach it; the assigned-role debate guarantees every student speaks with evidence, not just the loudest few.

**How to run the structured debate in 4–5 minutes (Phase 4):**

1. Pre-assign three roles before the debate begins: **Pro-plant** (argue a benefit with its evidence), **Caution** (argue a risk with its evidence), and **Fact-checkers** (rate one claim from each side for validity and reliability). Rotate roles across periods so no student is locked into one stance.
2. Each side gets 60 seconds to state ONE claim and its evidence — they must cite the case packet, not their feelings.
3. Fact-checkers get 60 seconds to score one claim per side: "valid? reliable? why?"
4. Close by naming the move aloud: *"Notice — nobody won by being louder. The claims that held up were the ones with evidence that actually fit. That is scientific literacy."*

Crucially, the teacher does **not** reveal a personal verdict on nuclear power. The objective (HS-PS4-4) is the *skill of evaluating claims*, not arriving at a prescribed opinion. A student who ends the period leaning either way but can defend the judgment with valid, reliable evidence has met the standard.

**Connection to Hochman literacy:** the Because/But/So sentence in Phase 4 channels the debate into writing — students name a *cause* (radiation is involved), a *contrast* (but dose and control differ), and a *consequence* (so benefit vs. risk depends on dose). This sentence becomes the thesis scaffold for the argumentative position paper.

**CRSE connection:** Risk-benefit decisions about industrial siting are rarely neutral — historically, hazardous facilities are disproportionately proposed near lower-income and minority communities (environmental justice). The opening circle and the "compared to what alternative?" framing invite students to bring their community's lived experience into the analysis: who bears the risk, who gets the benefit, and is that fair? This honors students as stakeholders, not just test-takers, and connects the chemistry to a real civic voice.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **risk–benefit analysis** — a structured comparison that weighs the benefits of a technology (with its risks controlled) against its potential harms and against the realistic alternatives, in order to make a justified decision; not "is there any risk?" but "do the controlled benefits outweigh the harms?"
- **validity** — whether the evidence actually supports the specific claim being made; a claim can reference true events and still be invalid if the reasoning from evidence to conclusion does not hold
- **reliability** — whether a source is trustworthy and whether its result would hold up if checked or repeated; reliability is about the *source and repeatability*, while validity is about the *evidence-to-claim fit*
