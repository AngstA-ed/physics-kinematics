## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

1. **"A plant uses fission, a bomb uses fission, so a plant is a slow-motion bomb."**
   - **Not valid.** The shared fact (both involve fission) does not support the conclusion that a plant will explode like a bomb. A reactor uses **low-enriched** fuel in an engineered **controlled, sub-critical** chain reaction; a weapon requires **highly enriched** material and an engineered **uncontrolled** chain reaction. The same underlying process is engineered toward opposite ends.
   - **Dose/control difference:** it is the *control* of the reaction (and the enrichment of the fuel) that decides the outcome — not the presence of fission. A meltdown like Chernobyl was a steam/hydrogen explosion and a reactor-design failure, not a nuclear detonation. · *Rubric:* "not valid" + names control/enrichment difference → full credit; "not valid" alone → partial. · NYSSLS: HS-PS4-4.

2. **Hospital I-131 claim.**
   - **Valid and reliable.** Documented patient outcomes are evidence that directly supports the success claim (valid), and a hospital's treatment records are a trustworthy, repeatable source (reliable). The 8-day half-life supports the "clears the body" claim with a checkable physical fact.
   - **Decay-curve reading:** after about **24 days = 3 half-lives**, about **12.5%** of the I-131 remains (100 → 50 → 25 → 12.5). · *Rubric:* "valid and reliable" + 12.5% → full credit; correct percent with weak justification → partial. Accept "about 12%." · NYSSLS: HS-PS4-4.

3. **1986 Pripyat photo → "the 2025 plant will become a wasteland."**
   - **Conclusion is not valid.** A real historical accident does not, by itself, establish that a *different* plant — a modern 2025 design with different engineering, regulations, and safety systems — will fail the same way. The event is real; the reasoning from that event to this plant is the weak link. (The photo is also low-*reliability as evidence about the present* because it provides no current data.)
   - **What a valid safety argument would need:** evidence about *this* plant — its reactor design and containment, the operator's accident/incident record, independent regulatory inspection results, and the projected dose to nearby residents compared with background and with the alternatives it replaces. · *Rubric:* "not valid" + at least one piece of plant-specific evidence named → full credit. · NYSSLS: HS-PS4-4.

## Exit Ticket Answer

(a) **Not valid.** The mailer equates a power line with a nuclear meltdown, but power lines emit **low-frequency, non-ionizing** radiation, which lacks the energy to break chemical bonds or directly damage DNA, while a meltdown releases **high-energy ionizing** radiation. Because the two are different *types* (frequencies) of radiation with very different effects on matter, the evidence ("both emit radiation") does not support the conclusion that they cause the same cancers. (This is exactly the HS-PS4-4 idea: the effect on matter depends on the *frequency/energy* of the radiation.)

(b) **50 ÷ 7 ≈ 7.1**, so the annual worker limit is roughly **7 CT scans'** worth of dose. The limit (≈ 50 mSv) is set **above** a single chest CT scan (≈ 7 mSv). Accept "about 7" or "7 to 7.1." Full credit requires the correct setup (50 ÷ 7) and the correct above/below judgment.

(c) Sample full-credit response — any correct benefit/risk pair with a deciding variable:
- *Benefit:* a medical scan/treatment images or cures disease (or: a power plant supplies carbon-free electricity). *Paired risk:* an uncontrolled or excessive dose damages healthy DNA (or: an accidental release contaminates an area).
- *Deciding variable:* **dose and control** — how much radiation, how targeted, and whether it is contained. Any equivalent answer naming dose, amount, targeting, or control of the radiation earns full credit.

*Tolerance/rubric:* (a) 1 point — "not valid" with a reason that distinguishes ionizing from non-ionizing (or names the frequency/energy difference). (b) 1 point for ≈ 7 with the correct setup; 1 point for "above"; 2 points total. (c) 1 point for a correct benefit/risk pair; 1 point for naming dose/control as the deciding variable; 2 points total. NYSSLS: HS-PS4-4.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine response. Look for:

- A specific shift in reasoning — "I used to think all radiation was the same, but the dose chart changed that" — rather than a generic "it was interesting."
- Naming a specific source of the shift: a case study, a classmate's argument in the debate, or the dose/decay figure. This signals the active-learning structure landed.
- Any response that separates a *gut reaction to the word "nuclear"* from an *evidence-based judgment* shows the strongest transfer toward HS-PS4-4 and the position paper.

If a student writes that nothing changed and still rates every claim identically, have a brief, non-punitive one-on-one before the position paper to check that they are evaluating *evidence* (validity/reliability) rather than reacting to the topic. Note: students may legitimately land on either side of the plant decision — the standard is met by the quality of the evidence-evaluation, not by the conclusion reached.
