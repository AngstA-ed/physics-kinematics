**Validity vs. reliability — two different checks:**

> **Validity** asks: does the __________ actually support the claim?
> **Reliability** asks: is the __________ trustworthy, and would the result __________ if checked again?

A claim can reference a *real* event (like Chernobyl, 1986) and still be **invalid** if the reasoning to a *different* situation (a 2025 plant) does not hold.

## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A campaign mailer states: *"A nuclear plant uses fission and an atom bomb uses fission, so living near a plant is just like living next to a bomb that will go off."*

**Step 1 — Identify the claim and its evidence:**

- Claim: _______________________________________________
- Evidence offered: _______________________________________________

**Step 2 — Check validity (does the evidence support the claim?):**

The evidence is "both use fission." Does that support "a plant is a bomb that will go off"? __________ (yes/no), because a reactor is engineered for a __________ (controlled / uncontrolled) chain reaction with low-enriched fuel, while a weapon needs a __________ (controlled / uncontrolled) chain reaction with highly enriched fuel.

**Step 3 — Check reliability (is the source trustworthy/repeatable?):**

An anonymous campaign mailer with no data is __________ (high / low) reliability compared with an engineering safety study.

**Step 4 — Name the deciding variable:**

The variable that turns the same fission into a benefit or a catastrophe is __________ and __________.

**Step 5 — Because / But / So:**

A nuclear plant is not the same as a bomb
**because** _______________________________________________,
**but** _______________________________________________,
**so** _______________________________________________.

*Check your reasoning:* "because" should name the shared science (fission); "but" should name the difference in dose/control/enrichment; "so" should state the corrected conclusion.

**Reminder — how to evaluate any published claim about radiation:**
1. What exactly is the **claim**?
2. What **evidence** is given? → check **validity** (does it fit?).
3. Who is the **source**? → check **reliability** (trustworthy? repeatable?).
4. Compare benefit and risk **against the realistic alternative** — no real technology is zero-risk.

**Key references (from the Nuclear Chemistry unit):** ionizing radiation = high-energy (gamma, X-ray); half-life = time for half a sample to decay (Lesson 03); dose is measured in millisieverts (mSv).
