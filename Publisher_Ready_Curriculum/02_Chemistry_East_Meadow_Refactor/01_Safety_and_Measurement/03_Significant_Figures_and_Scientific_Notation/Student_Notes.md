## Guided Notes

**The big idea — Scale, Proportion, and Quantity:** Every digit you write is a __________ about what you measured. Writing more digits than your instrument supports is a false __________ claim. Writing fewer digits than your instrument supports throws away useful information.

**Estimate the last digit — the rule:**

When you read any measuring instrument, the last digit you record is the __________ digit. All the digits the instrument's scale told you directly, plus that one estimated digit, are your __________.

- Reading from the figure: _____________ mL
- Certain digits: _____________ Estimated digit: _____________
- Number of significant figures: _____

**The five sig-fig rules:**

| Rule | Example | Sig figs |
|---|---|---|
| All non-zero digits count | 34.7 | |
| Captive zeros (between non-zero digits) count | 102.0 | |
| Leading zeros do not count — they are place-holders | 0.0040 | |
| Trailing zeros after a decimal point count | 1500. | |
| Trailing zeros in a whole number without a decimal are ambiguous | 1500 | |

**Scientific notation — why use it:**

Standard notation: 6,420,000 → coefficient __________ × 10 raised to the power __________ → _______________

The coefficient must be a number ≥ 1 and < 10. The exponent tells you how many places you moved the decimal.

- Moving the decimal to the *left* → the exponent is __________ (positive / negative)
- Moving the decimal to the *right* → the exponent is __________ (positive / negative)

Scientific notation makes the sig-fig count **explicit**: 6.42 × 10⁶ has _____ sig figs; 6.420 × 10⁶ has _____ sig figs. Standard notation can be ambiguous; scientific notation is not.

**Connecting precision to sig figs:**

A reading of 27.5 mL claims precision to the __________ (ones / tenths / hundredths) of a milliliter.
A reading of 28 mL claims precision to the __________ of a milliliter.
The reading with more significant figures is (circle one): **more precise / less precise**.

## Worked Example

**Scenario:** A student uses a centimeter ruler (smallest markings: 0.1 cm) to measure the length of a metal rod. She estimates one digit past the markings and records 8.47 cm. A second student uses a less precise ruler (smallest markings: 1 cm) and records 8 cm. A calculation multiplying these two values on a calculator returns 67.76.

**Step 1 — Count sig figs in each measured value:**

- 8.47 cm has _____ sig figs (rule: all non-zero digits count)
- 8 cm has _____ sig fig

**Step 2 — Identify the limiting value:**

When multiplying or dividing, the result may carry no more sig figs than the __________ (most / least) precise measurement. Here, the limiting value is __________ cm with _____ sig fig.

**Step 3 — Round the calculator result:**

Calculator result: 67.76 → rounded to _____ sig fig → ___________

**Step 4 — Because / But / So**

The calculator answer of 67.76 is a false precision claim
**because** _______________________________________________,
**but** _______________________________________________,
**so** _______________________________________________.

*Check your reasoning:* "because" should name what the instrument can actually support; "but" should explain why the calculator ignores that limit; "so" should state the correctly rounded answer.

**Reminder:** When in doubt about trailing zeros, express the number in scientific notation — it removes the ambiguity and shows exactly how many sig figs you mean. Example: 1500 is ambiguous; 1.5 × 10³ is clearly two sig figs; 1.500 × 10³ is clearly four.
