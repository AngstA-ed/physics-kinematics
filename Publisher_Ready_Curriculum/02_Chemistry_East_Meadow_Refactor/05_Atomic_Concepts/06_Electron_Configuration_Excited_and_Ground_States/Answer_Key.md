## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Sodium (Na, 11 electrons): 2-7-2.**

- **Sum of digits:** 2 + 7 + 2 = 11 ✓ (equals sodium's atomic number).
- **Verdict:** Valid **excited state**.
- **Reason:** The configuration has the correct total (11 electrons, so it is still sodium), but shell 2 holds only 7 electrons even though shell 3 is occupied. In the ground state shell 2 fills to 8 before any electron enters shell 3. The gap in shell 2 with an electron sitting in shell 3 means one electron absorbed energy and jumped up — the signature of an excited state.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct verdict (excited state); 1 point for a reason that names BOTH the correct total (11) AND the gap in a lower shell (shell 2 not full while shell 3 occupied). A student who says "different element" has confused excited state with a change in electron count — correct it: same total, same atom. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**2. Lithium (Li, 3 electrons): 1-2.** (Ground state of lithium is 2-1.)

- **Sum of digits:** 1 + 2 = 3 ✓ (equals lithium's atomic number).
- **Verdict:** Valid **excited state**.
- **Reason:** The total is correct (3 electrons, so it is still lithium), but shell 1 holds only 1 electron even though shell 2 is occupied. The ground state fills shell 1 to its maximum of 2 first, giving 2-1. Because shell 1 has a gap while shell 2 holds electrons, an electron has jumped up — this is the excited state.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct verdict (excited state); 1 point for naming the gap in shell 1 (only 1 electron where the ground state would have 2) while shell 2 is occupied. Accept any wording showing the lowest-shell-first rule is violated. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**3. Neon (Ne, 10 electrons): 2-9.**

- **Sum of digits:** 2 + 9 = 11. **This does not equal neon's atomic number (10).**
- **Verdict:** **Impossible** — on two counts.
- **Reason:** First, the digits add to 11, not 10, so this is not a valid configuration of a neutral neon atom at all. Second, even setting the count aside, shell 2 cannot hold 9 electrons — its maximum is 8. Either reason alone makes 2-9 impossible for neon.

*Rubric:* 1 point for identifying it as impossible; 1 point for a correct reason — EITHER the digits do not sum to 10 OR shell 2 exceeds its capacity of 8 (full credit for either; both is ideal). A student who calls this an "excited state" missed that an excited state must still have the right total and obey shell limits. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Magnesium (Mg, 12 electrons): 2-8-2 vs. 2-7-3.**

- **Ground state: 2-8-2.** **Excited state: 2-7-3.**
- **How I know:** Both add up to 12 (2+8+2 = 12 and 2+7+3 = 12), so both are magnesium. In 2-8-2 the lower shells are filled first (shell 1 = 2, shell 2 = 8, then shell 3 = 2) — that is the ground state and matches the reference tables. In 2-7-3 shell 2 holds only 7 while shell 3 holds 3; an electron has jumped up from shell 2, leaving a gap, so 2-7-3 is the higher-energy excited state.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correctly labeling 2-8-2 as ground and 2-7-3 as excited; 1 point for a justification that uses the lowest-shell-first rule (shell 2 not full in the excited case) and notes both totals equal 12. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**(b) A valid excited-state configuration for fluorine (F, 9 electrons); ground state is 2-7.**

- **Accept any of:** **1-8** (an electron jumped from shell 1 up to shell 2, but note shell 2's max is 8, so 1-8 is valid and totals 9) or, more commonly, **2-6-1** (an electron jumped from shell 2 up to shell 3). Both total 9 and break the lowest-shell-first rule, so both are valid excited states.
- **Why it is valid:** the digits add to 9 (still fluorine), and no shell exceeds its limit (shell 1 ≤ 2, shell 2 ≤ 8, shell 3 ≤ 8). The configuration is not the ground state because a lower shell has a gap while a higher shell is occupied.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for any configuration whose digits sum to 9 with no shell over its capacity; 1 point for it being a genuine excited state (a lower shell left with a gap while a higher shell is occupied, i.e., NOT the ground-state 2-7). Common acceptable answers: 2-6-1, 1-8, 2-5-2. Reject 2-7 (that is the ground state) and reject anything not summing to 9 (e.g., 2-8 is neon, not excited fluorine). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**(c) Why a falling electron produces a flash of colored light.**

*Full-credit model answer:* When an electron falls from an excited (higher) shell back down to the ground state, it releases the energy it had absorbed as a photon of light; because the size of that energy gap is fixed for a given element, the photon has a fixed energy and therefore a specific color.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that states the electron *releases* (not absorbs) energy as it falls, and connects that released energy/photon to the color of light, earns full credit. Responses that say only "the electron moves and makes light" without indicating that energy is *released on the way down* should receive partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for identifying that energy is RELEASED (as a photon) when the electron falls back down; 1 point for connecting the released energy to the color/light observed. A response describing energy being absorbed (the upward jump) instead of released has the direction backward — partial credit and feedback. NYSSLS: HS-PS4-3 / HS-PS1-1.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the flame test or the fireworks and how the *color* connected to an electron falling back to the ground state — students who link the visible color to the invisible electron transition are showing that the Energy-and-Matter chain landed, not just the vocabulary.
- Responses that correctly use the phrase "same atom, different arrangement" or note that an excited state has the same number of electrons as the ground state — these students have cleared the most common misconception (that an excited state is a different element).
- Students who name a partner or a moment at the vertical surface ("when my group counted the electrons on Card B") are signaling that the BTC group structure supported their learning — note this as evidence the random-groups/vertical-surface format is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows a configuration that does not sum to the atomic number, or a shell over its capacity, the error is in applying the two checking rules — a brief one-on-one at the start of the next lesson (re-run the two checks on one card together) should be sufficient. If a student writes that energy is *released* on the upward jump, reverse the direction explicitly: energy in going up, energy out (as a photon) coming down.
