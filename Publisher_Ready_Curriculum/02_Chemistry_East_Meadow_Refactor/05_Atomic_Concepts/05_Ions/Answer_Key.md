# Ions: Why Sodium Reacts but Sodium Ion Sits Still — Answer Key

## Cover

Unit: Atomic Concepts — Lesson 05: Ions
East Meadow Schools × Valley Stream Central High School District
NYSSLS tag: HS-PS1-1 (number and charge of stable ions that form from atoms in a periodic-table group)

## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Potassium (K), Group 1:**

- Valence electrons: **1**
- Lose or gain: **lose 1 electron** (one valence electron is far easier to lose than to gain seven)
- Ion symbol and charge: **K⁺**

Step-by-step: potassium is in Group 1, so it has 1 valence electron. Losing that single electron leaves 19 protons and 18 electrons → net charge +1 → K⁺, a cation. It is in the same group as sodium, so it forms the same 1+ charge — the Patterns CCC in action.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ion (K⁺); 1 point for stating "lose 1 electron" with the correct reasoning (Group 1 → 1 valence electron). A student who writes K⁻ reversed the sign — provide feedback: losing a negative electron leaves a net positive. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**2. Calcium (Ca), Group 2:**

- Valence electrons: **2**
- Lose or gain: **lose 2 electrons** (losing 2 is the shorter path than gaining 6)
- Ion symbol and charge: **Ca²⁺**

Step-by-step: calcium is in Group 2, so it has 2 valence electrons. Losing both leaves 20 protons and 18 electrons → net charge +2 → Ca²⁺, a cation. The charge magnitude (2) matches the number of electrons lost.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ion (Ca²⁺); 1 point for "lose 2 electrons" with reasoning. A common error is Ca⁺ (correct sign, wrong magnitude) — note that both valence electrons are lost, so the charge is 2+. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**3. Oxygen (O), Group 16:**

- Valence electrons: **6**
- Lose or gain: **gain 2 electrons** (gaining 2 to reach 8 is far easier than losing 6)
- Ion symbol and charge: **O²⁻**

Step-by-step: oxygen is in Group 16, so it has 6 valence electrons. Gaining 2 fills the outer shell to 8, giving 8 protons and 10 electrons → net charge −2 → O²⁻, an anion. A frequent error is O⁶⁻ or O⁶⁺ (using the group number as the charge) — the charge comes from the *shortest path to a full shell* (8 − 6 = 2 gained), not from the group number itself.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ion (O²⁻); 1 point for "gain 2 electrons" with reasoning. Partial credit for correct sign but wrong magnitude. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Magnesium (Mg), Group 2:**

- Valence electrons: **2**
- Magnesium **loses 2 electrons** (the shorter path to a full shell than gaining 6).
- Ion: **Mg²⁺** (cation)

Magnesium is in Group 2, so it has 2 valence electrons. Losing both leaves 12 protons and 10 electrons → net charge +2 → Mg²⁺. Mg is *not* one of the worksheet practice elements, so students must apply the Group-2 pattern rather than recall a memorized answer (it forms the same charge as calcium, also Group 2).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ion (Mg²⁺); 1 point for "lose 2 electrons" with reasoning tied to Group 2. Accept Mg⁺² notation. A student who writes Mg²⁻ reversed the sign; Mg⁺ has the wrong magnitude. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**(b) Sulfur (S), Group 16:**

- Valence electrons: **6**
- Sulfur **gains 2 electrons** (gaining 2 to reach 8 is far easier than losing 6).
- Ion: **S²⁻** (anion)

Sulfur is in Group 16, so it has 6 valence electrons. Gaining 2 fills the shell to 8 → 16 protons and 18 electrons → net charge −2 → S²⁻. Sulfur is *not* a worksheet practice element; students apply the Group-16 pattern (it forms the same charge as oxygen, also Group 16).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ion (S²⁻); 1 point for "gain 2 electrons" with reasoning. A student who writes S⁶⁻ used the group number as the charge — redirect to the shortest-path rule (8 − 6 = 2). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**(c) Why Na⁺ in table salt does not react with water like sodium metal:**

*Full-credit model answer:* The sodium in table salt is a sodium **ion** (Na⁺) — it has already lost its single reactive outer electron and reached a stable full outer shell, so unlike sodium metal it has no loosely held electron left to give away and does not react with water.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that uses the word *ion* and explains that Na⁺ has already lost its outer electron and reached a stable/full outer shell (so it has nothing left to react) earns full credit. Responses that say only "the salt is safe" or "it's already reacted" without naming the lost electron or the full shell receive partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for correctly using the term *ion* and identifying Na⁺ as the form in salt; 1 point for explaining the cause (lost outer electron → full stable shell → nothing left to react). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the single electron as "the whole story" — students who name the one-electron change as the difference between an explosive metal and a safe seasoning are showing that the phenomenon resolved for them.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("an ion — an atom that gained or lost electrons — ...") are demonstrating the Hochman writing move from Phase 3 and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary into active sentence structure.
- Students who name a partner who helped them catch a sign error (lose → +, gain → −) are signaling that the group investigation structure supported their learning — note this as evidence the collaborative table format is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows correct ion magnitudes but wrong signs (e.g., Mg²⁻ instead of Mg²⁺, or S²⁺ instead of S²⁻), the error is in the sign rule, not the periodic-table lookup — a brief check-in on "lose → positive, gain → negative" at the start of the next lesson should be sufficient. If a student uses the group number directly as the charge (e.g., O⁶⁻, S⁶⁻), reteach the shortest-path rule (charge magnitude for nonmetals = 8 − valence electrons) before the bonding unit builds on ion charges.
