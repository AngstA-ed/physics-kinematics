# Isotopes & Average Atomic Mass — Answer Key

## Cover

Unit: Atomic Concepts — Lesson 04: Isotopes & Average Atomic Mass
East Meadow Schools × Valley Stream Central High School District
NYSSLS tag: HS-PS1-8 (front end) — qualitative; average atomic mass is interpreted, not calculated.

## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Are they isotopes? (Atom X and Atom Y)**

(a) **Element:** boron. An atom with 5 protons has atomic number 5, which is boron (B) on the Periodic Table.

(b) **Yes — X and Y are isotopes of the same element.** Both have 5 protons (so both are boron), but X has 5 neutrons and Y has 6 neutrons. Same proton count, different neutron count — that is the definition of isotopes.

(c) **Mass numbers:** Atom X = 5 + 5 = **10** (boron-10). Atom Y = 5 + 6 = **11** (boron-11).

*Rubric:* 1 point for identifying boron from the proton count; 1 point for correctly stating they ARE isotopes with the proton-same / neutron-different reasoning; 1 point for both mass numbers (10 and 11). A student who says "different elements" has confused neutrons with protons — provide feedback: the element is set by the proton count, which is identical here. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-8.

**2. Boron's average atomic mass (10.81)**

(a) 10.81 is **closer to 11**.

(b) **Boron-11 is the more abundant isotope.** The average atomic mass leans toward whichever isotope is most abundant; since 10.81 sits much closer to 11 than to 10, boron-11 must make up the larger share of natural boron. (For reference only — students are not required to know it — boron is about 80% boron-11 and 20% boron-10.)

*Rubric:* 1 point for "closer to 11"; 1 point for naming boron-11 as more abundant AND citing the "average leans toward the most abundant isotope" reasoning. A correct direction without reasoning earns partial credit. No calculation is required or expected. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-8.

**3. One average, many atoms**

(a) **No** — no single carbon atom weighs 12.011 u.

(b) Each individual carbon atom is a whole-numbered **isotope** — its mass number is 12, 13, or 14, never 12.011. The 12.011 is a *weighted average* across the whole population of carbon atoms, and because carbon-12 is by far the most **abundant** isotope, the average lands just above 12. The average describes the mixture, not any one atom — exactly like the average pennium mass, which no single penny in the cup actually has.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "no"; 1 point for an explanation that uses *isotope* (atoms come in whole-numbered versions) AND *abundant* (the average is pulled toward the most common isotope). Reference to the pennium analogy is a bonus, not required. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-8.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Are Atom 1 and Atom 2 isotopes?**

| | Protons | Neutrons | Mass number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atom 1 | 17 | 18 | 35 |
| Atom 2 | 17 | 20 | 37 |

**Yes — they are isotopes of the same element.** The element is **chlorine** (17 protons = atomic number 17 = Cl). Both have 17 protons, so both are chlorine; Atom 1 has 18 neutrons (mass number 17 + 18 = **35**, chlorine-35) and Atom 2 has 20 neutrons (mass number 17 + 20 = **37**, chlorine-37). Same protons, different neutrons → isotopes.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for "yes, isotopes" with the same-protons / different-neutrons reasoning and naming chlorine; 1 point for both correct mass numbers (35 and 37). Partial credit (1 point) for correct reasoning but one arithmetic slip in a mass number. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-8.

**(b) Which chlorine isotope is more abundant?**

**Chlorine-35 is more abundant.** Chlorine's average atomic mass (35.5) is closer to 35 than to 37, and the average always leans toward the most abundant isotope — so chlorine-35 must make up the larger share. (For reference only: chlorine is about 75% chlorine-35 and 25% chlorine-37. Students are NOT required to produce these percentages — only the direction.)

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for naming chlorine-35; 1 point for the reasoning that the average leans toward the most abundant isotope and 35.5 is closer to 35. A bare "chlorine-35" with no reasoning earns 1 point. Any attempt to compute an exact percentage is acceptable but not required. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-8.

**(c) Why radiocarbon dating depends on an unstable isotope:**

*Full-credit model answer:* Radiocarbon dating works because carbon-14 is an **unstable** isotope that **decays** at a fixed, known rate, while carbon-12 is **stable** and never decays; by measuring how much of the unstable carbon-14 is left in the shell, scientists can tell how long ago the turtle died.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that identifies carbon-14 as the unstable/radioactive isotope that decays over time (the "clock"), and contrasts it with stable carbon-12 (or simply notes that a stable isotope could not keep time), earns full credit. Responses that say only "they measure the carbon" without naming the unstable isotope or the idea of decay earn partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for identifying carbon-14 as the unstable isotope that decays; 1 point for connecting the decay (or the stable/unstable contrast) to telling time / age. Use of the words *stable*, *unstable*, or *decay* is expected. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-8.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A specific "aha" about the *same-but-different* idea — for example, "I didn't know two atoms could both be carbon but weigh different amounts" — rather than a generic "it was interesting." Students who articulate the proton-same / neutron-different distinction in their own words are showing the core concept landed.
- Any mention of the pennium activity (sorting, counting, the lopsided cup) as the moment the abundance → average idea clicked — this signals the ACTIVE LEARNING structure did its job.
- Naming a partner who helped them sort out protons vs. neutrons, or who caught a mass-number error, signals the collaborative table format supported learning.

If a student writes that they "still don't get why the average isn't just the middle," that is a high-value flag: a 30-second check at the start of the next lesson — "do you have *more* of one isotope?" — usually resolves it, since the weighting is the one piece most easily missed. If a student's Exit Ticket confuses protons and neutrons (e.g., calls Atom 1 and Atom 2 "different elements"), revisit the rule that the proton count alone sets the element before the next lesson builds on isotope notation.
