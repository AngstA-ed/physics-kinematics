## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Solubility of KNO₃ (solid) at 40 °C:**

Reading the curve: find 40 °C on the x-axis, trace up to the KNO₃ (purple, rising) curve, read across to the y-axis. **≈ 64 g per 100 g of water** (accept 60–68 g — graph reading). Method credit: student must describe the x-axis → curve → y-axis trace.

*Rubric:* 1 point for a value in the 60–68 g range; 1 point for a correct description of how the value was read. A student who reads the *gas* curve at 40 °C (≈ 34 g) read the wrong curve — point them to the rising purple curve. NYSSLS: SEP-4.

**2. Solubility of NH₃ (gas) at 60 °C:**

Reading the curve: find 60 °C on the x-axis, trace up to the NH₃ (blue, falling) curve, read across. **≈ 20 g per 100 g of water** (accept 16–24 g). Note the contrast with item 1: at the same kind of lookup, the gas value is small and the gas curve is dropping.

*Rubric:* 1 point for a value in the 16–24 g range; 1 point for reading the correct (falling) curve. A student who gives a large value (50+ g) likely read KNO₃ — redirect to the blue curve. NYSSLS: SEP-4.

**3. 40 g of KNO₃ in 100 g of water at 20 °C (curve value ≈ 32 g):**

- **Saturated / unsaturated / excess:** the solution is **saturated, with excess solute undissolved** — 40 g is *above* the 32 g maximum at 20 °C.
- **Grams that cannot dissolve:** about **40 − 32 = 8 g** stay undissolved at the bottom.
- **What to do without removing water:** **raise the temperature** — heating the water raises the KNO₃ curve (a solid), so the 8 g excess can dissolve once the curve value rises above 40 g (around 25–30 °C and beyond).

*Rubric:* 1 point for "saturated / excess undissolved"; 1 point for ≈ 8 g (accept 6–10 g given graph reading); 1 point for "heat the water" (raise temperature). A student who says "add more water" did not follow the constraint — note the *without removing water* condition. NYSSLS: SEP-4.

**4. Soda warming from refrigerator to warm-room temperature:**

The water holds **LESS** dissolved carbon dioxide when warm. Carbon dioxide is a **gas**, and on Reference Table G gas curves go **down** as temperature rises — so warm water has a lower maximum for dissolved gas. The excess CO₂ escapes, which is why the warm soda goes flat faster.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "LESS"; 1 point for citing the falling gas curve / temperature-solubility relationship for gases as the reason. A student who says "more" likely applied the solid rule to a gas — clarify that gases behave oppositely to solids. NYSSLS: SEP-4.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Solubility of KNO₃ at 80 °C; is 150 g in 100 g water at 80 °C saturated?**

Reading the curve: at 80 °C the KNO₃ (rising) curve reads **≈ 167 g per 100 g of water** (accept 160–175 g). Since **150 g is BELOW the ≈ 167 g maximum**, the solution is **unsaturated** — all 150 g dissolves and the water could still hold more (about 17 g more) at 80 °C.

*Rubric:* 1 point for a value in the 160–175 g range; 1 point for "unsaturated" with the comparison (150 g < curve value). A student who answers "saturated" or "excess" misread the curve as lower than 150 g — have them re-trace 80 °C up to the KNO₃ curve. NYSSLS: SEP-4.

**(b) Carbonated water moved from the refrigerator to a warm room:**

The water now holds **LESS** dissolved carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is a gas; by the temperature-solubility relationship, **gas solubility decreases as temperature increases** (gas curves on Table G fall as temperature rises). Warm water cannot hold as much dissolved CO₂, so the excess gas comes out of solution.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "LESS"; 1 point for correctly invoking the gas part of the temperature-solubility relationship (gas solubility decreases with temperature). NYSSLS: SEP-4.

**(c) How slope tells you solid vs. gas:**

*Full-credit model answer:* A curve that **rises** (slopes upward) as temperature increases is a **solid** — it dissolves more in hot water; a curve that **falls** (slopes downward) as temperature increases is a **gas** — it dissolves less in hot water.

*Acceptable variations:* any response stating that an upward/rising slope = solid (more dissolves when hot) and a downward/falling slope = gas (less dissolves when hot). Responses that only say "the slope tells you" without naming the up = solid / down = gas direction earn partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for rising = solid, 1 point for falling = gas. Connecting the direction to "more / less dissolves when heated" is full-credit reasoning. NYSSLS: SEP-4.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A specific reading move that clicked — "I finally got that you trace *up* to the curve, then *across*" — rather than a generic "I learned about solubility." Students who can name the three-step reading method have the procedural skill.
- Any connection back to the soda phenomenon and the falling gas curve — students who tie the arithmetic of the graph to the flat-soda experience are showing that the table is real, not abstract.
- Naming a group member who helped them read a curve or catch a wrong-curve error signals that the BTC vertical-surface structure supported their learning — note this as evidence the random-grouping practice is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows the value read off the *wrong* curve (solid value where a gas was asked, or vice versa), the error is in identifying the curve by slope, not in the lookup mechanics — a brief one-on-one at the start of the next lesson, pointing to the rising-vs-falling distinction, should be sufficient. If the trace itself is off (reading the wrong axis), reteach the x-axis → curve → y-axis method before the next Table G task.
