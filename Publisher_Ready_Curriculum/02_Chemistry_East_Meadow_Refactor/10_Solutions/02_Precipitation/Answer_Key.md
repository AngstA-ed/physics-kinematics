## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + KI(aq):**

- **Four ions present:** Pb²⁺, NO₃⁻, K⁺, I⁻.
- **Two new pairings (swap partners):** KNO₃ (K⁺ with NO₃⁻) and PbI₂ (Pb²⁺ with I⁻).
- **Soluble or insoluble:** KNO₃ is soluble (potassium is a Group 1 metal; nitrates are soluble). PbI₂ is insoluble (iodides are soluble *except* with Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ — lead is on the except list).
- **Precipitate:** PbI₂ (lead(II) iodide), the bright yellow solid. K⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectator ions and stay dissolved.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correctly swapping partners to get KNO₃ and PbI₂; 1 point for naming PbI₂ as the insoluble precipitate using the iodide-except rule. A student who names KNO₃ as the precipitate has reversed the soluble/insoluble logic — point them to the iodide rule. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

**2. Na₂CO₃(aq) + CaCl₂(aq):**

- **Four ions present:** Na⁺, CO₃²⁻, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻.
- **Two new pairings (swap partners):** NaCl (Na⁺ with Cl⁻) and CaCO₃ (Ca²⁺ with CO₃²⁻).
- **Soluble or insoluble:** NaCl is soluble (sodium is Group 1; this chloride is not on the except list). CaCO₃ is insoluble (carbonates are insoluble *except* with Group 1 and NH₄⁺ — calcium is neither).
- **Precipitate:** CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate), a white solid. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectators.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the swap to NaCl and CaCO₃; 1 point for identifying CaCO₃ as insoluble via the carbonate rule. Common error: assuming "anything with a metal precipitates" — redirect to the rule that Group 1 and ammonium carbonates *are* soluble. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

**3. NaCl(aq) + KNO₃(aq):**

- **Four ions present:** Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺, NO₃⁻.
- **Two new pairings (swap partners):** NaNO₃ (Na⁺ with NO₃⁻) and KCl (K⁺ with Cl⁻).
- **Soluble or insoluble:** NaNO₃ is soluble (Group 1 metal; nitrate). KCl is soluble (Group 1 metal; chloride not on the except list).
- **Precipitate:** none — both new pairings are soluble, so the mixture stays clear.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the swap to NaNO₃ and KCl; 1 point for concluding "no precipitate" because both are soluble. A student who invents a precipitate here is not checking both pairings against the rules — full credit requires explicitly stating both are soluble. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

**Part 3 — Reading the solubility curve (Table G):**

- (a) At 60 °C, KNO₃ ≈ **110 g** per 100 g of water (accept 105–115 g). 
- (b) At 60 °C, NaCl ≈ **37 g** per 100 g of water (accept 36–38 g). 
- (c) Sample full-credit response: *"Both curves are above zero, so both compounds dissolve and count as soluble — but the KNO₃ curve is far higher than the NaCl curve at 60 °C (about 110 g versus about 37 g), so 'soluble' tells you a compound dissolves, not how much."* Any answer that uses the two curve heights to show soluble compounds dissolve in different amounts earns full credit.

*Rubric:* 1 point each for the two readings within tolerance; 1 point for a sentence that contrasts the curve heights to explain that "soluble" is not a fixed amount. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Classify each compound (solubility-guidelines table):**

- (i) **BaSO₄ (barium sulfate): insoluble.** Sulfates are soluble *except* with a short list of cations that includes Ba²⁺ (also Pb²⁺, Sr²⁺; Ca²⁺ is slightly soluble). Barium sulfate is the classic insoluble sulfate.
- (ii) **(NH₄)₂CO₃ (ammonium carbonate): soluble.** Carbonates are insoluble *except* with Group 1 metals and ammonium (NH₄⁺). Because the cation is ammonium, this carbonate is soluble.

*Rubric:* 1 point each. For BaSO₄, the student must use the sulfate-except rule (not guess); for (NH₄)₂CO₃, they must apply the ammonium exception to the carbonate rule. A student who marks (NH₄)₂CO₃ insoluble has missed the ammonium exception — provide written feedback on the "except Group 1 and NH₄⁺" clause. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

**(b) AgNO₃(aq) + KCl(aq):**

- **Two new pairings (swap partners):** AgCl (Ag⁺ with Cl⁻) and KNO₃ (K⁺ with NO₃⁻).
- **AgCl is insoluble** (chlorides are soluble *except* with Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ — silver is on the except list). **KNO₃ is soluble** (Group 1 metal; nitrate).
- **A precipitate forms: AgCl (silver chloride), a white solid.** K⁺ and NO₃⁻ are spectator ions.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for swapping partners to get AgCl and KNO₃; 1 point for naming AgCl as the precipitate via the chloride-except rule. Partial credit (1 point) for a correct swap with the wrong solubility conclusion. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

**(c) KNO₃ solubility at 40 °C from the Table G curve:**

- About **64 g** of KNO₃ per 100 g of water (accept 60–68 g).

*Rubric:* 1 point for a reading within tolerance. A reading near the NaCl curve (~37 g) means the student read the wrong curve — remind them to follow the steeply rising purple KNO₃ curve, not the flat blue NaCl curve. NYSSLS: SEP-2 / Patterns.

*Note on transfer:* the Exit Ticket deliberately uses different compounds (BaSO₄, ammonium carbonate, the AgNO₃ + KCl pair) and a different temperature (40 °C) than the worksheet practice (PbI₂, CaCO₃, AgCl + NaCl; 60 °C reading). A student who succeeds here is applying the *rules and the curve*, not recalling a memorized worksheet answer.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the moment the pattern "clicked" in the Javalab data — students who name the realization that Group 1 ions and nitrates always stay dissolved are showing they extracted the pattern, not just memorized cases.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("a precipitate — an insoluble solid — forms when…") demonstrate the Hochman writing move from Phase 3 and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary into active sentence structure.
- Students who name a partner who helped them catch a swap error (e.g., checking *both* new compounds, not just one) are signaling that the collaborative investigation supported their learning — evidence the partner structure is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows a correct swap but the wrong soluble/insoluble call, the gap is in *reading the table*, not the method — a brief table-reading check at the start of the next lesson should be sufficient. If a student does not swap partners at all (pairs each solution with itself), reteach the swap step before the next lesson builds on double-replacement reactions.
