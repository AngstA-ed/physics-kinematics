## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. FeSCN²⁺ equilibrium — add more SCN⁻:**

- **Shift direction:** toward products (right, →).
- **Justification:** SCN⁻ is a reactant. Adding more of it stresses the reactant side, so the system shifts to *use up* the added SCN⁻ by making more FeSCN²⁺ — a shift toward products. This is Le Châtelier's principle: the system moves in the direction that partially relieves the stress.
- **Predicted color change:** the solution deepens in red (more deep-red FeSCN²⁺). · *Rubric:* 1 point for "toward products / right"; 1 point for a justification that names SCN⁻ as a reactant and says the system uses up the added reactant; the deepening-red prediction is the evidence check. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

**2. Haber equilibrium — increase the pressure:**

- **Shift direction:** toward products (right, → toward 2 NH₃).
- **Justification:** Count the gas molecules. The reactant side has 1 N₂ + 3 H₂ = **4** gas molecules; the product side has **2** NH₃. Increasing the pressure (by decreasing the volume) stresses the system, which relieves the stress by shifting toward the side with *fewer* gas molecules — the 2-molecule product side. This matches the rising-yield curve in `ammonia_yield_pressure.png`. · *Rubric:* 1 point for "toward products / right"; 1 point for correctly counting 4 gas molecules vs. 2 and stating "fewer gas molecules relieves a pressure increase." A student who answers "products" without the molecule count earns partial credit. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

**3. Haber equilibrium — raise the temperature:**

- **Shift direction:** toward reactants (left, ←) — the ammonia yield *decreases*.
- **Justification:** ΔH = −92 kJ means the forward reaction is **exothermic**, so heat behaves like a **product** (it is released as ammonia forms). Raising the temperature adds heat, which stresses the product side, so the system shifts *back toward reactants* to use up the added heat — lowering the NH₃ yield. (This is why the cooler 400 °C curve sits above the 500 °C curve.) · *Rubric:* 1 point for "toward reactants / left / lower yield"; 1 point for correctly treating heat as a *product* because the forward reaction is exothermic (ΔH negative). A student who treats heat as a reactant and answers "toward products" has the classic exo/endo error — flag it. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

**4. Haber equilibrium — add an iron catalyst:**

- **Effect on the equilibrium position:** **no shift** — the position (and the yield) is unchanged.
- **Justification:** A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward *and* reverse reactions by the same amount, so it speeds both directions equally. The system reaches equilibrium *faster*, but at the *same* position. A catalyst is not a stress; it changes the rate, not the yield. · *Rubric:* 1 point for "no shift / position unchanged"; 1 point for the reason that a catalyst speeds the forward and reverse equally (rate, not position). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

## Exit Ticket Answer

Reaction: **2 SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2 SO₃(g),   ΔH = −198 kJ** (forward reaction exothermic). This is a *different* reaction from the worksheet's FeSCN²⁺ and Haber problems, so students must transfer the reasoning.

**(a) Increase the pressure → shift toward products (right, → more SO₃).**

Count the gas molecules: reactant side = 2 SO₂ + 1 O₂ = **3** gas molecules; product side = **2** SO₃. Increasing the pressure shifts the equilibrium toward the side with *fewer* gas molecules — the 2-molecule product side — to relieve the squeeze. · *Rubric:* 1 point for "toward products / right"; 1 point for the 3-vs-2 gas-molecule count. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

**(b) Raise the temperature → shift toward reactants (left, ← less SO₃).**

ΔH = −198 kJ means the forward reaction is exothermic, so heat is effectively a **product**. Raising the temperature adds heat to the product side, so the system shifts *back toward reactants* to consume the added heat — decreasing the SO₃ yield. · *Rubric:* 1 point for "toward reactants / left"; 1 point for treating heat as a product because the reaction is exothermic. A "toward products" answer is the exo/endo error — partial credit only if some valid reasoning is shown. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

**(c) Add a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst → the equilibrium position does NOT change.**

The catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward and reverse reactions equally, so the reaction reaches equilibrium *faster* but at the *same* position — the SO₃ yield is unchanged. A catalyst is not a stress; it affects rate, not the equilibrium position. · *Rubric:* 1 point for "no change to the position/yield"; 1 point for the reason (speeds forward and reverse equally → rate, not position). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-6.

*Tolerance/rubric note:* The full Exit Ticket is worth 6 points (2 per part). The single most important discriminator is part (b): students who get the *direction* of a temperature shift correct have internalized "heat as a substance whose side depends on the sign of ΔH," which is the conceptual core of HS-PS1-6 for thermochemical equilibria.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A specific moment named — a color change in the FeSCN²⁺ tubes, the gap between the 400 °C and 500 °C yield curves, or a classmate's board argument — rather than a generic "it made sense." Students who anchor the abstract rule to a concrete observation are showing real transfer.
- Responses that correctly use "stress" and "shift" together ("when I added Fe³⁺ that was a *stress*, and the equilibrium *shifted* to make more red") demonstrate the Phase 3 vocabulary is now active.
- Any mention of a peer at the vertical surface (a BTC group member who caught a molecule-count error or argued a shift direction) signals the random-grouping structure supported the learning — note these students as evidence the collaborative practice is working.

If a student's worksheet shows the temperature error (treating an exothermic reaction's heat as a reactant, predicting "toward products" when the temperature rises) or the catalyst error (predicting a catalyst raises the yield), schedule a brief, low-stakes opener check next lesson — these are the two predictable conceptual traps for Le Châtelier and are worth re-teaching before the unit assessment.
