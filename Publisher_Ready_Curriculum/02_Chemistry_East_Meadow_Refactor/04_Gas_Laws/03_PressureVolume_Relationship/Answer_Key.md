## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Compress 50.0 mL → 25.0 mL at 100. kPa; find P₂.**

> P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → (100. kPa)(50.0 mL) = P₂(25.0 mL)
> P₂ = (100. × 50.0) ÷ 25.0 = 5000 ÷ 25.0 = **200. kPa**

Step-by-step: the product before the change is 100. × 50.0 = 5000 (kPa·mL); dividing by the new volume 25.0 mL gives 200. kPa. Proportional check: the volume was cut in half, so the pressure doubled (100. → 200.) — the arithmetic and the inverse proportion agree.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct substitution into P₁V₁ = P₂V₂; 1 point for the correct answer (200. kPa) with the unit. A student who gets 50 kPa likely divided in the wrong direction (multiplied volume up instead of solving) — point them to the proportional check: smaller volume must give *larger* pressure. NYSSLS: Boyle's Law / SEP Using Mathematics.

**2. Expand 4.0 L → 8.0 L at 2.0 atm; find P₂.**

> P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → (2.0 atm)(4.0 L) = P₂(8.0 L)
> P₂ = (2.0 × 4.0) ÷ 8.0 = 8.0 ÷ 8.0 = **1.0 atm**

Step-by-step: product before = 2.0 × 4.0 = 8.0 (atm·L); dividing by the new volume 8.0 L gives 1.0 atm. Proportional check: the volume doubled, so the pressure was halved (2.0 → 1.0) — consistent with the inverse relationship.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct substitution; 1 point for the correct answer (1.0 atm) with the unit. A student who gets 4.0 atm has the direction reversed (treated it as direct) — redirect to the syringe: bigger volume means lower pressure. NYSSLS: Boyle's Law / SEP Using Mathematics.

**3. Compress 6.0 L until pressure rises 90. kPa → 270 kPa; find V₂.**

> P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → (90. kPa)(6.0 L) = (270 kPa)V₂
> V₂ = (90. × 6.0) ÷ 270 = 540 ÷ 270 = **2.0 L**

Step-by-step: product before = 90. × 6.0 = 540 (kPa·L); dividing by the new pressure 270 kPa gives 2.0 L. Proportional check: the pressure tripled (90. → 270), so the volume was divided by three (6.0 → 2.0) — consistent with the inverse relationship.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct substitution and solving for V₂; 1 point for the correct answer (2.0 L) with the unit. A student who gets 18 L multiplied instead of dividing — note that compressing must *decrease* the volume. NYSSLS: Boyle's Law / SEP Using Mathematics.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Compress 8.0 L → 3.0 L at 1.5 atm; find P₂.**

> P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → (1.5 atm)(8.0 L) = P₂(3.0 L)
> P₂ = (1.5 × 8.0) ÷ 3.0 = 12 ÷ 3.0 = **4.0 atm**

Product before = 1.5 × 8.0 = 12 (atm·L); dividing by the new volume 3.0 L gives 4.0 atm. Proportional check: volume dropped to about three-eighths of its starting value, so the pressure rose by the reciprocal factor (× 8/3), 1.5 → 4.0 atm.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct substitution into P₁V₁ = P₂V₂; 1 point for the correct answer (4.0 atm) with the unit. A smaller-than-1.5 answer signals a direction error (smaller volume must raise pressure). NYSSLS: Boyle's Law / SEP Using Mathematics.

**(b) Pressure drops 200. kPa → 50. kPa in a 2.0 L container; find V₂.**

> P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ → (200. kPa)(2.0 L) = (50. kPa)V₂
> V₂ = (200. × 2.0) ÷ 50. = 400 ÷ 50. = **8.0 L**

Product before = 200. × 2.0 = 400 (kPa·L); dividing by the new pressure 50. kPa gives 8.0 L. Proportional check: the pressure dropped to one-quarter (200. → 50.), so the volume grew four-fold (2.0 → 8.0) — consistent with the inverse relationship.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct substitution and solving for V₂; 1 point for the correct answer (8.0 L) with the unit. A student who gets 0.5 L inverted the operation — note that falling pressure means *expanding* volume. NYSSLS: Boyle's Law / SEP Using Mathematics.

**(c) Particle-model sentence — why decreasing volume increases pressure:**

*Full-credit model answer:* When the volume decreases, the same number of gas particles is packed into less space, so the particles collide with the container walls more often per second — more collisions per unit area means greater pressure.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that ties higher pressure to *more frequent collisions with the walls* caused by the same number of particles in a smaller space earns full credit. Responses that say only "it gets more crowded" or "the pressure goes up" without naming collisions with the walls receive partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for stating that the same number of particles occupies a smaller space; 1 point for connecting that to more frequent wall collisions (and therefore higher pressure). NYSSLS: Boyle's Law / SEP Analyzing Data.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of *feeling* the syringe resistance grow and connecting it to the rising-pressure data — students who link the sensation in their hand to the numbers on the board are showing that the inverse relationship is real, not abstract.
- Responses that use the proportional language ("when I cut the volume in half the pressure doubled") demonstrate that the Scale, Proportion, and Quantity lens landed.
- Students who name a groupmate who helped them spot the constant product (P × V) are signaling that the BTC vertical-surface structure supported their reasoning — note this as evidence the strategy is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows pressure and volume moving in the *same* direction (e.g., compresses the gas but reports a *lower* pressure), the direction of the inverse relationship has not landed — a brief one-on-one with the syringe at the start of the next lesson should resolve it. If the substitution into P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ is correct but the arithmetic (divide vs. multiply) is reversed, target the proportional check ("smaller volume → larger pressure") rather than re-teaching the whole law.
