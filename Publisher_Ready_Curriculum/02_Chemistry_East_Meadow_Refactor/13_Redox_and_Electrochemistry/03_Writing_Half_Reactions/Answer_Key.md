## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu**

> Oxidation (loses e⁻): **Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻**
> Reduction (gains e⁻): **Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu**
> Electrons transferred: **2** Net ionic equation: **Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu**

Step-by-step: Zn's oxidation number goes 0 → +2, so it loses 2 electrons (e⁻ on the product side). Cu's goes +2 → 0, so Cu²⁺ gains 2 electrons (e⁻ on the reactant side). 2 lost = 2 gained, so no scaling is needed; the electrons cancel when the halves are added, leaving the net ionic equation.

*Rubric:* 1 point for each correctly written half-reaction with e⁻ on the correct side (2 points); 1 point for stating 2 electrons transferred and a correct net ionic equation. A student who writes the reduction half as "Cu²⁺ → Cu + 2e⁻" placed the electrons on the wrong side — note that Cu²⁺ *gains* electrons, so e⁻ must be a reactant. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2 / PS1-12.

**2. Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂**

> Oxidation (loses e⁻): **Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻**
> Reduction (gains e⁻): **2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂**
> Electrons transferred: **2** Net ionic equation: **Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂**

Step-by-step: Mg goes 0 → +2, losing 2 electrons. Each H⁺ goes +1 → 0, gaining 1 electron; because two H⁺ combine to form one H₂ molecule, the reduction half takes in 2 electrons total (2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂). 2 lost = 2 gained, no scaling needed.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the oxidation half; 1 point for the reduction half with the diatomic H₂ correctly balanced (2H⁺ and 2e⁻); 1 point for 2 electrons transferred and the correct net ionic equation. A common error is "H⁺ + e⁻ → H₂" (unbalanced atoms) — note that one H₂ molecule requires two H atoms, hence 2H⁺ and 2e⁻. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2 / PS1-12.

**3. Al + Cu²⁺ → Al³⁺ + Cu** *(scale to the least common multiple)*

> Oxidation (loses e⁻), scaled: **2Al → 2Al³⁺ + 6e⁻**
> Reduction (gains e⁻), scaled: **3Cu²⁺ + 6e⁻ → 3Cu**
> Electrons transferred: **6** Net ionic equation: **2Al + 3Cu²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Cu**

Step-by-step: Before scaling, Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ (3 lost) and Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (2 gained). 3 ≠ 2, so the electrons are not yet conserved. The least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6: multiply the aluminum half by 2 (giving 6e⁻) and the copper half by 3 (giving 6e⁻). Now 6 lost = 6 gained. Adding the balanced halves and cancelling the 6 electrons gives the net ionic equation.

*Rubric:* 1 point for identifying the unequal counts (3 vs. 2) and the LCM of 6; 1 point for each correctly scaled half-reaction with *all* coefficients multiplied — atoms, ions, and electrons (2 points); 1 point for 6 electrons transferred and the correct net ionic equation. A student who multiplies only the electrons (e.g., "Al → Al³⁺ + 6e⁻") did not scale the whole half-reaction — note that the atoms and ion charges must scale too. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2 / PS1-12.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Ca + 2Ag⁺ → Ca²⁺ + 2Ag** *(equal electrons — no scaling needed)*

> Oxidation half-reaction (loses e⁻): **Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻**
> Reduction half-reaction (gains e⁻): **2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag**
> Electrons transferred: **2**

Ca goes 0 → +2, losing 2 electrons (e⁻ on the product side). Each Ag⁺ goes +1 → 0, gaining 1 electron; because the equation has two silver ions (2Ag⁺ → 2Ag), the reduction half takes in 2 electrons total. 2 lost = 2 gained, so no scaling is required. This reaction is different from the worksheet practice set, so students must apply the method rather than recall an answer.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the calcium oxidation half (Ca → Ca²⁺ + 2e⁻); 1 point for the silver reduction half correctly written for two silver ions (2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag); 1 point for stating 2 electrons transferred. A student who writes "Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag" without the coefficient of 2 has the correct single-ion form but did not match the given equation's two silver ions — accept with note if they then state the electrons balance at 2. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2 / PS1-12.

**(b) Aluminum with nickel(II) ions** *(balance the electrons by least common multiple)*

> Least common multiple of 3 and 2: **6**
> Oxidation half-reaction, scaled: **2Al → 2Al³⁺ + 6e⁻**
> Reduction half-reaction, scaled: **3Ni²⁺ + 6e⁻ → 3Ni**
> Electrons transferred: **6**

Given Al → Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ (3 lost) and Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Ni (2 gained), the counts do not match. The least common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6: multiply the aluminum half by 2 (→ 6e⁻) and the nickel half by 3 (→ 6e⁻). Now 6 lost = 6 gained. (The corresponding net ionic equation, if asked, is 2Al + 3Ni²⁺ → 2Al³⁺ + 3Ni.)

*Rubric:* 1 point for the LCM of 6; 1 point for each correctly scaled half-reaction with all coefficients multiplied (2 points); 1 point for stating 6 electrons transferred. A student who scales only one half-reaction, or multiplies only the electrons, did not balance correctly — note that both halves must end with the same electron count (6) and the whole half-reaction scales together. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-2 / PS1-12.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the voltmeter or the cell diagram connecting to the written half-reactions — students who link "the needle moved" to "2 electrons per zinc atom traveled through the wire" are showing that the abstract bookkeeping maps onto a real, measurable flow.
- Responses that name the conservation idea — electrons lost equal electrons gained — as the thing that finally clicked. This is the Energy and Matter CCC landing.
- Students who name a partner who helped them catch a side-of-the-arrow error or a scaling error are signaling that the BTC vertical-surface grouping supported their learning — note this as evidence that the random-group structure is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows electrons on the wrong side (reversing oxidation and reduction), the misconception is about *direction* of electron movement — a brief check using the oxidation-number change (up = lose = product side) at the start of the next lesson should resolve it. If a student wrote correct single-ion half-reactions but never scaled part (b), the gap is the least-common-multiple step — target that student for a one-on-one before the half-reaction balancing quiz.
