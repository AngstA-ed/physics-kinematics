## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Gram formula mass of CO₂, and moles in 88 g.**

*Question type:* mole conversion (grams → moles), Table T.

| Element | Atomic mass (g/mol) | Subscript | Mass contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 12.0 | 1 | 12.0 |
| O | 16.0 | 2 | 32.0 |
| **GFM** | | | **44.0 g/mol** |

Moles = mass ÷ GFM = 88 g ÷ 44.0 g/mol = **2.0 mol.**

Step-by-step: C contributes 12.0 × 1 = 12.0; O contributes 16.0 × 2 = 32.0; GFM = 44.0 g/mol. Then 88 ÷ 44 = 2.0 mol. The most common error is dropping the subscript 2 on oxygen (giving GFM = 28 and moles = 3.14) — flag the subscript step if seen. · *Rubric:* 1 point for correct GFM (44 g/mol) with the O × 2 shown; 1 point for the correct mole setup and answer (88 ÷ 44 = 2.0 mol). · NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7 / SEP-5.

**2. Reference-table retrieval: density of aluminum; solubility of AgCl.**

*Question type:* reference-table lookup.

- Density of aluminum = **2.70 g/cm³** — found on **Table S** (Properties of Selected Elements).
- AgCl is **insoluble** in water — found on **Table F** (Solubility Guidelines). Chlorides are generally soluble *except* those of Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, and Hg₂²⁺; AgCl falls under that exception.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct density value with Table S named; 1 point for "insoluble" with Table F named. Partial credit (1 of 2) for correct values without naming the tables. The point of the station is table fluency, so naming the table is part of full credit. · NYSSLS: SEP-5 / HS-PS1-3.

**3. Balancing: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O.**

*Question type:* balancing / conservation of mass.

Balanced: **2 H₂ + 1 O₂ → 2 H₂O.**

Check: left side has 4 H (2 × 2) and 2 O (1 × 2); right side has 4 H (2 × 2) and 2 O (2 × 1). Atoms balance, so mass is conserved. **Mass is conserved because atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction — they are only rearranged**, so the total mass of reactants equals the total mass of products (Law of Conservation of Mass).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct whole-number coefficients (2, 1, 2 — accept the leading 1 omitted); 1 point for a conservation-of-mass explanation that references atoms being rearranged, not created or destroyed. A student who writes "2 H₂ + 1.5 O₂ → 2 H₂O" has the right ratio but must clear the fraction to whole numbers for Regents credit — note this. · NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Moles in 36 g of water (H₂O):**

| Element | Atomic mass (g/mol) | Subscript | Mass contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1.0 | 2 | 2.0 |
| O | 16.0 | 1 | 16.0 |
| **GFM** | | | **18.0 g/mol** |

Moles = mass ÷ GFM = 36 g ÷ 18.0 g/mol = **2.0 mol.**

H contributes 1.0 × 2 = 2.0; O contributes 16.0 × 1 = 16.0; GFM = 18.0 g/mol. Then 36 ÷ 18 = 2.0 mol. Note these are deliberately different values from the station practice (which used CO₂ at 88 g) — a student who simply copies the station answer (44 g/mol, or 2.0 mol from 88 g) has not done the new calculation; check that the GFM shown is 18.0, not 44.0.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct GFM of H₂O (18.0 g/mol) with the H × 2 shown; 1 point for the correct mole setup and answer (36 ÷ 18 = 2.0 mol). A student who writes only "2.0 mol" with no GFM or setup earns partial credit at most — constructed-response points live in the shown work. · NYSSLS: HS-PS1-7 / SEP-5.

**(b) Is sodium nitrate (NaNO₃) soluble in water?**

NaNO₃ is **soluble** in water — found on **Table F**. Table F states that compounds containing the nitrate ion (NO₃⁻) are soluble (no exceptions), and compounds of Group 1 / alkali metal ions (including Na⁺) are also soluble. Both rules agree: NaNO₃ is soluble.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "soluble"; 1 point for naming Table F. Bonus note (not required for full credit) if the student cites the nitrate rule or the Group 1 rule by name. The station used AgCl (insoluble) — NaNO₃ here is the contrasting soluble case, so the answer is genuinely different from the practice. · NYSSLS: SEP-5 / HS-PS1-3.

**(c) Pattern sentence connecting two units:**

*Full-credit model answers (any one of these, or an equivalent defensible link):*

- "Atomic Concepts and The Periodic Table connect because the table is organized by the *pattern* of how electrons fill atoms, so an atom's position predicts its behavior."
- "Reactions and Heat & Energy connect because the same *pattern* — atoms rearranging — both conserves mass and exchanges energy with the surroundings."
- "Moles and Solutions connect because both follow the *pattern* of measuring how much substance is present, using the reference tables either way."

*Acceptable variations:* any sentence that (1) names two units of the course, (2) uses the word "pattern" (or clearly describes a repeated structure), and (3) ties the link to matter or energy. Reject sentences that only restate a single unit or list facts without a connection. · *Rubric:* 2 points — 1 for naming two distinct units, 1 for a valid matter-or-energy connection using "pattern." · NYSSLS: SEP-2 (models) / Patterns CCC.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A *named* topic that feels less scary now and a *named* helper (a partner, a specific station) — this signals the active-learning station structure reduced anxiety and built peer support, the dual aim of the opening restorative circle.
- A concrete review plan for tonight ("I'll re-drill Table F solubility") rather than a vague "I'll study" — specificity predicts follow-through.
- Any response that connects a station back to the concept map or to the "one story" framing shows the Pattern CCC landed.

Use the flagged "still shaky" topics from the circle and the reflection to re-weight the practice Regents the next day. If many students flag the same table (commonly Table F solubility), open the next period with a 3-minute table-fluency drill before the practice exam. A student who writes "everything is still scary" should get a brief, supportive one-on-one and a focused two-topic study list — not a generic reassurance.
