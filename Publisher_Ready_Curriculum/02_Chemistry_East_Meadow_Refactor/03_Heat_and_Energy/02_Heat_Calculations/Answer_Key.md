## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Heating — q = mcΔT (120. g water, 25.0 → 100. °C):**

> Equation: q = mcΔT
> ΔT = 100. − 25.0 = 75.0 °C
> q = (120.)(4.18)(75.0) = **37,620 J ≈ 3.76 × 10⁴ J**

q is **positive** — the water warmed up, so energy flowed *in*. The temperature was changing (no phase change), so q = mcΔT is the correct equation.

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing q = mcΔT and computing ΔT = 75.0 °C (final − initial); 1 point for the correct value with the unit J. A student who uses ΔT = 100 (the final temperature) instead of the change gets 50,160 J — flag the ΔT = final − initial rule. NYSSLS: PS3-1.

**2. Melting — q = mH_f (45.0 g ice at 0 °C):**

> Equation: q = mH_f
> q = (45.0)(334) = **15,030 J ≈ 1.50 × 10⁴ J**

q is **positive** — energy flows *in* to melt the ice. There is no ΔT and no *c*: melting happens at a constant 0 °C, so all the energy goes into the phase change, not into raising temperature.

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing q = mH_f (recognizing a phase change, not a temperature change); 1 point for the correct value with the unit J. A student who tries q = mcΔT here has misread the situation — note that the temperature stays at 0 °C. NYSSLS: PS3-1.

**3. Boiling — q = mH_v (30.0 g water at 100 °C):**

> Equation: q = mH_v
> q = (30.0)(2260) = **67,800 J ≈ 6.78 × 10⁴ J**

q is **positive** — energy flows *in* to boil the water. Again no ΔT and no *c*: boiling happens at a constant 100 °C. Note this is far larger per gram than melting (Problem 2), because H_v (2260 J/g) is nearly seven times H_f (334 J/g).

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing q = mH_v; 1 point for the correct value with the unit J. Bonus credit for noting that boiling takes much more energy per gram than melting. NYSSLS: PS3-1.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Heating copper — q = mcΔT (80.0 g, c = 0.385 J/g·°C, 25.0 → 75.0 °C):**

> Equation: q = mcΔT
> ΔT = 75.0 − 25.0 = 50.0 °C
> q = (80.0)(0.385)(50.0) = **1,540 J ≈ 1.54 × 10³ J**

q is positive — the copper warmed up, so energy flowed in. The temperature was changing, so q = mcΔT applies. Note that copper's specific heat (0.385) is much smaller than water's (4.18) — copper heats up fast, which is why metal objects in the sun burn you quickly.

*Rubric:* 1 point for setting up q = mcΔT with ΔT = 50.0 °C; 1 point for the correct value (1,540 J or 1.54 × 10³ J) with the unit J. A student who writes ΔT = 75 missed the final − initial step. NYSSLS: PS3-1.

**(b) Boiling water — q = mH_v (25.0 g at 100 °C, H_v = 2260 J/g):**

> Equation: q = mH_v
> q = (25.0)(2260) = **56,500 J ≈ 5.65 × 10⁴ J**

q is positive — energy flows in to boil the water. No ΔT and no *c*: boiling is a phase change at a constant 100 °C.

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing q = mH_v (recognizing a phase change at constant temperature, not using q = mcΔT); 1 point for the correct value (56,500 J or 5.65 × 10⁴ J) with the unit J. A student who tries to include a temperature term has misread the constant-temperature boiling. NYSSLS: PS3-1.

**(c) Sign convention when a sample cools:**

*Full-credit model answer:* When a sample cools down, its q is **negative**, because energy is flowing *out* of the sample (it is losing thermal energy to its surroundings); by the PS3-1 convention, energy flowing into a sample is positive, so energy flowing out is negative.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that states q is negative AND links the negative sign to energy *leaving* (flowing out of) the sample earns full credit. A response that uses the calorimeter idea — "the cooling object's q is negative and equals the negative of the warming object's q (q_lost = −q_gained)" — also earns full credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for "negative"; 1 point for explaining it as energy flowing *out* of the sample. A response that says "negative" with no reason earns 1 point. NYSSLS: PS3-1.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the beach phenomenon and how the specific heat number (sand ≈ 0.84 vs. water ≈ 4.18) explained the ~5× temperature difference — students who connect the calculation back to the barefoot beach walk are showing that the equation models a real experience, not just an abstraction.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("specific heat capacity — the energy to raise one gram by one degree — is the c in q = mcΔT") are demonstrating the Hochman writing move from Phase 3 and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary into their active sentence structure.
- Students who name a partner who helped them decide *which* equation to use (q = mcΔT vs. q = mH_f vs. q = mH_v) are signaling that the group investigation supported their learning — note this as evidence the collaborative format is working.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows a correct equation choice but a wrong ΔT (used the final temperature instead of final − initial), the error is in the ΔT step, not the concept — a brief check-in at the start of the next lesson should resolve it. If a student used q = mcΔT for the boiling problem in part (b), they have not yet internalized that phase changes occur at constant temperature — target that student for a short review of the heating curve and the equation-choice flowchart before the next lesson builds on calorimetry.
