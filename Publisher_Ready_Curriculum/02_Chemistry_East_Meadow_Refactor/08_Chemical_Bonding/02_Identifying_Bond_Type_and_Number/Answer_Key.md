## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

Electronegativity values used: Na = 0.9, Cl = 3.0, O = 3.5, H = 2.1, Cu = 1.9.

**1. Bond in Na–Cl:**

| Element | Metal/Nonmetal | EN | ΔEN | Predicted bond |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na | metal | 0.9 |  |  |
| Cl | nonmetal | 3.0 | **2.1** | **ionic** |

Step-by-step: ΔEN = 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1, which is ≥ 1.7, and the pair is a metal + a nonmetal — both clues say ionic. The metal (Na) transfers its electron to the nonmetal (Cl), forming Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. This matches the phenomenon: those charged ions are what polar water can pull apart.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ΔEN (2.1); 1 point for naming the bond ionic AND citing either the ≥ 1.7 cutoff or the metal + nonmetal pairing. A student who calculates ΔEN but labels it covalent has applied the number line incorrectly — provide feedback on the 1.7 cutoff. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

**2. Bond in H–O (in H₂O):**

| Element | Metal/Nonmetal | EN | ΔEN | Predicted bond |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | nonmetal | 2.1 |  |  |
| O | nonmetal | 3.5 | **1.4** | **polar covalent** |

Step-by-step: ΔEN = 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4, which is below the 1.7 ionic cutoff, and both elements are nonmetals — so the atoms share electrons (covalent), but unevenly because oxygen pulls harder (polar covalent). This lopsided sharing is exactly why a water molecule is slightly charged on its ends and can dissolve ionic salt.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ΔEN (1.4); 1 point for naming the bond covalent (accept "polar covalent" for full credit; "covalent" alone earns full credit too). A student who labels it ionic missed that 1.4 < 1.7 — redirect to the number line. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

**3. Bond in Cu–Cu (copper metal):**

| Element | Metal/Nonmetal | EN | ΔEN | Predicted bond |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cu | metal | 1.9 |  |  |
| Cu | metal | 1.9 | **0** | **metallic** |

Step-by-step: ΔEN = 1.9 − 1.9 = 0. The number alone looks like nonpolar covalent (the same ΔEN as O₂), but the element types are both *metals* — two metals pool their valence electrons into a shared "sea" rather than sharing a single pair, which is a metallic bond. Element type breaks the tie.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct ΔEN (0); 1 point for naming the bond metallic AND justifying with "both are metals" (not just the ΔEN). A student who answers nonpolar covalent has used only the number and ignored element type — this is the key misconception to address. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

**4. Multiple choice:** The correct answer is **B** — *A large electronegativity difference between a metal and a nonmetal produces an ionic bond.*

- A is wrong: two metals form a metallic bond (electron sea), not a shared-pair covalent bond.
- C is wrong: electronegativity measures pull on shared electrons, not mass.
- D is wrong: ΔEN = 0 between two *nonmetals* is nonpolar covalent; metallic requires two *metals*. ΔEN alone does not determine metallic.

*Rubric:* 1 point for B. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

## Exit Ticket Answer

Electronegativity values used: Mg = 1.2, F = 4.0, H = 2.1, Cl = 3.0. Fe is a metal. **These pairs are deliberately different from the worksheet practice — students must apply the rule, not recall an answer.**

**(a) Bond in MgF₂:**

| Element | Metal/Nonmetal | EN | ΔEN | Predicted bond |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mg | metal | 1.2 |  |  |
| F | nonmetal | 4.0 | **2.8** | **ionic** |

ΔEN = 4.0 − 1.2 = 2.8, which is well above the 1.7 cutoff, and the pair is a metal (Mg) + a nonmetal (F) — both clues say ionic. Magnesium transfers two electrons (one to each fluorine), forming Mg²⁺ and two F⁻ ions.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct ΔEN (2.8); 1 point for naming the bond ionic AND citing the ≥ 1.7 cutoff or the metal + nonmetal pairing. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

**(b) Bond in HCl:**

| Element | Metal/Nonmetal | EN | ΔEN | Predicted bond |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | nonmetal | 2.1 |  |  |
| Cl | nonmetal | 3.0 | **0.9** | **polar covalent** |

ΔEN = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, which is below the 1.7 ionic cutoff and above 0.4, and both elements are nonmetals — so the bond is covalent, specifically polar covalent (uneven sharing, chlorine pulls harder). Accept "covalent" for full credit.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correct ΔEN (0.9); 1 point for naming the bond covalent (polar covalent earns full credit; covalent alone also full credit). A student who labels it ionic missed that 0.9 < 1.7 — redirect to the cutoff. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

**(c) Bond in solid iron (Fe):**

*Full-credit model answer:* The bond in iron is **metallic.** I know without calculating ΔEN because iron is a metal bonded to itself — a metal + metal pair always forms a metallic bond, in which the atoms pool their valence electrons into a shared "sea" around fixed cations.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that names the bond metallic and justifies it with "Fe is a metal" / "two metals" / "metal bonded to itself" earns full credit. A ΔEN calculation is not required and is not the expected justification — recognizing the element type is the skill.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for naming the bond metallic; 1 point for justifying with the metal (or metal + metal) element type. A student who tries to compute ΔEN = 0 and concludes "nonpolar covalent" has made the key error — note the metallic-vs-covalent distinction for a brief follow-up. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.i / HS-PS1-2.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any connection between the bond-type prediction and the observable phenomenon (salt dissolving, oil floating) — students who link the ΔEN they calculated to what they saw in the glass are showing that the abstract rule explains a real observation, which is the lesson's central transfer.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("electronegativity — how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons —") are demonstrating the Hochman writing move from Phase 3 and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary into their active sentence structure.
- Students who name a partner who helped them catch the Cu–Cu (metallic vs. covalent) trap are signaling that the group investigation structure supported their learning — note this as evidence that the two-clue check landed.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows a correct ΔEN but the wrong band (e.g., labels HCl's 0.9 as ionic), the error is in applying the cutoff, not the arithmetic — a brief number-line check at the start of the next lesson should be sufficient. If a student labels iron's bond nonpolar covalent, the error is using ΔEN alone and ignoring element type — that student needs a targeted review of the metallic case (two metals → electron sea) before the next lesson builds on bonding.
