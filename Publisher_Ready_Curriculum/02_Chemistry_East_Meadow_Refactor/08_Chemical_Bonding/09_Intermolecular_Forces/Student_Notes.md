## Worked Example

**Scenario:** Three liquids are raced down a tilted plate. **Water** finishes first, **cooking oil** finishes in the middle, and **honey** finishes last (oozes down slowest).

**Step 1 — Rank the flow speed (viscosity):**

Fastest → slowest: __________ , __________ , __________

**Step 2 — Use viscosity to rank the pull between molecules:**

The **slowest** liquid (__________) has the __________ (strongest / weakest) intermolecular forces, because its molecules grip each other hardest and resist sliding past one another.

**Step 3 — Predict the boiling points:**

Stronger intermolecular forces → __________ (higher / lower) boiling point. So of these three, __________ would be predicted to have the highest boiling point and __________ the lowest.

**Step 4 — Cause and Effect:**

- Cause: the __________ of the intermolecular force.
- Effect: the __________ (a bulk property you can measure — here, viscosity).

**Step 5 — Because / But / So:**

Honey is more viscous than water
**because** _______________________________________________,
**but** _______________________________________________,
**so** _______________________________________________.

*Check your reasoning:* "because" should name the stronger pull between molecules; "but" should note the covalent bonds inside the molecules stay intact; "so" should state the observable result (slow flow / high viscosity).

## Summary

Complete the Because / But / So sentence:

> A water strider can stand on top of a pond
> because __________________________________________,
> but __________________________________________,
> so __________________________________________.

**Remember:**
- **Inside** a molecule = strong covalent bond. **Between** molecules = weaker intermolecular force.
- Boiling overcomes the force **between** molecules, not the bonds **inside** them.
- Stronger intermolecular force → **higher** boiling point, **higher** viscosity, **higher** surface tension.

**Key idea (HS-PS1-3 / HS-PS2-6):** You can *infer the invisible* (the strength of the pull between molecules) from *what you can see and measure* (how fast it pours, how it beads up, what temperature it boils at). Engineers use this to design materials — waterproof sprays, non-drip paints, lubricants — by controlling the molecule-to-molecule attraction.
