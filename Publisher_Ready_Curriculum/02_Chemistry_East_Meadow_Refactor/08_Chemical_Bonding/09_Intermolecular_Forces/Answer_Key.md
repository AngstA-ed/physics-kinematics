# Intermolecular Forces — Answer Key

## Cover

Unit: Chemical Bonding — Lesson 09: Intermolecular Forces
East Meadow Schools × Valley Stream Central High School District
NYSSLS tag: HS-PS1-3, HS-PS2-6

## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Liquid X pours slowly in a thick blob; liquid Y splashes out thin.**

- **Stronger pull between molecules:** liquid X. Its molecules attract one another more strongly, so they resist sliding past each other.
- **Property:** viscosity (resistance to flow).
- **Explanation (sample full-credit):** *"Liquid X has stronger intermolecular forces, so its molecules grip each other and resist flowing, which is why it pours slowly in a thick blob — it has high viscosity."*

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing X as stronger; 1 point for naming viscosity AND connecting the stronger pull to slow flow. A student who says "X is thicker so it's heavier/denser" has the viscosity/density misconception — note that viscosity is about the pull between molecules, not density (oil is less dense than water but more viscous). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**2. One penny holds 28 drops; another holds only 9 drops of a different liquid.**

- **Stronger pull between molecules:** the liquid that held **28** drops. A stronger inward surface pull lets the drop dome higher before it spills.
- **Property:** surface tension — the inward pull at the surface that lets the liquid dome past the rim.
- **Explanation (sample full-credit):** *"The 28-drop liquid has stronger intermolecular forces, so its surface molecules pull inward more tightly (higher surface tension) and the drop can pile up higher before overflowing."*

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing the 28-drop liquid; 1 point for naming surface tension AND linking it to the inward pull. A common error is choosing the 9-drop liquid by reasoning "fewer drops fit, so it must be stronger" — redirect: more doming (more drops) = stronger surface tension. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**3. Substance P boils at 65 °C; substance Q boils at 210 °C.**

- **Stronger pull between molecules:** substance Q (the higher boiling point).
- **What is broken when it boils:** the **forces between molecules** (intermolecular forces), NOT the covalent bonds inside each molecule. The molecules leave as intact molecules.
- **Explanation (sample full-credit):** *"Q has stronger intermolecular forces, so it takes more energy (a higher temperature) to pull its whole molecules away from each other into a gas, giving it the higher boiling point of 210 °C."*

*Rubric:* 1 point for choosing Q; 1 point for stating that boiling overcomes the forces *between* molecules (not the covalent bonds inside) AND linking stronger forces to higher boiling point. A student who writes "boiling breaks the molecules apart into atoms" holds the central misconception — provide feedback: boiling separates whole molecules; the covalent bonds stay intact (steam is still H₂O). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Rubbing alcohol races down quickly; glycerin oozes slowly.**

- **Stronger intermolecular forces:** glycerin (it flows slowest → highest viscosity → strongest pull between molecules).
- **Higher boiling point (prediction):** glycerin.
- **Sample full-credit explanation:** *"Glycerin flows the slowest, so its molecules attract one another more strongly; stronger intermolecular forces also mean more energy is needed to separate the molecules, so glycerin is predicted to have the higher boiling point."*

*Actual values for the teacher (not required from students):* rubbing alcohol (isopropanol) boils at about 82 °C; glycerin (glycerol) boils at about 290 °C — confirming the prediction. These liquids are **distinct** from the water/oil/honey set used in the worksheet, so students must reason from the viscosity clue rather than recall a memorized answer.

*Rubric:* 1 point for identifying glycerin as having the stronger forces; 1 point for predicting glycerin has the higher boiling point AND linking the prediction to the strength of the intermolecular forces (not just "it's thicker"). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

**(b) Soap is added and the floating paper clip sinks.**

- **Sample full-credit explanation:** *"The clip was held up by surface tension — the inward pull of the water's surface molecules. The soap weakens (lowers) that surface tension, so the surface film can no longer support the clip and it sinks."*

*Rubric:* 1 point for stating that surface tension held the clip up; 1 point for stating that soap lowers/weakens the surface tension so the film can no longer hold the clip. A response that says "the soap made the water lighter/thinner" earns partial credit — redirect to "soap lowers surface tension by weakening the pull between surface molecules." Note: the clip floating was never buoyancy (steel is denser than water). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3, HS-PS2-6.

**(c) "Stronger intermolecular forces give a liquid a higher boiling point" — identify cause and effect.**

- **Cause:** stronger intermolecular forces (the stronger pull between molecules).
- **Effect:** a higher boiling point.

*Rubric:* 1 point for correctly labeling the cause (stronger intermolecular forces / stronger pull between molecules); 1 point for correctly labeling the effect (higher boiling point). Accept paraphrases that preserve the direction of the relationship. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-3.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A specific everyday example the student now sees differently — "I'll notice the bugs on the pond," "I get why the oil beads on my driveway puddle," "I see why honey is so slow" — rather than a generic "science is cool." Specific transfer signals the concept landed.
- Naming a groupmate who helped a piece click (e.g., "Mara figured out the penny held more drops without soap"). This signals the BTC random-group structure supported their learning — note it as evidence the collaborative vertical-surface work is functioning.
- Any response that correctly separates the *inside-the-molecule* bond from the *between-molecules* force shows the strongest conceptual transfer, since that distinction is the lesson's main hurdle.

If a student's worksheet shows they still think boiling "breaks the molecules apart into atoms," flag it for a brief one-on-one before the next lesson — this is the highest-priority misconception to correct, since the rest of the bonding unit assumes molecules stay intact through phase changes.
