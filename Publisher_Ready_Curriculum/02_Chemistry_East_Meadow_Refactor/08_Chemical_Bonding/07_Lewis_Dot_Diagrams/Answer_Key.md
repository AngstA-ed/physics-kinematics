## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Carbon (C, Group 14):**

Carbon has **4 valence electrons** (group number 14 → 4). The correct diagram is the symbol **C** with four single dots, one on each side (top, bottom, left, right) — no pairs, because the first four electrons go one per side. Carbon is **4 electrons short of an octet**, so the octet rule predicts carbon forms **4 bonds**.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct valence count (4); 1 point for a diagram with four *single* dots, one per side (not paired); 1 point for predicting 4 bonds. A student who draws two pairs has paired before placing all four singles — correct count, wrong arrangement; give partial credit and note the one-per-side rule. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**2. Fluorine (F, Group 17):**

Fluorine has **7 valence electrons** (group number 17 → 7). The correct diagram is **F** with three lone pairs (on three sides) and **one single, unpaired dot** on the fourth side. That single unpaired electron is the one available to bond, so fluorine has **1 unpaired electron** and forms one bond.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct valence count (7); 1 point for showing exactly one unpaired (single) dot with three pairs. A student who draws all seven without an unpaired electron (e.g., bunches them) has hidden the bonding electron — note the one-per-side-first rule. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**3. Oxygen (O, Group 16):**

Oxygen has **6 valence electrons** (group number 16 → 6). The correct diagram is **O** with two lone pairs (on two sides) and **two single, unpaired dots** (on the other two sides). Oxygen needs **2 more electrons** to reach an octet, which is why it forms two bonds (as in water).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct valence count (6); 1 point for the arrangement (two single dots + two pairs); 1 point for "needs 2 more to reach an octet." NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**4. The molecule F₂:**

Each fluorine starts with 7 valence electrons (one unpaired). In F₂ the two unpaired electrons form **one shared (bonding) pair** — a single bond — drawn as a pair of dots or a line between the two F symbols. Each fluorine also keeps **three lone pairs**. Counting around one fluorine: 1 shared pair (2 e⁻) + 3 lone pairs (6 e⁻) = **8 electrons** — a full octet for each fluorine.

*Rubric:* 1 point for showing the shared/bonding pair between the two F atoms; 1 point for "8 electrons surround each fluorine" (full octet). A common error is to forget that the shared pair counts for *both* atoms, yielding 7 — correct this directly: the bonding pair belongs to each atom's count. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Lewis dot diagram for nitrogen (N, Group 15):**

Nitrogen has **5 valence electrons** (group number 15 → 5). The correct diagram is the symbol **N** with **three single, unpaired dots** (one each on three sides) and **one lone pair** (on the fourth side). Place the first three dots one per side, then the fourth and fifth form one pair.

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct valence count (5); 1 point for a diagram showing three single dots + one pair (the one-per-side-first arrangement). A student who draws five dots as two pairs and a single, or all clustered, has the right count but the wrong arrangement — partial credit; reinforce that the three unpaired electrons are why nitrogen forms three bonds. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**(b) Lewis structure for Cl₂:**

Chlorine is in Group 17, so each chlorine has **7 valence electrons** with one unpaired. In Cl₂ the two unpaired electrons form **one shared (bonding) pair** — a single bond — drawn between the two Cl symbols (a pair of dots or a line). Each chlorine also has **three lone pairs**. Counting around either chlorine: 1 shared pair (2 e⁻) + 3 lone pairs (6 e⁻) = **8 electrons**, a full octet that should be circled on one Cl as instructed.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for showing the shared (bonding) pair between the two chlorine atoms; 1 point for circling a complete octet (8 electrons) on one chlorine. Note: Cl₂ is structurally identical to the worksheet's F₂ (both Group 17 diatomics), so a student who mastered F₂ should transfer the method directly; a student who draws only 7 around each Cl forgot to count the shared pair for both atoms. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

**(c) Why neon (Ne, Group 18) does not normally bond:**

*Full-credit model answer:* Neon is in Group 18 and already has **eight valence electrons — a full octet** — so it has no electrons to gain, lose, or share to become more stable, and therefore does not normally bond with other atoms.

*Acceptable variations:* Any response that identifies neon as already having a full outer shell / a complete octet / eight valence electrons, and connects that to having no reason to bond, earns full credit. A response that says only "neon is a noble gas" without explaining the full octet should receive partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for stating neon already has 8 valence electrons (a full octet); 1 point for connecting that to "no need to bond / already stable." NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the sodium-vs-neon phenomenon and how drawing the dots made the difference visible — students who connect the dot count (one vs. eight) to the observed reactivity are showing that the diagram is a real predictive tool, not an abstract drawing.
- Responses that name a Pictionary moment or a partner who helped them catch a miscount are signaling that the active-learning structure supported their learning — note this as evidence that the game-based repetition is working.
- Students who use the vocabulary precisely ("octet," "valence electrons," "shared pair") in their reflection are integrating the Phase 3 terms into active speech — note these students as having locked in the language.

If a student's Exit Ticket shows the correct *number* of dots but the wrong *arrangement* (paired before placing singles), the error is procedural, not conceptual — a brief check at the start of the next lesson should be sufficient, and it matters because bond-counting depends on identifying the unpaired electrons. If the dot *count* itself is wrong, the student is likely misreading the group number (e.g., using the period or the atomic number) — reteach the group-number → valence-electron pattern before the next lesson builds toward ionic and covalent bonding.
