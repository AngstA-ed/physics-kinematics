## Curated Resources (from East Meadow Scope & Sequence)

### NYSSLS Standards

**DCPS2.B** — *"Attraction and repulsion between electric charges at the atomic scale explain the structure, properties, and transformations of matter."* This lesson takes that disciplinary core idea down to the level of a single bond and then a whole molecule. When two atoms share a bonding pair of electrons unequally — because one atom pulls harder (higher electronegativity) — the bond carries partial charges (δ⁺ and δ⁻). Whether the *molecule* ends up polar or nonpolar depends on a second factor: its three-dimensional shape. Symmetric shapes can let equal bond dipoles cancel; asymmetric shapes let them add up to a net molecular dipole. Students use the attraction/repulsion idea to explain a macroscopic, observable property: which liquids a substance dissolves or floats in.

The Cross-Cutting Concept of **Structure and Function** is the explicit lens: the *structure* of a molecule (which atoms, how electronegative, and the geometry holding them) determines its *function* — how it interacts with other substances. "Like dissolves like" is Structure and Function stated as a rule of thumb: polar substances mix with polar substances, nonpolar with nonpolar.

### Phenomenon

Drop a few M&Ms into a clear dish of room-temperature water, printed-letter side up, and wait. Within a minute the colored sugar coating dissolves and the white printed "m" floats free to the surface — intact, like a tiny edible decal. Now run the identical setup in a dish of vegetable oil. Nothing happens: the coating stays put, no color bleeds, no "m" floats. Same candy, same temperature, same amount of liquid — completely different outcome.

The colored coating is mostly **sucrose and water-soluble dyes** (polar). Water is polar; oil is nonpolar. The coating dissolves in water because *like dissolves like* — and it does not dissolve in oil because the polar sugar molecules and the nonpolar oil molecules do not attract each other strongly enough to mix. The "m" is printed in an edible **shellac/nonpolar lacquer** that does *not* dissolve in water, so it survives and floats. The whole demonstration is the attraction-and-repulsion DCI made visible with candy.

**Driving question:** Why does the same M&M coating dissolve and float in water but do absolutely nothing in oil?

### Javalab / Labs

- **Floating M&Ms exploration (Engage/Explore):** Each group gets a dish of water and a dish of oil and two M&Ms. Drop one in each, letter-side up; observe and time what happens. Students record where the coating dissolves, where the "m" floats, and where nothing happens. No external URL required; the figure `figures/molecular_polarity.png` is projected during the Explain phase to formalize what they saw.
- **"Like dissolves like" stations (Explore):** Three small stations. (1) Salt (NaCl, ionic/polar-soluble) shaken in water vs. oil. (2) A drop of food coloring (water-based, polar) in water vs. oil. (3) A drop of vegetable oil (nonpolar) in water vs. more oil. Students fill a dissolve/no-dissolve grid and look for the pattern.
- **Bending water with a charged rod (Explore/Elaborate, quick demo):** Run a thin stream of water from a faucet, then bring a charged plastic rod (rubbed on hair/wool) near it. The stream visibly bends toward the rod because polar water molecules have a δ⁺ and δ⁻ end and orient toward the charge. A nonpolar liquid stream (if available) does not bend — strong evidence that water molecules carry separated charge.
- **Reference:** A Javalab "polarity of molecules" or PhET "molecule polarity" simulation can be projected to let students toggle electronegativity and watch the bond dipole grow; optional and not required for the hands-on flow.

### Assessments

- **Acetone Mystery Case** (constructed response, following lesson / station card): acetone (a moderately polar solvent) dissolves nail polish but water does not; students explain using bond polarity, molecular polarity, and "like dissolves like." See `Assessments/` folder once created.
- **Bonding & Solutions Assessment Question** (district checkpoint): given a molecule's formula and shape, predict polar vs. nonpolar and whether it dissolves in water.
- **Exit Ticket** (Phase 5): three items — classify a bond as polar/nonpolar from electronegativity; decide whether CCl₄ (symmetric) is a polar or nonpolar molecule; predict which solvent a substance dissolves in. **Lesson objectives — students can:**

- Classify a covalent bond as polar or nonpolar using the electronegativity difference between the two bonded atoms.
- Explain that a molecule's polarity depends on *both* its bond dipoles *and* its three-dimensional shape; identify cases where symmetric shape causes bond dipoles to cancel.
- Use "like dissolves like" to predict whether a substance dissolves in a polar solvent (water) or a nonpolar solvent (oil), and connect that prediction to the M&M phenomenon.
- Explain, using Structure and Function, why the same M&M coating dissolves in water but not in oil.

## Phase 1 · Engage *(0 – 10 min)*

### 0–3 min · Opening Circle + Do Now

Begin with a brief **community-building opening circle** (2–3 min). Everyone — teacher included — answers: *"Name two things that just won't mix no matter how hard you stir — in cooking, cleaning, or anywhere in life. Why do you think they refuse to mix?"* One round, one sentence each, no judgment. Students will offer oil-and-water, oil-and-vinegar dressing that separates, water-and-grease on a pan. This surfaces the everyday intuition that some substances mix and some don't — exactly the puzzle today resolves at the molecular level.

Then post the **Do Now**:

> *"Two clear dishes are on the front table. One holds water, one holds vegetable oil. I'm about to drop one identical M&M into each, printed side up. In one sentence: predict what will happen in each dish, and why you think they might be different."*

Give students 2 minutes to write silently, then take two or three predictions.

**Anticipated student responses to Do Now:**

- "The candy will dissolve in both because it's just sugar." — capture the prediction; we'll test it. Ask: are water and oil really the same kind of liquid?
- "It'll dissolve in water but not oil because water is what you drink and oil is greasy." — good intuition; affirm and ask them to hold that thought — *why* does greasy matter?
- "The 'm' will float." — excellent specific prediction; we'll watch for it. Ask: why might the printed letter behave differently from the colored coating?

### 3–8 min · Phenomenon hook — the floating M&M

**Teacher actions.** Drop one M&M printed-side-up into the water dish and one into the oil dish at the same time. Start the timer. Within about a minute, the water dish shows a spreading halo of color and the white "m" lifts off and floats; the oil dish shows nothing — coating intact, no color, no floating "m." Walk the two dishes around or project under a document camera.

> "Same candy. Same temperature. Same amount of liquid. In the water, the color bled out in seconds and the little 'm' floated up like a sticker peeling off. In the oil — nothing. The coating is just sitting there. Why would the same coating behave so differently in two clear liquids that, honestly, look pretty similar?"

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "Water dissolves sugar and oil doesn't." — exactly the observation we want; we'll explain *why* water can and oil can't.
- "Oil is thicker, so it's slower." — test the idea: it's not just slower — wait longer and the oil dish *still* shows nothing. Speed isn't the explanation; something about the kind of liquid is.
- "The 'm' is made of something that doesn't dissolve in water at all." — sharp observation; the printed letter is a nonpolar edible lacquer — we'll connect that to the pattern.

**Driving question** (post on the board and leave it there):

> *Why does the same M&M coating dissolve and float in water but do absolutely nothing in oil?*

### 8–10 min · Notice & Wonder + Turn-and-Talk #1

Two columns on the board: **I notice… / I wonder…**

Collect 3–4 responses about the two dishes. Then:

> "Turn to your partner: water and oil are both clear liquids — but the candy treats them completely differently. What might be *different about the liquids themselves*, at a level too small to see, that decides whether the coating dissolves?"

Target insight (leave open if no one lands it yet): the two liquids must differ in some hidden property of their molecules — one "matches" the sugar coating and one doesn't. That hidden property is **polarity**, which we'll name and measure in Phase 3.

## Phase 2 · Explore *(10 – 30 min)*

### 10–14 min · Initial Model (silent / individual)

Prompt on the board:

> *"Before we name anything: draw the water dish and the oil dish from the demo. For each one, draw what you think is happening to the M&M coating at a scale too small to see — are the tiny pieces of coating spreading out into the liquid, or staying clumped together? Why might the two liquids be different?"*

Students draw/write silently for 3–4 minutes. This surfaces prior knowledge from Lessons 02 and 05 (some students will already say "water has charges" or "oil doesn't mix with water") and sets up the station work. Collect the range: most will have a "dissolves vs. doesn't" model; few will yet name *why*. That gap is the instructional target.

### 14–24 min · ACTIVE LEARNING stations — "Like dissolves like" (data BEFORE vocabulary)

**Before any formal vocabulary is introduced**, students generate their own dissolve/no-dissolve data set. Set up three quick stations (or run all three at each table). At each, students add a small amount of the test substance to **water** and to **oil**, stir gently, and record what happens.

| Station | Test substance | In WATER | In OIL |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Salt (NaCl) | dissolves (disappears) | does not dissolve (stays as grains) |
| 2 | Food coloring (water-based dye) | mixes / spreads (dissolves) | beads up, does not mix |
| 3 | Vegetable oil (a few drops) | beads up, does not mix | mixes completely |

Students fill the grid and then look for the **pattern**: every substance dissolves in *one* of the two liquids, not both — and salt + food coloring behave one way while oil behaves the opposite way.

**Teacher facilitation language (circulate):**

> "Don't just write 'dissolves' — watch closely. Does the salt vanish, or does it sit at the bottom? Does the food coloring spread out, or pull into a tight bead? The candy coating in the demo behaved like which of these station substances — the salt and dye, or the oil?"

> "Sort your three test substances into two groups by behavior. What does the water group have in common? What does the oil group have in common? You're discovering a rule before I give you the words for it."

**Anticipated student responses during stations:**

- "Salt and food coloring both dissolve in water; oil doesn't." — exactly; that's the first half of the pattern.
- "Oil only mixes with oil." — perfect; push it: so things seem to mix with *their own kind*. What kind is the candy coating?
- "Why does food coloring bead up on the oil like little dots?" — great observation; the dye molecules attract each other and the water far more than they attract the oil, so they refuse to spread. That attraction difference is the whole story.

### 24–28 min · Bending water demo + record the evidence

Run the **charged-rod demo** for the class. Turn on a thin, steady stream of water. Rub a plastic rod on wool or hair to charge it, then bring it near the stream — the stream bends visibly toward the rod.

> "Watch the water bend toward a charged rod that never even touches it. The rod is pulling on the water. For that to happen, water molecules must have a positive end and a negative end — separated charge inside each molecule — so the charged rod can grab them and turn them. Oil molecules don't have that, so a stream of oil wouldn't bend."

Students record in their worksheet: *the water bent → water molecules have separated (+/−) charge; this is the hidden property that decides what dissolves in what.*

### 28–30 min · Reconnect — surface the rule

Bring groups back together. Put two columns on the board: **dissolves in water** (salt, food coloring, M&M coating) and **dissolves in oil** (oil). Ask:

> "Everything in the left column carries separated charge or dissolves things that do — water itself bends toward a charged rod. Everything in the right column does not. So what determines whether two substances mix? It looks like *similar* substances mix with each other. Hold that — in three minutes I'll give you the exact words and show you how to predict it from the Periodic Table."

Surface the key idea without naming it yet: substances mix when they are *alike* in this hidden charge property; the candy coating is in the "water" group, so it dissolves in water and ignores oil.

## Phase 3 · Explain *(30 – 36 min)*

### 30–33 min · Turn-and-Talk #2 + class consensus

> "Look at your station grid and the bending-water demo. Turn to your partner: water bends toward a charge but oil doesn't. What does that tell you must be true about a water molecule that is *not* true about an oil molecule? Try to describe it in terms of charge."

Target consensus: a water molecule has a positive end and a negative end — the charge inside it is *separated*, not evenly spread. Oil molecules have charge spread evenly, no positive or negative end. Substances with separated charge mix with each other; substances with even charge mix with each other.

> "Where does that separated charge come from? Think back to Lesson 02 — when two atoms share electrons in a covalent bond, do they always share *equally*?"

Target: no — one atom can pull the shared electrons closer (it is more electronegative), giving that atom a slightly negative end and leaving the other atom slightly positive. That unequal sharing is the source of the separated charge.

### 33–36 min · Vocabulary introduction (exactly 3 terms)

**Sample teacher language:**

> "Now let's name what you discovered. When two atoms share a bonding pair of electrons *unequally* — because one atom is more **electronegative** and pulls harder — the bond has a slightly negative end (δ⁻, where the electrons spend more time) and a slightly positive end (δ⁺). That unevenly-shared bond is a **polar bond**. You find it from the Periodic Table: look up each atom's electronegativity. A big difference (about 0.5 or more) means a polar bond; almost no difference — two identical atoms, like the two O's in O₂ — means a **nonpolar bond**, where the electrons are shared evenly."

> "But here's the twist your stations set up. A molecule can be full of polar bonds and *still* be a **nonpolar molecule** — if its shape is symmetric enough that the bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel out. The whole molecule has no net positive or negative end. CO₂ is the classic case: two polar C=O bonds, but because the molecule is *linear*, the two bond dipoles pull in exactly opposite directions and cancel. Net dipole zero. Nonpolar molecule. Water is the opposite: its two O–H bonds are polar *and* the molecule is *bent*, so the dipoles add up to one net dipole — water is a **polar molecule**. That net dipole is exactly the positive-and-negative end that let the stream bend toward the rod."

Project the figure and walk through all three panels:

![Three-panel diagram comparing bond polarity and molecular polarity. Left panel: an HCl polar bond with a small blue hydrogen atom labeled delta-plus and a larger purple chlorine atom labeled delta-minus, with an orange net dipole arrow pointing toward chlorine. Center panel: a linear CO₂ molecule with a central blue carbon and two purple oxygens; two orange bond-dipole arrows point outward in opposite directions and are labeled 'bond dipoles cancel,' with a green 'net dipole = 0' label, marking CO₂ as a nonpolar molecule. Right panel: a bent H₂O molecule with a purple oxygen labeled delta-minus at top and two blue hydrogens labeled delta-plus below; a single orange arrow points up toward oxygen labeled 'net dipole,' marking water as a polar molecule.](figures/molecular_polarity.png)

Post the three terms on the board. Students fill them in on their notes.

**Discussion prompts to deploy here:**

- "CO₂ has two polar bonds but is a nonpolar *molecule*. How is that possible?" — *Expected response:* the molecule is linear and symmetric, so the two equal bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel; there is no net positive or negative end.
- "The M&M coating dissolves in water. Based on what you now know, is the coating polar or nonpolar?" — *Expected response:* polar — it dissolves in water (a polar solvent), so by "like dissolves like" the sugar/dye coating must be polar too.

## Phase 4 · Elaborate *(36 – 40 min)*

### 36–39 min · Revise the model + Because/But/So expansion

Students return to their Initial Model (the two dishes drawn in Phase 2). They revise it by labeling: (a) the water side "polar solvent — has a net dipole," (b) the oil side "nonpolar solvent — no net dipole," and (c) the dissolving candy coating as "polar — like dissolves like." Then run a **Because/But/So** sentence about the phenomenon. Starter on the board:

> *"The M&M coating dissolves in water but not in oil."*

Model one aloud:

> "The M&M coating dissolves in water **because** the coating is made of polar sugar and dye molecules, and water is a polar solvent, so 'like dissolves like' lets them attract and mix — **but** vegetable oil is a nonpolar solvent with no net dipole, so the polar coating molecules have nothing to attract them into the oil — **so** the coating stays intact in oil and only dissolves in water, exactly what we saw in the two dishes."

Then have students write their own B/B/S using one of these starters:

- *"CO₂ contains two polar bonds, but the whole molecule is nonpolar…"* (hint: shape — linear, dipoles cancel)
- *"A stream of water bends toward a charged rod, but a stream of oil would not…"*

**Anticipated student responses:**

- "Because the two C=O dipoles point opposite ways and cancel." — strong; push for the So: "so CO₂ has no net dipole, which makes it a nonpolar molecule even though its bonds are polar."
- "Because water molecules have a positive and negative end." — good; push for the But/So: "but oil molecules have charge spread evenly, so only water gets pulled by the rod."

### 39–40 min · Return to the phenomenon

> "Return to the two dishes. We started with a puzzle: same coating, two liquids, totally different outcome. Now you have the structure-and-function rule. In one sentence using the word *polar*: explain why the coating dissolves in water but not in oil."

Target: "The coating is polar, water is a polar solvent and the two are alike enough to mix (like dissolves like), but oil is nonpolar, so the polar coating does not dissolve in it."

> "And the floating 'm'? That printed letter is an edible *nonpolar* lacquer — it does not dissolve in polar water, so it survives and floats free while the polar coating around it washes away. The same rule explains both halves of what you saw."

## Phase 5 · Evaluate *(40 – 42 min)*

### 40–42 min · Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

Post or read aloud:

> *(a) The N–H bond has an electronegativity difference of about 0.9. Is this bond polar or nonpolar? Explain in one sentence using the word electronegativity.*
> *(b) CCl₄ has four polar C–Cl bonds arranged in a symmetric tetrahedral shape. Is CCl₄ a polar or a nonpolar molecule? Explain.*
> *(c) Iodine (I₂) is nonpolar. Predict whether it dissolves better in water or in hexane (a nonpolar solvent), and state the rule you used.*

Expected answers are in `Answer_Key.docx`. (These molecules — N–H, CCl₄, I₂ — are intentionally different from the worksheet practice, which uses HCl, O₂, H₂O, CO₂, and the M&M coating.)

**Closing Reflection (SEL, 30 seconds):**

> "Today a piece of candy taught us a rule that explains why oil and water won't mix in your kitchen. In one sentence: what is one everyday thing you now understand differently — and who, a partner or a station, helped you see it?"

Collect worksheets; note which students correctly separated *bond* polarity (electronegativity difference) from *molecular* polarity (shape decides). Students who call CO₂ polar "because it has polar bonds" have the most common misconception — target them for a 30-second check at the start of the next lesson.

## Common Misconceptions

- **Misconception:** "If a molecule has polar bonds, the whole molecule must be polar." → **Correction:** Molecular polarity depends on *both* the bond dipoles *and* the shape. In a symmetric molecule, equal bond dipoles point in opposite directions and cancel, leaving zero net dipole. CO₂ has two polar C=O bonds but is a nonpolar molecule because it is linear; CCl₄ has four polar C–Cl bonds but is nonpolar because it is symmetric tetrahedral. Shape can override the bonds.
- **Misconception:** "Polar and nonpolar are the same as ionic and covalent." → **Correction:** Polar/nonpolar describes how *evenly* a covalent bonding pair is shared. A nonpolar covalent bond (e.g., O₂) shares evenly; a polar covalent bond (e.g., H–Cl) shares unevenly. Ionic bonding is a separate, more extreme case where electrons transfer entirely. Polarity is a spectrum *within* covalent bonding, set by the electronegativity difference.
- **Misconception:** "Things dissolve based on how thick or thin the liquid is." → **Correction:** Dissolving is about *attraction*, not viscosity. Oil is thicker than water, but the coating still won't dissolve in it no matter how long you wait. "Like dissolves like" — polar substances dissolve in polar solvents, nonpolar in nonpolar — because only alike molecules attract each other strongly enough to mix.
- **Misconception:** "Bigger atoms are always more electronegative / pull electrons harder." → **Correction:** Electronegativity is its own periodic trend (it increases up and to the right, peaking at fluorine), not a size ranking. You read it from the Reference Tables for each atom; the *difference* between the two bonded atoms decides bond polarity. Fluorine, a small atom, is the most electronegative element.
- **Misconception:** "δ⁺ and δ⁻ mean the atom gained or lost a whole electron / has a full charge." → **Correction:** The δ (delta) means *partial*. In a polar bond the shared electrons merely spend *more time* near the more electronegative atom, giving it a partial negative charge, not a full −1. No electron is transferred — that would be ionic. Partial charge is exactly why we write δ rather than a full + or −.

## Access & Differentiation

- **ELL/ENL supports:** Sentence frames for the stations and Exit Ticket: *"In water the ___ dissolved, but in oil it ___, because ___."* and *"This molecule is ___ (polar/nonpolar) because its shape is ___."* Word-choice box displayed on the board throughout: {electronegativity, polar, nonpolar, dipole, like dissolves like, symmetric, net dipole}. Pair each vocabulary term with a gesture: pull both hands unevenly to one side for *polar bond*, pull evenly for *nonpolar*; cup hands into a bent shape for water, flat line for CO₂. Allow students to label the figure panels in their first language and then write the explanation in English.
- **IEP/SPED supports:** Provide a pre-filled station grid with the substance names and "water / oil" column headers already printed; the student's task is to circle *dissolves* or *does not dissolve* and look for the pattern (not to set up the table). Pre-highlight the electronegativity column on the Reference Table and give a worked "difference = bigger − smaller" example card. Assign clear partner roles at stations: one pours, one observes and times, one records. Offer a cut-and-sort version of the figure where students match molecule cards (HCl, CO₂, H₂O) to "polar molecule / nonpolar molecule" bins.
- **Extensions:** (1) Predict and then check: is NH₃ (trigonal pyramidal) a polar or nonpolar molecule? Why does its shape *not* cancel the bond dipoles the way CO₂'s does? (2) Soap and detergents have a polar "head" and a nonpolar "tail" — research how that dual structure lets soap dissolve *both* grease and water, and connect it to "like dissolves like." (3) Rank these bonds by polarity using Reference-Table electronegativities: C–H, O–H, N–H, F–H. Which is the most polar, and why does fluorine win?

## Strategy Spotlight

**ACTIVE LEARNING — data before vocabulary.** Active learning means students *do the science* and generate the evidence before the teacher supplies the labels. In this lesson the dissolve/no-dissolve stations and the M&M demo come *first*; the words *polar*, *nonpolar*, and *electronegativity* arrive only in Phase 3, after students have already sorted substances into two behavior groups with their own hands. This sequencing (the ABC rule — Activity Before Concept) means that when the vocabulary lands, it names something students have already felt to be true, rather than arriving as an abstract definition to memorize.

**How to run it in this lesson (Phase 2 → Phase 3):**

1. Give every group real materials (water, oil, salt, food coloring, candy) and a blank grid. Resist the urge to pre-teach "polar." Let them discover that substances sort into two groups.
2. Circulate with *questions*, not answers: "What do the substances that dissolve in water have in common?" The goal is for the *pattern* (like dissolves like) to be the students' finding.
3. In Phase 3, name the pattern they found. The reveal is satisfying precisely because it explains data they already trust: "The property you discovered is called polarity, and here's how to predict it from the Periodic Table."
4. Close the loop in Phase 4: students re-explain the original M&M phenomenon using the new vocabulary — proof that the active investigation, not the lecture, drove the understanding.

**Why active learning fits this content:** Polarity is invisible and abstract, and students routinely conflate it with ionic/covalent or with "thickness." Hands-on dissolving data gives the abstraction a concrete anchor — students can *point to* the salt vanishing in water and the oil beading up. When a student later forgets the definition of "polar," they can reconstruct it from the memory of the candy coating dissolving in water but not oil.

**CRSE connection:** The opening-circle prompt (two things that won't mix — in cooking, cleaning, anywhere) invites students to bring kitchen and home knowledge into chemistry: oil-and-vinegar dressing that separates, grease that water alone won't cut, the reason dish soap is needed. The M&M phenomenon uses a familiar, low-cost candy that every student recognizes. Grounding an atomic-scale idea (separated charge) in substances students handle at home honors out-of-school knowledge and signals that chemistry explains their own world, not just the lab.

## Key Vocabulary (max 3)

- **electronegativity** — a measure of how strongly an atom pulls on a shared bonding pair of electrons; read from the Reference Tables for each element. The *difference* in electronegativity between two bonded atoms decides whether their bond is polar (uneven sharing) or nonpolar (even sharing); the trend increases up and to the right on the Periodic Table, peaking at fluorine
- **polar bond** — a covalent bond in which the bonding electrons are shared *unequally* because one atom is more electronegative, giving that atom a partial negative charge (δ⁻) and the other a partial positive charge (δ⁺); a bond between identical atoms (no electronegativity difference) is instead a *nonpolar bond*
- **polar molecule** — a molecule that has an overall net dipole — a positive end and a negative end — because its polar bonds do *not* cancel; this happens when the molecular shape is asymmetric (e.g., bent H₂O). A *nonpolar molecule* has no net dipole, either because its bonds are nonpolar or because a symmetric shape (e.g., linear CO₂) makes equal bond dipoles cancel
