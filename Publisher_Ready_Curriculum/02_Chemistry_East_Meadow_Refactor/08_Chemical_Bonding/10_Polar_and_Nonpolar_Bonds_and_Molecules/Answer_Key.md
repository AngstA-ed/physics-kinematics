## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. Classify the H–Cl bond.**

- Electronegativity difference: 3.2 − 2.2 = **1.0**.
- This bond is **polar** (the difference, 1.0, is well above 0.5, so the electrons are shared unequally).
- Chlorine gets the partial negative charge (**δ⁻**) because it has the higher electronegativity (3.2 vs. 2.2), so it pulls the shared bonding electrons closer to itself; hydrogen is left with the partial positive charge (δ⁺).

*Rubric:* 1 point for the correct difference (1.0) and "polar"; 1 point for naming Cl as the δ⁻ atom *and* citing higher electronegativity as the reason. A student who says "Cl is bigger" instead of "more electronegative" gets partial credit — redirect to the electronegativity value. NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

**2. Molecule vs. bond — CO₂.**

- The C=O bonds are **polar** (oxygen, ≈3.4, is much more electronegative than carbon, ≈2.6; difference ≈ 0.8).
- The whole molecule is **nonpolar**.
- A molecule made of polar bonds can still be nonpolar when its shape is **symmetric**: CO₂ is linear, so the two equal C=O bond dipoles point in exactly opposite directions and cancel. There is no net dipole — no overall positive or negative end — so the molecule is nonpolar even though each bond is polar.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "bonds polar, molecule nonpolar"; 1 point for explaining that the linear/symmetric shape makes the equal bond dipoles cancel. The most common error is calling CO₂ polar "because it has polar bonds" — if seen, note that shape decides molecular polarity. NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

**3. Molecule vs. bond — H₂O.**

- The whole molecule is **polar**.
- Water is **bent** (asymmetric), so its two polar O–H bond dipoles do *not* point in opposite directions — they add up to one net dipole pointing toward the oxygen. CO₂ is linear, so its dipoles cancel; water's bent shape prevents that cancellation, leaving a net positive end (the H side) and a net negative end (the O side).

*Rubric:* 1 point for "polar molecule"; 1 point for explaining that the bent shape lets the dipoles add (rather than cancel as in linear CO₂). Accept "the two O–H dipoles don't cancel because the molecule isn't symmetric." NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

**4. Back to the candy.**

- The coating is **polar**. We know because it dissolves in water (a polar solvent) and not in oil (a nonpolar solvent) — by "like dissolves like," only a polar substance would dissolve in polar water.
- The rule used is **"like dissolves like"**: polar substances dissolve in polar solvents; nonpolar substances dissolve in nonpolar solvents.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "polar" with the water-dissolving evidence; 1 point for naming "like dissolves like." A student who reasons only "it dissolves in water so it's like water" without naming the rule gets partial credit. NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

*Investigation note (Parts 1–3, for teacher reference):* Station grid — Salt: dissolves in water, not in oil; Food coloring: dissolves in water, not in oil; Vegetable oil: not in water, dissolves in oil. Group A (water) = salt, food coloring (and the M&M coating); Group B (oil) = vegetable oil. Bending-water demo — the stream **bends toward** the rod because water molecules have separated charge (a δ⁺ and δ⁻ end); an oil stream would **not** bend (oil molecules are nonpolar, charge spread evenly). Bond table — H–Cl: 3.2 − 2.2 = 1.0, **polar**; O=O: 3.4 − 3.4 = 0, **nonpolar**; O–H: 3.4 − 2.2 = 1.2, **polar**.

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) N–H bond, electronegativity difference ≈ 0.9:**

The N–H bond is **polar**. An electronegativity difference of about 0.9 is well above the ~0.5 threshold, which means nitrogen and hydrogen share the bonding electrons unequally — nitrogen, the more electronegative atom, pulls them closer and carries the partial negative charge.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "polar"; 1 point for explaining with electronegativity (the 0.9 difference means unequal sharing / N pulls harder). NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

**(b) CCl₄ — polar or nonpolar molecule:**

CCl₄ is a **nonpolar molecule**. Although it has four polar C–Cl bonds, the symmetric tetrahedral shape points the four equal bond dipoles outward in a balanced arrangement so that they cancel completely. With no net dipole, the molecule is nonpolar — the same reasoning as linear CO₂, just in three dimensions.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "nonpolar"; 1 point for explaining that the symmetric tetrahedral shape makes the four equal bond dipoles cancel. A student who says "polar because it has polar bonds" earns 0 for this item but should be flagged for the shape-decides misconception. NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

**(c) Iodine (I₂) — which solvent:**

I₂ dissolves better in **hexane** (the nonpolar solvent), not water. Iodine is nonpolar, and by **"like dissolves like,"** a nonpolar substance dissolves in a nonpolar solvent. Water is polar, so it will not dissolve nonpolar I₂ well.

*Rubric:* 1 point for "hexane"; 1 point for citing "like dissolves like" (nonpolar dissolves in nonpolar). NYSSLS: DCPS2.B.

*Tolerance/rubric note:* These Exit Ticket molecules (N–H, CCl₄, I₂) are deliberately different from the worksheet practice set (HCl, O₂, H₂O, CO₂, M&M coating), so students must transfer the method, not recall a memorized answer. Full credit requires the *reasoning* (electronegativity for bonds; symmetric-shape-cancels for molecules; like-dissolves-like for solubility), not just the one-word classification.

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- A concrete everyday connection — "now I understand why oil-and-vinegar dressing separates" or "why I need dish soap to get grease off a pan" — rather than a generic "polarity is cool." Students who name a real home/kitchen example are transferring the structure-and-function idea beyond the lab.
- Naming a partner or a specific station (salt, food coloring, the bending water) as what helped them see the pattern — this signals the active-learning station structure landed and that the discovery, not a lecture, drove understanding.
- Responses that correctly separate *bond* polarity from *molecule* polarity (e.g., "I learned a molecule with polar bonds can still be nonpolar") show the deepest grasp — note these students.

If a student's Exit Ticket calls CCl₄ polar "because it has polar bonds," the shape-decides idea has not yet landed — a 30-second check at the start of the next lesson (revisit CO₂ and CCl₄ symmetry) should resolve it before intermolecular forces (Lesson 09 / next unit) builds on molecular polarity.
