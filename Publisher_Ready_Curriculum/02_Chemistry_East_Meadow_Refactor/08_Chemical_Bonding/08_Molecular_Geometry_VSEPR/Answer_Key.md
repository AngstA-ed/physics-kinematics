# Molecular Geometry (VSEPR) — Answer Key

## Cover

Unit: Chemical Bonding — Lesson 08: Molecular Geometry (VSEPR)
East Meadow Schools × Valley Stream Central High School District

## Make-It-Make-Sense Answers

**1. CO₂:**

| Bonded atoms | Lone pairs on center | Electron groups | Shape name | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | 2 | linear | 180° |

Step-by-step: carbon is double-bonded to two oxygens. Each double bond counts as ONE electron group, so there are 2 electron groups and 0 lone pairs on carbon. Two groups push to opposite sides, giving a straight line at 180°.

*Rubric:* 1 point for shape (linear) and 1 point for the correct electron-group count (2) with the angle (180°). A student who writes "4 electron groups" likely counted each double bond as 2 — remind them a multiple bond is one group. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

**2. CH₄:**

| Bonded atoms | Lone pairs on center | Electron groups | Shape name | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0 | 4 | tetrahedral | 109.5° |

Step-by-step: carbon is bonded to four hydrogens with no lone pairs. Four electron groups spread into a three-dimensional tetrahedron at 109.5° — they cannot all lie flat.

*Rubric:* 1 point for shape (tetrahedral) and 1 point for the angle (109.5°). A student who draws it flat (square or cross) has missed that 4 groups force a 3-D arrangement — redirect to the 4-balloon model. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

**3. H₂O:**

| Bonded atoms | Lone pairs on center | Electron groups | Shape name | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | 4 | bent | ≈104.5° |

Step-by-step: oxygen is bonded to two hydrogens and has two lone pairs. That is 4 electron groups total — arranged tetrahedrally — but two of them are invisible lone pairs. The shape is named by where the atoms are, so it is bent, with an angle of about 104.5° (slightly less than 109.5° because lone pairs push harder than bonds).

*Rubric:* 2 points — 1 for counting all 4 electron groups (including the 2 lone pairs), 1 for the bent shape. A student who answers "linear" or "tetrahedral" missed the lone-pair effect — the most common error. Accept any angle from 104° to 105°. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

**4. Why CO₂ and H₂O have different shapes:**

*Full-credit model answer:* Both molecules have three atoms, but the central atoms have different numbers of electron groups: carbon in CO₂ has 2 electron groups and no lone pairs (linear), while oxygen in H₂O has 4 electron groups, two of which are lone pairs that push the hydrogens out of a straight line (bent). The shape depends on the electron groups on the central atom, not on the number of atoms.

*Rubric:* full credit requires using both *lone pair* and *electron group* and stating that the lone pairs on oxygen are what bend water. Partial credit for "they're different atoms" without naming the lone-pair cause. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

## Exit Ticket Answer

**(a) Carbon disulfide, CS₂:**

| Bonded atoms | Lone pairs on center | Electron groups | Shape | Bond angle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | 2 | linear | 180° |

Carbon is double-bonded to two sulfur atoms with no lone pairs on carbon. Each double bond is one electron group, so there are 2 electron groups, which push to opposite sides — linear, 180°. CS₂ has the same shape as CO₂ for the same reason: a central atom with 2 groups and no lone pairs.

*Rubric:* 1 point for linear; 1 point for the angle (180°) or for showing 2 electron groups / 0 lone pairs. A student who answers "bent" copied the water result without checking lone pairs — note the error. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

**(b) Ammonia, NH₃:**

| Bonded atoms | Lone pairs on center | Electron groups | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 1 | 4 | trigonal pyramidal |

Nitrogen is bonded to three hydrogens and has one lone pair — 4 electron groups total. The lone pair takes the fourth position (where the fourth "balloon" would be), pushing the three hydrogens into a pyramid below the nitrogen. The named shape (atoms only) is trigonal pyramidal, with an angle of about 107°. NH₃ was **not** practiced on the worksheet, so students must apply the method to a new molecule.

*Rubric:* 2 points — 1 for counting 4 electron groups (3 bonds + 1 lone pair), 1 for the trigonal pyramidal shape. Accept "pyramidal." A student who answers "trigonal planar" forgot the lone pair (which would make it planar only if there were 3 groups and 0 lone pairs) — this is the target re-teach. NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

**(c) Why two three-atom molecules can have different shapes:**

*Full-credit model answer:* The shape of a molecule is set by the number of electron groups — bonds plus lone pairs — on its central atom, not by how many atoms it has; so two molecules with three atoms each can differ in shape if their central atoms have different numbers of lone pairs (for example, CO₂ has no lone pairs and is linear, while H₂O has two lone pairs and is bent).

*Acceptable variations:* any response identifying the electron groups (or lone pairs) on the central atom as the deciding factor, rather than atom count, earns full credit. Responses that say only "they have different atoms" without naming lone pairs or electron groups receive partial credit.

*Rubric:* 2 points total — 1 point for stating that electron groups / lone pairs on the central atom determine shape, 1 point for explicitly contrasting it with atom count (same atom count, different shape). NYSSLS: HS-PS1-1 (supporting).

## Closing Reflection (rubric)

The closing reflection is formative, not graded. Award full credit for any genuine, specific response. Look for:

- Any mention of the balloon model — students who connect the self-arranging balloons to electron-pair repulsion are showing that the abstract VSEPR rule became concrete and physical for them.
- Responses that use the appositive structure ("molecular geometry — the three-dimensional shape a molecule takes — is determined by...") are demonstrating the Hochman writing move from Phase 3 and Phase 4. Note these students as having integrated the vocabulary into active sentence structure.
- Students who connect water's bent shape to a real property (water is a liquid, ice floats, water dissolves salt) are showing the Structure and Function CCC transfer — the strongest evidence the lesson landed.
- Students who name a partner who helped them catch a lone-pair miscount are signaling that the station structure supported their learning.

If a student's Exit Ticket counts only bonded atoms and ignores lone pairs (answering "trigonal planar" for NH₃ or "linear" for water), the error is in the lone-pair-counting step, not the overall method — a brief check at the start of the next lesson, using the 4-balloon model with one balloon labeled "lone pair," should be sufficient. If a student counts a double bond as multiple groups (calling CO₂ or CS₂ bent), re-teach the multiple-bond rule before the next lesson builds on molecular shape.
