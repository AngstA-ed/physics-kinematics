Lesson 07 · Unit: Kinematics

Thrown Upwards / Vertical Projectiles

What does the velocity of a vertically-thrown object look like at launch, at the peak, and at landing?

Download teacher guide (DOCX)

Phenomenon

Toss a volleyball straight up so it rises about 2 meters before falling back into your hands. A classmate times the air-time on a phone. The ball spent some of that time going up, the rest coming down. Was the time spent going up the same as the time coming down? At the very top of the throw, was the ball ever not moving?

Driving Question

What does the velocity of a vertically-thrown object look like at launch, at the peak, and at landing?

Notice & Wonder

Predict: at the very peak of the throw, what is the ball's velocity? What is its acceleration? Are they the same number?

Initial Model

Sketch a v-t graph for the entire trip from launch to landing. Mark the peak.

Investigate

Slide v₀ to set the launch speed, then press Throw. Watch the ball go up, peak, and come back down. The v-t graph draws alongside.

Time: 0.0 s · velocity: 0.0 m/s · height: 0.0 m

Time to peak: — s · peak height: — m · time-of-flight: — s

If a friend launched the ball at 10 m/s and another launched it at 20 m/s, would the second ball stay in the air twice as long? Slide and check.

Make It Make Sense

  1. Set v₀ = 10 m/s. What is the velocity at the peak? At what time does the ball reach the peak?
  2. Why is the v-t graph a straight line, even though the ball is sometimes going up and sometimes going down?
  3. A ball is thrown straight up at 8 m/s. How fast is it moving when it returns to the launch height?
Stuck? Sentence frame

"At the peak, the ball's velocity is ___ but its acceleration is ___."

Key Vocabulary (max 3)

  • peak — the highest point of the throw; v = 0 momentarily
  • time-of-flight — total time from launch to landing
  • symmetry — time-up = time-down; speed at any height on the way up equals the speed at the same height on the way down

Revise Your Model

Update your initial v-t sketch. Add: (1) the peak time, (2) the launch and landing velocities, (3) where the slope changes (it doesn't — but draw the temptation, then refute it).

Return to the Phenomenon

Look back at the volleyball toss from the start of class. If your air-time was about 1.0 s, what was your launch speed? Use today's tools.

Exit Ticket + Closing Reflection

  1. A volleyball thrown straight up with v₀ = 12 m/s (g = 9.8 m/s²). (a) Time to peak? (b) Max height above launch? (c) Velocity at return to launch height? (d) Total time-of-flight?
  2. Reflection: What is one thing that surprised you today? Who helped you make sense of something?
